# Screenwriting and Dramatic Structure — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Three-act structure** — foundational framework dividing narrative into setup, confrontation, and resolution
2. **Setup-confrontation-resolution** — the core dramatic progression that shapes audience expectation
3. **Narrative arc** — the overall shape of story progression from beginning to end
4. **Inciting incident** — the event that disrupts status quo and launches the story. Depends on: three-act structure
5. **Catalyst** — the specific moment that forces protagonist into action. Depends on: inciting incident
6. **Plot points** — major turning points that redirect story trajectory. Depends on: three-act structure
7. **Turning points** — moments of irreversible decision or revelation. Depends on: plot points
8. **Point of no return** — the moment when protagonist commits fully to the journey. Depends on: turning points
9. **Midpoint** — the structural center that raises stakes and shifts perspective. Depends on: three-act structure, plot points
10. **False victory / false defeat** — midpoint outcomes that reverse in Act Three. Depends on: midpoint
11. **Stakes escalation** — progressive raising of consequences throughout the story. Depends on: plot points, midpoint
12. **Climax** — the final confrontation where all conflicts converge. Depends on: three-act structure, stakes escalation
13. **Resolution** — the new equilibrium after climax. Depends on: climax
14. **Thematic unity** — coherence between structure and meaning. Depends on: narrative arc, resolution
15. **Want vs need** — external desire versus internal requirement for character growth
16. **External goal** — the tangible objective driving plot action. Depends on: want vs need
17. **Internal arc** — the psychological transformation journey. Depends on: want vs need
18. **Character flaw** — the internal obstacle preventing growth. Depends on: internal arc
19. **Antagonist function** — the force creating pressure for protagonist transformation. Depends on: external goal, character flaw
20. **Opposition** — systematic resistance to protagonist's goals. Depends on: antagonist function
21. **Character biography** — backstory informing present behavior and motivation
22. **Backstory** — events before the story begins that shape character. Depends on: character biography
23. **Motivation** — the why behind character choices. Depends on: backstory, want vs need
24. **Internal conflict** — psychological struggle within protagonist. Depends on: character flaw, want vs need
25. **External conflict** — plot obstacles and antagonist opposition. Depends on: antagonist function, opposition
26. **Transformation** — fundamental character change by story's end. Depends on: internal arc, climax
27. **Act breaks** — structural divisions between acts. Depends on: three-act structure
28. **Pacing** — rhythm and tempo of story progression. Depends on: act breaks, plot points
29. **Rising tension** — progressive increase in conflict intensity. Depends on: stakes escalation, pacing
30. **B-story** — thematic subplot exploring internal arc. Depends on: internal arc, want vs need
31. **A-story vs B-story** — distinction between plot (A) and theme (B). Depends on: B-story, external goal
32. **All is lost** — the lowest moment before final act. Depends on: three-act structure, stakes escalation
33. **Dark night of the soul** — moment of despair before breakthrough. Depends on: all is lost
34. **Setup and payoff** — planting elements that deliver narrative satisfaction. Depends on: thematic unity
35. **Save the Cat** — Blake Snyder's 15-beat structural framework. Depends on: three-act structure
36. **Beat sheet** — detailed scene-by-scene outline with page targets. Depends on: Save the Cat
37. **Hero's Journey** — Campbell's archetypal structure pattern. Depends on: three-act structure
38. **Sequence method** — eight-sequence approach to feature structure. Depends on: three-act structure
39. **Genre structure** — how genre shapes structural expectations. Depends on: three-act structure, genre conventions
40. **Scene purpose** — every scene must advance plot or character. Depends on: pacing
41. **Goal-conflict-disaster** — micro-structure within individual scenes. Depends on: scene purpose
42. **Visual storytelling** — showing rather than telling through action. Depends on: show don't tell
43. **Subtext** — meaning beneath surface dialogue and action. Depends on: visual storytelling
44. **Logline** — one-sentence story summary capturing core dramatic premise. Depends on: external goal, antagonist function

## Dependencies

### Foundation Layer
- **Three-act structure** is the bedrock — nearly all other concepts build on understanding setup, confrontation, and resolution
- **Want vs need** is the character foundation — internal arc, character flaw, and transformation all depend on this distinction
- These two concepts form the dual foundation: structure (three-act) and character (want vs need)

### Structural Dependencies
- **Plot points depend on three-act structure** — you must understand acts before you can place turning points effectively
- **Midpoint depends on both acts and plot points** — it's simultaneously an act-two event and a major plot point
- **Climax requires understanding stakes escalation** — without progressive rising stakes, climax has no weight
- **All is lost depends on three-act structure** — it's specifically an Act Two/Act Three boundary beat

### Character Dependencies
- **Internal arc depends on want vs need** — the arc IS the journey from want to need
- **Character flaw blocks the internal arc** — the flaw is what prevents easy transformation
- **Antagonist function requires understanding character flaw** — antagonist pressure exposes and tests the flaw
- **Transformation requires both internal arc and climax** — character change happens when internal and external conflicts align

### Integration Points (where structure and character merge)
- **Midpoint** — often a false victory/defeat that tests character assumptions
- **All is lost** — structural low point that forces character introspection (dark night of the soul)
- **Climax** — external conflict (structure) and internal conflict (character) resolve simultaneously
- **B-story** — thematic subplot that teaches protagonist what they need while A-story tests what they want

### Advanced Framework Dependencies
- **Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, and Sequence Method** all depend on three-act structure — they're elaborations, not replacements
- **Beat sheet depends on Save the Cat** — it's the practical application tool
- **Genre structure depends on understanding base structure** — you must know the rules before you can see how genre bends them

## Bottlenecks

### Critical Bottleneck: Want vs. Need
Many students struggle here because it requires understanding:
1. External goal (plot-level, conscious)
2. Internal requirement (theme-level, often unconscious to character)
3. How the gap between them creates dramatic irony

If students don't grasp want vs need, they'll struggle with:
- Character arc design
- Why antagonists exist (to force the character to confront their need)
- How B-story works (it teaches the need while A-story pursues the want)
- Why climax must address both (external and internal resolution)

### Critical Bottleneck: Midpoint
Midpoint is structurally complex because it:
- Occurs at Act Two center (page 55-60 in a 110-page script)
- Reverses something from Act One
- Raises stakes significantly
- Often shifts from reactive to proactive protagonist behavior
- Creates the momentum leading to "all is lost"

Students who don't understand midpoint write sagging Act Twos.

### Critical Bottleneck: Setup and Payoff
This requires thinking about the story in two directions simultaneously:
- Forward: what does this scene establish?
- Backward: what earlier setup does this scene pay off?

Without this skill, students write episodic rather than integrated narratives.

## Prerequisite Topics

### External Prerequisites (before starting this curriculum)
- **Basic narrative literacy** — needed for understanding story progression, causality
- **Film/TV viewing experience** — needed for recognizing structural patterns, genre conventions
- **Critical analysis ability** — needed for identifying why stories work or fail structurally

### Internal Prerequisites (within the curriculum)
- **Three-act structure must come before advanced frameworks** — Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, and Sequence Method are all elaborations on three-act
- **Want vs need must come before B-story** — B-story is literally the subplot teaching the protagonist their need
- **Character foundation before Act Two complexity** — understanding character arc makes Act Two challenges (sagging, B-story integration) solvable
- **Scene-level craft comes last** — you must understand macro structure before micro structure makes sense

## Common Misconception Connections

### "Structure is a formula that makes stories predictable"
Actually, structure is an expectation-management tool. The concepts of setup/payoff, turning points, and climax exist because audiences need orientation. Creativity lives in execution, not in defying fundamental human story perception.

**Concept connection**: Understanding thematic unity and genre structure shows how the same structural framework produces infinite variation.

### "Character arc is separate from plot"
The dependency map reveals this is false — internal arc (character) and external goal (plot) converge at climax. The entire curriculum builds toward understanding that character transformation IS plot progression.

**Concept connection**: Want vs need, A-story vs B-story, internal/external conflict, transformation at climax.

### "The midpoint is just the middle"
Midpoint is the structural hinge — it's where false victory/defeat occurs, stakes escalate irreversibly, and protagonist often shifts from reactive to proactive.

**Concept connection**: Midpoint depends on and influences plot points, stakes escalation, pacing, and leads directly to all is lost.

### "Antagonists are villains"
Antagonist function is to create pressure that forces protagonist growth. Sometimes that's a villain, sometimes it's society, nature, or the protagonist's own psychology.

**Concept connection**: Antagonist function depends on character flaw and want vs need — the antagonist tests the exact thing the protagonist needs to learn.

### "Save the Cat replaces three-act structure"
Save the Cat IS three-act structure with finer granularity (15 beats instead of 3 acts). It's an elaboration, not a replacement.

**Concept connection**: Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, and Sequence Method all depend on three-act structure as foundation.
