# Film Theory — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Film grammar** — the formal vocabulary of cinema (shots, angles, cuts, composition)
2. **Denotation vs. connotation** — literal content vs. implied meaning
3. **Formal analysis** — systematic examination of cinematic techniques and their effects
4. **Continuity system** — classical Hollywood's invisible editing conventions. Depends on: film grammar
5. **Mise-en-scene** — everything placed before the camera (setting, lighting, costume, staging). Depends on: formal analysis
6. **Frame composition** — spatial arrangement within the frame. Depends on: mise-en-scene
7. **Camera placement and angle** — psychological and narrative effects of camera position. Depends on: frame composition
8. **Lighting schemes** — high-key vs. low-key, chiaroscuro, emotional tone. Depends on: mise-en-scene
9. **Spatial composition** — foreground/background tension, depth staging. Depends on: frame composition, camera placement
10. **Color theory in film** — palette as mood, symbolic color, color grading. Depends on: mise-en-scene
11. **Production design and costume** — world-building through visual detail. Depends on: mise-en-scene, color theory
12. **Formalism vs. naturalism** — aesthetic approaches to composition. Depends on: mise-en-scene, spatial composition
13. **Soviet montage theory** — editing as collision of ideas, not just linkage. Depends on: film grammar, formal analysis
14. **Kuleshov effect** — viewer creates meaning from juxtaposed shots. Depends on: Soviet montage theory
15. **Eisenstein's five montages** — metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, intellectual. Depends on: Soviet montage theory
16. **Rhythmic montage** — tempo manipulation for emotional effect. Depends on: Eisenstein's montages
17. **Continuity editing** — 180-degree rule, match on action, eyeline match, shot-reverse-shot. Depends on: continuity system
18. **Jump cut** — deliberate violation of continuity for aesthetic effect. Depends on: continuity editing
19. **Collision vs. linkage montage** — dialectical vs. seamless editing approaches. Depends on: Soviet montage theory, continuity editing
20. **Three-act structure** — setup, confrontation, resolution in classical Hollywood. Depends on: continuity system
21. **Non-linear narrative** — story vs. plot, syuzhet vs. fabula, flashback/flash-forward. Depends on: three-act structure
22. **Unreliable narrator** — subjective point-of-view and narrative trust. Depends on: non-linear narrative
23. **Parallel editing** — cross-cutting between multiple storylines. Depends on: continuity editing, rhythmic montage
24. **Genre theory** — narrative conventions and viewer expectations. Depends on: three-act structure
25. **Integrated analysis** — mise-en-scene + montage + narrative working together. Depends on: ALL PREVIOUS CONCEPTS

## Dependencies

### Foundational Chain
- **Formal analysis** is the foundation for everything — you can't analyze what you can't articulate
- **Film grammar** provides the vocabulary that makes formal analysis possible
- **Denotation vs. connotation** separates surface content from interpretive depth

### Mise-en-scene Branch
- **Frame composition** requires understanding mise-en-scene as a system of choices
- **Camera placement** builds on composition — where you put the camera determines what relationships are visible
- **Spatial composition** synthesizes frame composition and camera placement — depth and layering
- **Lighting, color, production design** are parallel specializations of mise-en-scene, all building on the core concept

### Montage Branch
- **Soviet montage theory** rejects the transparency of continuity editing — collision creates meaning
- **Kuleshov effect** is the empirical proof of montage theory — the experiment that showed viewers construct meaning
- **Eisenstein's five montages** formalize different collision strategies
- **Continuity editing** runs parallel to Soviet montage but with opposite goals (seamlessness vs. collision)
- **Jump cut** makes sense only after understanding what it violates

### Narrative Branch
- **Three-act structure** is the baseline narrative model in Hollywood
- **Non-linear narrative** subverts chronological storytelling — requires understanding the classical model first
- **Parallel editing** combines montage technique with narrative complexity
- **Genre theory** shows how conventions shape and constrain narrative

### Integration
- **Integrated analysis** is the capstone — mise-en-scene sets the visual stage, montage controls rhythm and juxtaposition, narrative provides meaning arc. All three must work together.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic film terminology** — needed for film grammar, formal analysis. If student doesn't know shot types (close-up, medium shot, wide shot), start there.
- **Familiarity with diverse genres** — needed for genre theory and recognizing conventions. Student should have watched films across multiple genres (noir, Western, horror, comedy, etc.).
- **Critical viewing skills** — ability to watch actively and take notes. Needed for all formal analysis work.

## Bottlenecks

### Denotation vs. Connotation
If the student can't distinguish literal content from implied meaning, formal analysis becomes mechanical — they'll describe what they see but not interpret it. This is the gatekeeper concept.

### Kuleshov Effect
This is the conceptual breakthrough for montage — if the student doesn't grasp that viewers create meaning, they'll see editing as mere sequencing. The Kuleshov effect demonstrates that film meaning is constructed, not recorded.

### Continuity Editing
Until the student understands the invisible rules of continuity, violations (jump cuts, French New Wave disruptions) won't register as violations. They'll just look "weird" without the student knowing why.

### Integrated Analysis
This is where everything converges. Students often learn mise-en-scene, montage, and narrative as separate silos. Integration requires seeing how Hitchcock's camera placement (mise-en-scene) works with his cross-cutting (montage) to create suspense (narrative function).

## Common Misconceptions

### "Mise-en-scene is just set design"
Mise-en-scene includes everything in the frame — lighting, costume, actor staging, color, camera placement. It's the total visual composition, not just scenery.

### "Montage means fast cutting"
In everyday usage, yes. In film theory, montage is any editorial juxtaposition that creates meaning. Slow cuts can be montage. A single long take is anti-montage (Bazinian realism).

### "Three-act structure is formulaic and bad"
Three-act structure is descriptive (most Hollywood films follow it) not prescriptive. It's a framework, not a straitjacket. Understanding it helps you recognize when films subvert it effectively.

### "The director controls everything"
Students often over-attribute. Cinematography, editing, production design, color grading — these are collaborative crafts. Auteur theory is useful but overstates directorial control.

### "Analysis kills enjoyment"
The resistance to "over-analyzing." In fact, formal analysis deepens appreciation — you see more, notice patterns, understand craft. It's like music theory for musicians.
