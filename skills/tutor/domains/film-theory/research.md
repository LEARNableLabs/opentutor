# Film Theory Research Summary

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Mise-en-scene
The visual composition of the frame — everything placed before the camera. Includes setting, lighting, costume, actor staging, color theory, and spatial relationships. Core principle: every element in the frame is a choice that carries meaning.

### 2. Montage and Editing
The construction of meaning through juxtaposition and rhythm. Soviet montage theory (Eisenstein, Kuleshov, Vertov) established that collision of images creates intellectual and emotional impact beyond the content of individual shots. Modern editing includes continuity systems, rhythmic patterns, and temporal manipulation.

### 3. Narrative Structure
Story architecture in cinema — classical Hollywood three-act structure, hero's journey, non-linear narratives, parallel editing, flashbacks, unreliable narrators. Genre conventions as narrative frameworks.

### 4. Integration
How mise-en-scene, montage, and narrative work together to create cinematic meaning. The interplay of visual composition, temporal rhythm, and story structure.

## Key Sources

### Academic Foundations
- David Bordwell & Kristin Thompson's "Film Art: An Introduction" — comprehensive intermediate-level textbook covering formal analysis
- Sergei Eisenstein's "Film Form" and "The Film Sense" — foundational montage theory
- André Bazin's writings on realism vs. montage
- Christian Metz on film semiotics and narrative codes

### Educational Resources
- Criterion Collection video essays and director commentaries
- Every Frame a Painting (YouTube channel, archived) — visual essay format
- British Film Institute educational materials
- MIT OpenCourseWare film analysis courses
- Museum of the Moving Image resources

### Practical Analysis Tools
- Frame-by-frame analysis tools (YouTube slow speed, video essays)
- Color theory resources (Wes Anderson palettes, color grading breakdowns)
- Editing pattern breakdowns (Edgar Wright, Thelma Schoonmaker)

## Pedagogical Approach

Intermediate level requires:
- Moving beyond identification ("this is a close-up") to interpretation ("why does this close-up create intimacy here but claustrophobia there?")
- Historical context for montage theory and its evolution
- Practical analysis skills — student should be able to watch any scene and articulate formal choices
- Integration of concepts — mise-en-scene and editing working together
- Genre awareness as framework
