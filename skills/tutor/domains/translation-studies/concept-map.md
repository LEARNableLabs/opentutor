# Translation Studies — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Translation quality** — What makes a translation effective or defective
2. **Equivalence** — Relationship between source and target text (formal, dynamic, functional)
3. **Source vs. target orientation** — Whether translation prioritizes source text fidelity or target audience readability
4. **Translation norms** — Regularities in translation behavior within a culture (Toury)
5. **Decision-making** — How translators choose between competing solutions
6. **Translator agency** — The translator's active role as decision-maker and cultural mediator
7. **Linguistic turn** — Early focus on language systems and equivalence frameworks
8. **Cultural turn** — Shift to viewing translation as cultural transfer and rewriting
9. **Theoretical paradigms** — Major schools of thought in translation studies (linguistic, functionalist, cultural, sociological)
10. **Translation shift** — Formal changes that occur in the translation process
11. **Gain and loss** — What is added or removed in translation
12. **Untranslatability** — Concepts, wordplay, or cultural elements that resist direct transfer
13. **Holmes' map** — Organizing framework for the discipline (pure vs. applied, product/process/function)
14. **Descriptive Translation Studies (DTS)** — Empirical study of translations as cultural facts. Depends on: 4, 5, 8
15. **Toury's norms** — Initial, preliminary, and operational norms that govern translation behavior. Depends on: 4, 14
16. **Target culture** — The receiving culture's role in shaping translations. Depends on: 3, 8
17. **Polysystem theory** — Model of literary systems and translation's role within them. Depends on: 14, 16
18. **Literary systems** — How literatures compete and interact within a culture. Depends on: 17
19. **Canonization** — Process by which some translations become classics. Depends on: 17, 18
20. **Skopos theory** — Purpose-driven translation (Vermeer, Nord). Depends on: 3, 9
21. **Functionalism** — Translation guided by intended function rather than equivalence. Depends on: 2, 20
22. **Purpose-driven translation** — Adapting translation strategy to communicative goals. Depends on: 20, 21
23. **Ideology in translation** — How power, politics, and beliefs shape translation choices. Depends on: 6, 8
24. **Power relations** — Asymmetries between source/target cultures, languages, or agents. Depends on: 23
25. **Postcolonial translation** — Translation in contexts of colonialism and cultural dominance. Depends on: 23, 24
26. **Comparative analysis** — Systematic comparison of translations to identify patterns. Depends on: 10, 14, 15
27. **Descriptive methods** — Research techniques for analyzing actual translations. Depends on: 14, 26
28. **Literary translation** — Translating fiction, poetry, drama with attention to aesthetics
29. **Style** — Linguistic and rhetorical features that create a text's voice. Depends on: 10
30. **Voice preservation** — Maintaining a text's or character's distinctive expression. Depends on: 28, 29
31. **Technical translation** — Specialized domains (engineering, medicine, law) requiring subject expertise
32. **Terminology** — Domain-specific vocabulary and its standardization. Depends on: 31
33. **Standardization** — Using consistent terms and phrasing across documents. Depends on: 32
34. **Audiovisual translation (AVT)** — Subtitling, dubbing, voice-over for film, TV, games
35. **Subtitling** — Written translation constrained by time and space. Depends on: 34
36. **Dubbing** — Oral translation with lip-sync and timing constraints. Depends on: 34
37. **Constraints** — Spatial, temporal, technical, and semiotic limitations on translation. Depends on: 34, 35, 36
38. **Localization** — Adapting products (software, websites, games) for different markets
39. **Cultural adaptation** — Modifying cultural references, visuals, formats for target audience. Depends on: 8, 38
40. **Transcreation** — Creative re-conceptualization for marketing and branding. Depends on: 38, 39
41. **Translation strategies** — Techniques for solving translation problems (borrowing, calque, transposition, etc.). Depends on: 5, 22
42. **Genre awareness** — Understanding how text type shapes translation choices. Depends on: 21, 28, 31, 34
43. **Problem-solving** — Diagnosing and resolving translation challenges. Depends on: 5, 41
44. **CAT tools** — Computer-assisted translation software (Trados, MemoQ)
45. **Translation memory (TM)** — Database of previously translated segments. Depends on: 44
46. **Productivity** — Efficiency gains from technology and workflow optimization. Depends on: 44, 45
47. **Machine translation (MT)** — Automated translation (rule-based, statistical, neural)
48. **Neural MT** — Deep learning-based translation (Google Translate, DeepL). Depends on: 47
49. **MT limitations** — Errors in context, tone, ambiguity, and cultural nuance. Depends on: 47, 48
50. **Post-editing** — Human revision of MT output. Depends on: 47, 48
51. **MT quality assessment** — Evaluating machine translation adequacy and fluency. Depends on: 1, 50
52. **Effort metrics** — Measuring time and edits required for post-editing. Depends on: 50, 51
53. **Translation competence** — Knowledge, skills, and attitudes required for professional translation
54. **PACTE model** — Multidimensional competence framework (bilingual, extralinguistic, translation knowledge, instrumental, strategic). Depends on: 53
55. **Expertise development** — How competence grows from novice to expert. Depends on: 53, 54
56. **Quality assessment** — Evaluating translation fitness for purpose
57. **Error typologies** — Classification systems for translation mistakes (accuracy, fluency, terminology, style). Depends on: 56
58. **Metrics** — Quantitative measures for quality (DQF, MQM, BLEU). Depends on: 56, 57
59. **Translation ethics** — Moral principles guiding translator behavior
60. **Confidentiality** — Protecting client and source text information. Depends on: 59
61. **Cultural mediation** — Navigating cultural differences responsibly. Depends on: 6, 59
62. **Professional associations** — Organizations that certify and support translators (ATA, ITI, etc.)
63. **Certification** — Credential programs validating translator competence. Depends on: 62
64. **Quality standards** — Industry norms (ISO 17100, EN 15038). Depends on: 56, 62
65. **Translator visibility** — Whether translators should be acknowledged or effaced
66. **Domestication vs. foreignization** — Making text familiar vs. preserving foreignness (Venuti). Depends on: 3, 65
67. **Ethics of representation** — Responsibility for how cultures and voices are portrayed. Depends on: 59, 61, 65

## Dependencies

### Theory foundations support practice
- **Descriptive Translation Studies** (14) requires understanding **translation norms** (4) and **translator agency** (6) because DTS examines how norms guide translator decisions
- **Skopos theory** (20) builds on **source vs. target orientation** (3) and **theoretical paradigms** (9) because it represents a paradigm shift toward function over equivalence
- **Comparative analysis** (26) depends on **translation shift** (10), **DTS** (14), and **Toury's norms** (15) because it uses descriptive methods to identify norm-governed patterns

### Genre expertise requires foundational concepts
- **Literary translation** (28) builds on **style** (29) and **voice preservation** (30) because maintaining aesthetic qualities is central to translating literature
- **Technical translation** (31) requires **terminology** (32) and **standardization** (33) because specialized domains demand consistent, precise vocabulary
- **Audiovisual translation** (34) depends on understanding **constraints** (37) because subtitling and dubbing have unique spatial and temporal limitations

### Technology transforms practice
- **Translation memory** (45) depends on **CAT tools** (44) because TM systems are core features of CAT platforms
- **Post-editing** (50) requires understanding **MT** (47), **neural MT** (48), and **MT limitations** (49) because effective post-editing requires knowing typical MT error patterns
- **Effort metrics** (52) build on **post-editing** (50) and **MT quality assessment** (51) because they quantify the cost-effectiveness of using MT

### Professional competence integrates multiple dimensions
- **PACTE model** (54) depends on **translation competence** (53) because it operationalizes competence into measurable sub-competencies
- **Quality assessment** (56) informs **error typologies** (57) and **metrics** (58) because systematic evaluation requires classification and measurement
- **Translator visibility** (65) connects to **domestication vs. foreignization** (66) and **ethics of representation** (67) because visibility debates involve cultural politics and ethical responsibility

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Proficiency in at least one foreign language** — needed for practical translation exercises, comparative analysis, and understanding translation shifts
- **Basic linguistics** — needed for concepts like equivalence, translation shift, terminology, constraints
- **Cross-cultural awareness** — needed for cultural turn, ideology, postcolonial translation, localization, ethics
- **Reading in multiple genres** — needed to understand genre awareness, literary vs. technical translation, style and voice

## Bottlenecks

**Translation norms** (lesson 2) is the first bottleneck — students must grasp that translation behavior is culturally conditioned, not just individual choice. This concept unlocks DTS, Toury, polysystem theory, and ideology discussions.

**Skopos theory** (lesson 9) is the second bottleneck — shifting from equivalence-centered to purpose-centered thinking is a paradigm change. Students who resist functionalism will struggle with localization, transcreation, and genre-specific strategies.

**CAT tools and Translation Memory** (lesson 19) is the third bottleneck — understanding how TM works is essential for grasping modern professional workflows, productivity metrics, and the difference between CAT and MT.

**Domestication vs. foreignization** (lesson 27) is the final bottleneck — this debate synthesizes theoretical and ethical dimensions, requiring students to integrate norms, ideology, translator visibility, and cultural mediation.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Translation is just finding equivalent words"** — Students underestimate the complexity of cultural, pragmatic, and stylistic transfer. Address by introducing translation shifts and untranslatability early.

2. **"Good translators are invisible"** — Many assume transparency is always the goal. Introduce Venuti's critique of invisibility and the ethics of foreignization to show visibility as a legitimate choice.

3. **"Machine translation will replace human translators"** — Students overestimate MT capabilities or underestimate their own value. Address by teaching MT limitations, post-editing skills, and areas where human judgment is irreplaceable.

4. **"Technical translation is just terminology lookup"** — Students miss the expertise required for understanding specialized subject matter. Emphasize domain knowledge and risk assessment in technical fields.

5. **"Literal translation is always more accurate"** — Conflating word-level equivalence with functional adequacy. Teach through skopos theory and genre-specific examples where literal translation fails.

6. **"Norms are prescriptive rules"** — Confusing Toury's descriptive norms with prescriptive standards. Clarify that norms describe actual behavior, not what translators should do.
