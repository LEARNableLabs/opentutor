# Antitrust and Competition Law — Teaching Notes

## Approach

**Antitrust at intermediate level is best taught through the tension between economic theory and legal doctrine.** Students at this level already understand basic economics, so the challenge is showing how courts translate economic concepts into workable legal rules — and where those translations succeed or fail. Use real cases as anchors (Microsoft, Google Shopping, Apple e-books, Staples/Office Depot) to ground abstract concepts. The doctrine is doctrine-heavy (per se vs. rule of reason, structural vs. behavioral remedies), but students stay engaged when they see how billion-dollar deals turn on market definition fights or how a single email can prove conspiracy.

**Comparative perspective (US vs. EU) should be woven throughout, not siloed.** The jurisdictions differ in philosophy (consumer welfare vs. protecting competition as a process), burden of proof, and remedy design. Highlighting these differences reveals that competition law embodies policy choices, not universal truths.

**Balance theory-building with "war stories."** Intermediate students need to see both the analytical framework (how to structure a rule of reason analysis) and the messy reality (expert battles over market definition,Document X that tanked the defense). Vary between clean conceptual explanations and fact-intensive case deep-dives.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Market share = market power"

**Why students get this wrong**: HHI calculations and market definition discussions emphasize market share metrics, leading students to equate high share with dominance.

**How to correct**: Distinguish structural market power (high share in properly defined market) from behavioral market power (ability to profitably raise prices). Show cases where high share doesn't confer power (low entry barriers, buyer power, frequent repositioning) and vice versa. Use the *cellophane fallacy* to show how market definition can artificially inflate or deflate power measures.

### 2. "Vertical restraints are just sneaky horizontal restraints"

**Why students get this wrong**: Early cases (Dr. Miles, pre-Leegin RPM) treated many vertical restraints as per se illegal, and students carry that intuition forward.

**How to correct**: Emphasize that vertical restraints solve real business problems (free-riding, hold-up, quality certification). The post-Leegin shift to rule of reason reflects economic learning. Walk through specific procompetitive justifications (dealer services in Leegin, brand competition). Contrast with horizontal restraints where procompetitive stories are rare.

### 3. "Algorithms can't collude without explicit agreement"

**Why students get this wrong**: Traditional cartel law requires "agreement" — meeting of the minds, communications, plus factors. Algorithmic pricing seems too mechanical.

**How to correct**: Show how algorithms can learn to coordinate through reinforcement learning without human intent or communication. The legal challenge is that *conscious parallelism* is not itself illegal absent plus factors, but algorithms make parallel pricing more stable and harder to detect. This is an evolving area; students should understand the doctrinal gap, not assume existing rules resolve it.

### 4. "Efficiencies are a get-out-of-jail-free card"

**Why students get this wrong**: Merger guidelines and rule of reason frameworks explicitly allow efficiency defenses, so students assume efficiencies will save questionable deals.

**How to correct**: Emphasize the high bar for cognizable efficiencies: merger-specific (can't achieve through less restrictive means), verifiable (not speculative), sufficient to outweigh harms (including pass-through to consumers). Show failed efficiency defenses (e.g., Staples/Office Depot claimed procurement efficiencies but couldn't prove pass-through). Efficiencies arguments *can* work, but usually don't overcome clear competitive harm.

### 5. "Two-sided markets always justify different treatment"

**Why students get this wrong**: Platform economics literature emphasizes network effects, cross-subsidization, and positive feedback loops, leading students to think platforms are sui generis.

**How to correct**: Yes, two-sided markets require modified analysis (free side pricing, indirect network effects), but the core question remains: does conduct harm competition and consumers? Self-preferencing, MFN clauses, and exclusive dealing still foreclose rivals on platforms just as in traditional markets. Don't let "it's a platform" become an excuse to ignore competitive harm.

### 6. "Innovation competition doesn't matter if prices are low"

**Why students get this wrong**: The consumer welfare standard is often simplified to "price effects," and students overlook non-price dimensions.

**How to correct**: Modern antitrust (especially in tech) increasingly focuses on innovation competition, quality degradation, and reduced variety. The EU Google Shopping case emphasized harm to innovation incentives. Merger review considers whether merged firm will reduce R&D or delay new product launches. Train students to ask: what would have happened but for the conduct?

### 7. "Coordinated effects = conspiracy"

**Why students get this wrong**: Both involve multiple firms and parallel behavior, and the terminology overlaps.

**How to correct**: Coordinated effects in merger analysis refers to *tacit* collusion (oligopoly interdependence without agreement), while conspiracy requires explicit coordination. A merger can make coordinated effects more likely by reducing the number of firms, increasing transparency, or aligning incentives — but this is not itself illegal conspiracy. The legal standard is whether post-merger market structure makes tacit coordination more likely, not whether firms will explicitly agree.

### 8. "EU is always stricter than US"

**Why students get this wrong**: High-profile EU cases (Google, Microsoft, Intel) with large fines and aggressive remedies create perception of tougher enforcement.

**How to correct**: US and EU differ in *where* they're strict, not uniformly stricter. EU is more aggressive on dominance/monopolization (lower burden for Article 102) and merger remedies (more behavioral remedies). US is tougher on horizontal cartels (criminal penalties, leniency program) and historically more skeptical of vertical restraints (though that gap has narrowed post-Leegin). It's jurisdiction-specific, not EU > US across the board.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate vs. Beginner

- **Assume economic literacy**: Don't re-teach supply/demand or deadweight loss. Jump straight to SSNIP test, upward pricing pressure, and oligopoly interdependence.
- **Go deeper on doctrine**: Beginners get "per se vs. rule of reason." Intermediates get *why* the Supreme Court shifted Leegin from per se to rule of reason, and whether that was right.
- **Use real case materials**: Pull from actual complaints, expert reports, and judicial opinions (simplified excerpts). Beginners get hypotheticals; intermediates get messy facts.
- **Introduce strategic considerations**: How would you structure a joint venture to minimize antitrust risk? What divestiture package would satisfy regulators? This is pre-professional thinking.

### Intermediate vs. Advanced

- **Less formalism**: Advanced students get formal economic models (Cournot, Bertrand, vertical foreclosure models). Intermediates get intuition and graphical explanations without full derivations.
- **Skip econometric details**: Mention that experts use merger simulation and natural experiments, but don't teach the regression specifications.
- **Fewer jurisdiction comparisons**: Advanced courses dive into Chinese SAMR, Japanese JFTC, Brazilian CADE. Stick to US-EU comparison at intermediate.
- **No deep statutory interpretation**: Advanced students parse statutory text and legislative history. Intermediates learn the key statutes (Sherman Act §1 & §2, Clayton Act §7) but focus on application, not textual analysis.

## Rabbit Holes

### 1. The 2nd Circuit oral argument in *Apple e-books*

**When to drop**: After lesson 7 (conspiracy case). The judges' questioning of Apple's counsel is a masterclass in how courts think about circumstantial evidence and business justifications. [Listen here](https://www.ca2.uscourts.gov/decisions/isysquery/75708af8-f7fc-4c60-bac7-12d6dce56f98/1/doc/13-3741_complete_opn.pdf#page=1) (oral argument recording). Students love hearing real advocacy.

### 2. The cellophane fallacy

**When to drop**: After lesson 2 (market definition). *DuPont* (cellophane case) defined the market at monopoly prices, making substitutes look closer than they were. This error still haunts merger analysis. Cite the Staples/Office Depot market definition battle as modern example.

### 3. Herbert Hovenkamp's blog on "neo-Brandeisian" antitrust

**When to drop**: After lesson 4 (Chicago School). The current debate over antitrust goals (consumer welfare vs. broader social/political objectives) is live policy. Hovenkamp is a defender of consumer welfare standard; students should read his critiques of Lina Khan's FTC approach. Links political economy to doctrine.

### 4. The prisoner's dilemma and cartel instability

**When to drop**: After lesson 5 (price fixing). Game theory explains why cartels are unstable without enforcement mechanisms — cheating is individually rational. This motivates trade association monitoring (lesson 6) and leniency programs. Quick 5-minute game theory review pays dividends.

### 5. Areeda-Turner predatory pricing test

**When to drop**: After lesson 11 (exclusionary conduct). The debate over whether below-cost pricing is sufficient/necessary for predatory pricing claim. Economics says "price below marginal cost"; law uses average variable cost as proxy. Introduces antitrust's tradeoff between economic rigor and administrability.

### 6. The Essential Facilities Doctrine is dead (or is it?)

**When to drop**: After lesson 13 (refusal to deal). Debate over whether *Trinko* killed essential facilities doctrine or just raised the bar. EU still uses it actively (e.g., Microsoft interoperability). Illustrates US-EU divergence on duty to deal.

### 7. Platform MFN clauses and the "waterbed effect"

**When to drop**: After lesson 17 (MFN clauses). When Amazon bans sellers from offering lower prices elsewhere, sellers raise prices on all platforms to avoid losing Amazon access. Harm spreads to other platforms (the "waterbed"). Economic model is intuitive and shows cross-platform effects.

### 8. Why do regulators rarely challenge conglomerate mergers?

**When to drop**: After lesson 21 (merger case studies). Conglomerate theory (mergers creating portfolio power or foreclosure through bundling) has fallen out of favor post-Chicago School, but EU revived it in some cases. Students should know it exists and why it's controversial.

### 9. China's Anti-Monopoly Law and Alibaba/Meituan cases

**When to drop**: After lesson 27 (DMA vs. US). China's recent enforcement against tech giants shows a third model (state-driven, political objectives mixed with competition). Brief comparison shows antitrust is not just US-EU; China is now the world's most active enforcer by case volume.

### 10. The "Ohio v. American Express" two-sided market decision

**When to drop**: After lesson 24 (platform markets). Controversial 5-4 Supreme Court decision that you must prove harm on *both* sides of a platform, not just one. Heavily criticized by economists. Perfect case study for how doctrine can diverge from economics.

## Difficulty Progression

- **Lessons 1-4 (Foundations)**: Difficulty 1-3. Ease into market definition and basic economics. These are accessible reviews for intermediate students.
  
- **Lessons 5-9 (Horizontal Restraints)**: Difficulty 2-4. Ramp up with Apple e-books case (lesson 7) as first major peak. Per se vs. rule of reason is straightforward, but applying plus factors to circumstantial evidence is challenging.

- **Lessons 10-14 (Monopolization)**: Difficulty 2-4. Microsoft case (lesson 12) is peak difficulty — tying, technological integration, and exclusionary conduct all blur together. Essential facilities (lesson 13) is also conceptually hard. Review (lesson 14 teach-back) consolidates.

- **Lessons 15-18 (Vertical Restraints)**: Difficulty 3-4. Leegin decision (lesson 15) requires understanding Supreme Court's economic reasoning. Exclusive dealing foreclosure (lesson 16) and tying (lesson 18) are both difficulty 4 — these are subtle effects that require careful balancing.

- **Lessons 19-23 (Merger Control)**: Difficulty 2-4. Ease in with procedural overview (lesson 19), build to Staples case (lesson 21, difficulty 4), then review with teach-back (lesson 23). Merger analysis integrates everything prior.

- **Lessons 24-28 (Digital Markets)**: Difficulty 2-4. Google Shopping (lesson 25) and algorithmic collusion (lesson 26) are both difficulty 4 — cutting-edge issues without settled doctrine. Final review (lesson 28) steps back to difficulty 2 for synthesis.

**Overall arc**: Start accessible, build to multiple peaks (Apple e-books, Microsoft, exclusive dealing, Staples, Google Shopping, algorithmic collusion), with regular review days (lessons 9, 14, 23, 28) to consolidate. The topic is naturally challenging, so reviews are essential.

## Teaching Style Notes

- **Use Socratic questions aggressively**: "Why is price fixing per se illegal but RPM rule of reason? They're both vertical price restraints — what's the difference?" Push students to articulate the economic logic, not just memorize rules.
  
- **Embrace uncertainty**: Many areas (algorithmic pricing, platform MFNs, data as barrier to entry) don't have clear answers. Teach students to structure the tradeoffs, not find the "right" answer.

- **Tie to current events**: If there's a major merger announcement or DOJ/FTC case filing, work it into a lesson. Antitrust is live and students feel the relevance.

- **Encourage skepticism of economic models**: Economics provides frameworks, not answers. Markets are messier than models. Students should question assumptions (e.g., "does the SSNIP test work for zero-price products?").

- **Assign "moot court" style exercises**: Have students argue both sides of a case. This builds appreciation for how reasonable people can disagree on market definition, efficiency claims, and remedy design.
