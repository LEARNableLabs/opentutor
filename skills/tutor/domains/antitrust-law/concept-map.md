# Antitrust and Competition Law — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Market failure** — why unregulated markets can produce monopoly deadweight loss and reduce consumer welfare
2. **Consumer welfare standard** — the dominant framework for evaluating competitive harm (price, output, quality, innovation)
3. **Market definition** — identifying the relevant product and geographic market using SSNIP test and demand substitution. Depends on: market failure
4. **Market concentration** — measuring market power using HHI and concentration ratios. Depends on: market definition
5. **Chicago School economics** — efficiency-focused enforcement philosophy emphasizing consumer welfare and skepticism of intervention. Depends on: consumer welfare standard
6. **Per se illegality** — automatic condemnation of certain horizontal restraints (price fixing) without weighing procompetitive benefits
7. **Horizontal agreements** — coordination between competitors. Depends on: per se illegality
8. **Rule of reason** — balancing anticompetitive harms against procompetitive justifications
9. **Trade associations and information exchange** — gray area where competitor communication may facilitate collusion. Depends on: horizontal agreements, rule of reason
10. **Hub-and-spoke conspiracy** — vertical agreements used to facilitate horizontal collusion. Depends on: horizontal agreements
11. **Ancillary restraints doctrine** — when restraints are reasonably necessary to a legitimate collaboration. Depends on: rule of reason
12. **Monopolization** — acquiring or maintaining monopoly power through exclusionary conduct (Section 2 Sherman Act)
13. **Market power** — ability to profitably raise prices above competitive levels. Depends on: market concentration
14. **Exclusionary conduct** — conduct that harms rivals without legitimate business justification. Depends on: monopolization
15. **Anticompetitive intent** — evidence (often from documents) showing purpose to exclude competitors. Depends on: exclusionary conduct
16. **Tying arrangements** — conditioning sale of one product on purchase of another. Depends on: monopolization
17. **Essential facilities doctrine** — when a dominant firm must grant rivals access to critical infrastructure. Depends on: monopolization, exclusionary conduct
18. **Resale price maintenance (RPM)** — manufacturer sets minimum or maximum resale prices
19. **Leegin decision** — Supreme Court switch from per se to rule of reason for RPM. Depends on: resale price maintenance, rule of reason
20. **Exclusive dealing** — buyer or seller commits to deal exclusively with one party
21. **Foreclosure** — raising rivals' costs or denying access to inputs/customers. Depends on: exclusive dealing
22. **MFN clauses** — contractual provisions requiring parity with other trading partners
23. **Pre-merger notification** — mandatory reporting of large mergers before closing (HSR Act)
24. **Unilateral effects** — merger harms competition by eliminating direct rivalry without coordination. Depends on: market concentration
25. **Coordinated effects** — merger makes tacit collusion more likely by reducing number of competitors or increasing transparency. Depends on: market concentration
26. **Structural remedies** — divestiture of assets to restore competition
27. **Behavioral remedies** — ongoing obligations to prevent anticompetitive conduct (licensing, firewalls, conduct restrictions)
28. **Two-sided markets** — platforms serving distinct customer groups with cross-side network effects
29. **Network effects** — value increases as more users adopt, creating barriers to entry. Depends on: two-sided markets
30. **Self-preferencing** — dominant platform favoring its own products/services over rivals. Depends on: monopolization, exclusionary conduct
31. **Algorithmic pricing** — pricing determined by algorithms, raising tacit collusion concerns
32. **Ex-ante regulation** — preemptive rules for "gatekeepers" (EU DMA) vs. ex-post case-by-case enforcement (US antitrust)

## Dependencies

### Foundation → Application

- **Market definition** is prerequisite for nearly all substantive analysis — you can't assess competitive effects without knowing the relevant market
- **Rule of reason** depends on understanding **consumer welfare standard** — the balancing test weighs harms/benefits against that benchmark
- **Exclusionary conduct** requires distinguishing from vigorous competition, which depends on understanding **market power** (no concern if firm lacks power)

### Horizontal → Vertical → Merger

- **Horizontal agreements** (competitor coordination) is conceptually simpler than **vertical restraints** (supplier-distributor) because horizontal harms are more direct
- **Foreclosure** theory in vertical restraints carries over to **vertical merger** analysis
- **MFN clauses** bridge vertical restraints and platform/digital markets (used by Amazon, booking sites)

### Merger Analysis Integration

- **Unilateral effects** relies on **market concentration** and **market definition**
- **Coordinated effects** builds on **horizontal agreements** logic (tacit vs. explicit collusion)
- **Structural vs. behavioral remedies** choice depends on merger-specific facts but informed by monopolization case law (behavioral remedies are consent decree conditions)

### Digital Markets Synthesis

- **Two-sided markets** and **network effects** require rethinking traditional **market definition** methods
- **Self-preferencing** applies **exclusionary conduct** framework from monopolization to platform settings
- **Algorithmic pricing** raises **coordinated effects** concerns similar to trade association information exchange, but without explicit agreement

## Critical Bottlenecks

### Market Definition

Nearly all analysis bottlenecks at market definition. If you define the market too broadly, the firm looks small and harmless. Too narrow, and trivial conduct looks monopolistic. Master the SSNIP test and substitution analysis early.

### Rule of Reason Framework

Most modern cases use rule of reason (only naked cartels are per se). Understanding how to structure procompetitive justifications and measure net effects is essential for vertical restraints, monopolization defenses, and merger efficiencies.

### Digital Platform Economics

Traditional tools (HHI, price-based market definition) struggle with zero-price markets, multi-sided platforms, and dynamic competition. Need to understand network effects, multi-homing, and data-driven barriers to entry as distinct concepts.

## Common Misconception Linkages

- **Monopoly ≠ monopolization**: Having market power is legal; exclusionary conduct to get/keep it is not
- **Vertical restraints ≠ always bad**: Post-Leegin, many vertical restraints are procompetitive (solve free-rider problems, enable service competition)
- **Concentration ≠ harm**: High HHI triggers scrutiny but doesn't prove anticompetitive effects (efficiencies may outweigh)
- **Platform dominance ≠ antitrust violation**: Network effects create natural concentration; need exclusionary conduct or abuse of dominance, not just size

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Microeconomics** — needed for: market failure, deadweight loss, elasticity, strategic behavior (game theory for coordinated effects)
- **Basic legal reasoning** — needed for: statutory interpretation, burden of proof, standard of review, case law precedent
- **Contracts** — needed for: vertical restraints (RPM, exclusive dealing, tying), merger remedies
- **Industrial organization** — helpful for: oligopoly theory, entry barriers, predatory pricing models

## Learning Checkpoints

Students should be able to:

1. After Foundations (lessons 1-4): Define relevant markets and calculate HHI for simple scenarios
2. After Horizontal Restraints (lessons 5-9): Distinguish per se vs. rule of reason and identify circumstantial evidence of coordination
3. After Monopolization (lessons 10-14): Apply Section 2 framework to tech company fact patterns
4. After Vertical Restraints (lessons 15-18): Evaluate procompetitive justifications for RPM and exclusive dealing
5. After Merger Control (lessons 19-23): Perform basic unilateral effects analysis and design remedies
6. After Digital Markets (lessons 24-28): Critique traditional tools and apply platform-specific frameworks
