# International Law and Human Rights — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Sovereignty** — the principle that states have supreme authority within their territory and equality in international relations
2. **Consent** — the foundation of international law; states are bound only by obligations they accept
3. **International legal order** — the system of rules, institutions, and practices governing relations between states and other international actors
4. **Monism vs. dualism** — competing theories on the relationship between international and domestic law. Depends on: sovereignty, consent
5. **International legal personality** — the capacity to hold rights and obligations under international law. Depends on: international legal order
6. **Treaties** — binding written agreements between states or international organizations. Depends on: consent, sovereignty
7. **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties** — the "treaty on treaties"; codifies rules for treaty formation, interpretation, and termination. Depends on: treaties
8. **Pacta sunt servanda** — the principle that agreements must be kept. Depends on: treaties, consent
9. **Customary international law** — law arising from consistent state practice followed out of legal obligation. Depends on: state practice, opinio juris
10. **Opinio juris** — the belief that a practice is legally required. Depends on: customary international law
11. **State practice** — what states actually do in their international relations. Depends on: customary international law
12. **Jus cogens** — peremptory norms that cannot be derogated from by treaty. Depends on: customary international law, treaties
13. **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** — foundational 1948 document articulating fundamental human rights. Depends on: international legal order
14. **Universalism** — the view that human rights apply to all persons regardless of culture or context. Depends on: UDHR
15. **Cultural relativism** — the view that rights and values are culturally specific. Depends on: universalism (as counterpoint)
16. **Civil and political rights** — "first generation" rights protecting individual freedom (life, liberty, expression, fair trial). Depends on: UDHR
17. **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** — binding treaty elaborating civil and political rights. Depends on: UDHR, civil and political rights
18. **Economic, social, and cultural rights** — "second generation" rights to work, education, health, housing. Depends on: UDHR
19. **International Covenant on Economic, Social, and Cultural Rights (ICESCR)** — binding treaty on ESC rights. Depends on: UDHR, economic, social, and cultural rights
20. **Progressive realization** — principle that ESC rights are achieved over time as resources permit. Depends on: ICESCR
21. **State obligations** — duties to respect, protect, and fulfill human rights. Depends on: ICCPR, ICESCR
22. **Derogation** — temporary suspension of certain rights during emergencies. Depends on: state obligations, ICCPR
23. **CEDAW and CRC** — treaties on women's and children's rights, recognizing special vulnerabilities. Depends on: UDHR, state obligations
24. **Regional human rights systems** — European, Inter-American, African systems with own courts and mechanisms. Depends on: UDHR, treaties
25. **International humanitarian law (IHL)** — law of armed conflict; protects persons during war. Depends on: international legal order
26. **Armed conflict** — threshold for IHL application (international or non-international). Depends on: IHL
27. **Geneva Conventions** — four treaties (1949) protecting wounded, prisoners of war, and civilians. Depends on: IHL
28. **Distinction** — IHL principle requiring parties to distinguish between combatants and civilians. Depends on: Geneva Conventions
29. **Proportionality** — principle limiting collateral civilian harm relative to military advantage. Depends on: distinction
30. **Military necessity** — principle allowing only force necessary to achieve legitimate military objectives. Depends on: proportionality, distinction
31. **War crimes** — serious violations of IHL (e.g., targeting civilians, torture). Depends on: Geneva Conventions
32. **Crimes against humanity** — widespread or systematic attacks on civilians. Depends on: war crimes (related concept)
33. **Genocide** — acts intended to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Depends on: crimes against humanity
34. **International Court of Justice (ICJ)** — UN's principal judicial organ; resolves disputes between states. Depends on: international legal order, treaties
35. **ICJ jurisdiction** — based on state consent through treaty, declaration, or forum prorogatum. Depends on: ICJ, consent
36. **Advisory opinions** — non-binding legal advice given by ICJ to UN organs. Depends on: ICJ
37. **International Criminal Court (ICC)** — permanent tribunal prosecuting individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes. Depends on: war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide
38. **Rome Statute** — treaty establishing the ICC. Depends on: ICC, treaties
39. **Complementarity** — ICC acts only when national courts are unwilling or unable to prosecute. Depends on: ICC jurisdiction
40. **Treaty bodies** — committees monitoring state compliance with human rights treaties. Depends on: treaties, state obligations
41. **State reporting** — periodic reports states submit to treaty bodies. Depends on: treaty bodies
42. **Individual complaints** — mechanism allowing individuals to petition treaty bodies. Depends on: treaty bodies
43. **Universal jurisdiction** — principle allowing states to prosecute certain crimes (torture, genocide) regardless of where committed. Depends on: war crimes, crimes against humanity
44. **State immunity** — protection of officials from foreign prosecution. Depends on: sovereignty, universal jurisdiction (tension)
45. **State responsibility** — framework for holding states accountable for internationally wrongful acts. Depends on: state obligations, international legal order
46. **Reparations** — remedies for violations (restitution, compensation, satisfaction). Depends on: state responsibility
47. **Non-refoulement** — prohibition on returning refugees to places where they face persecution. Depends on: 1951 Refugee Convention
48. **Business and human rights** — framework for corporate accountability for rights abuses. Depends on: state obligations, international legal personality

## Dependencies

### Foundational Clusters
- **Sovereignty, consent, international legal order** form the conceptual foundation. All other concepts build on these.
- **Treaties and customary law** are the two primary sources; students must understand both to grasp how international law develops.

### Human Rights Architecture
- **UDHR → ICCPR/ICESCR → specialized treaties** represents the chronological and conceptual development of the international human rights system.
- **Universalism vs. cultural relativism** is a recurring tension students must navigate throughout the curriculum.
- **State obligations** (respect, protect, fulfill) anchor all human rights concepts.

### IHL Branch
- **Armed conflict threshold → Geneva Conventions → IHL principles (distinction, proportionality, military necessity)** is the IHL logic chain.
- **IHL and human rights law** operate in parallel but sometimes overlap (especially in occupation scenarios).

### Enforcement Mechanisms
- **ICJ (state-to-state) vs. ICC (individual criminal responsibility)** represent different enforcement models.
- **Treaty bodies** provide ongoing monitoring; **courts** provide binding judgments.
- **Universal jurisdiction** bridges gaps when territorial or nationality jurisdiction is absent.
- **State immunity** creates tension with accountability goals.

### Contemporary Applications
- **Climate, migration, business and human rights** apply foundational concepts to emerging challenges.
- Students must synthesize earlier learning to analyze these complex, multi-faceted issues.

## Bottlenecks

### 1. Consent and Sovereignty
If students don't grasp why states would bind themselves to international law, the entire system appears arbitrary. Emphasize **reciprocity, reputation, and institutional design** as motivations.

### 2. Custom vs. Treaty
Students often confuse these sources. Use concrete examples (diplomatic immunity from custom, Geneva Conventions from treaty) to distinguish.

### 3. IHL vs. Human Rights Law
The relationship between these regimes confuses many students. Clarify:
- **IHL** = law of armed conflict (lex specialis in war)
- **Human rights law** = peacetime and wartime (continues to apply)
- Some rights are non-derogable even in conflict

### 4. Enforcement Gaps
Students may become disillusioned when they learn how limited enforcement mechanisms are. Frame this as:
- **Realistic assessment** of current system
- **Opportunities for improvement** through advocacy and reform

### 5. Jurisdiction and Immunities
The interplay of **territorial, nationality, universal jurisdiction** and **state/diplomatic immunity** is complex. Use case studies (Pinochet, ICJ cases) to make concrete.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "International law has no teeth"
**Reality:** While enforcement is decentralized, most states comply most of the time due to reciprocity, reputation, and domestic incorporation.

### 2. "The UN creates international law"
**Reality:** The UN facilitates treaty-making and codification, but states (through consent and practice) create the law.

### 3. "Human rights are Western impositions"
**Reality:** UDHR was drafted by diverse committee (including Eleanor Roosevelt, Charles Malik, P.C. Chang); non-Western states have contributed significantly to rights development.

### 4. "The ICC can prosecute anyone anywhere"
**Reality:** ICC has limited jurisdiction—only for crimes in member states or by nationals of member states (or Security Council referral).

### 5. "Customary law is just state practice"
**Reality:** Custom requires both practice AND opinio juris (belief it's legally required).

### 6. "All human rights are immediately enforceable"
**Reality:** Civil and political rights are generally immediate; economic, social, and cultural rights allow progressive realization.

### 7. "IHL allows war"
**Reality:** IHL regulates HOW war is conducted (jus in bello); it doesn't address WHETHER war is lawful (jus ad bellum, covered by UN Charter).

## Prerequisite Topics

### From Outside This Curriculum
- **Basic legal reasoning** — understanding concepts like jurisdiction, liability, remedies (needed throughout)
- **Political science fundamentals** — state sovereignty, international organizations, power dynamics (needed for: sovereignty, consent, enforcement)
- **Modern history** — WWII, decolonization, Cold War (needed for: UDHR, Geneva Conventions, ICJ/ICC development)
- **Logic and argumentation** — for analyzing case law and treaty interpretation (needed for: legal reasoning throughout)

### Internal Prerequisites (Within Curriculum)
- **Modules 1-2** prerequisite for all later modules (foundation and sources)
- **Module 3** prerequisite for Module 5 (must understand rights before accountability mechanisms)
- **Module 4** stands somewhat alone but connects to Module 5 (enforcement)
- **Module 6** synthesizes all earlier modules

## Pedagogical Notes

- **Concept density increases** from Modules 1-2 (foundational, broad) to Modules 4-5 (technical, specific).
- **Case law and examples** essential for making abstract concepts concrete.
- **Primary sources** (treaty excerpts, case excerpts) should be introduced gradually, with scaffolding.
- **Visual aids** helpful for institutional structures (UN human rights system, ICC vs. ICJ) and procedural flows.
- **Current events** can motivate learning but risk politicization—maintain analytical distance.
