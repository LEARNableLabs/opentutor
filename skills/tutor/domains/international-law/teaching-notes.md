# International Law and Human Rights — Teaching Notes

## Approach

International law and human rights is a **doctrinal yet applied** field—students need both theoretical foundations (treaties, custom, jurisdiction) and practical skills (case analysis, advocacy framing, policy critique). At the intermediate level, emphasize **real-world cases and contemporary challenges** to maintain engagement while building technical knowledge. Use a **Socratic approach** for conceptual tensions (universalism vs. cultural relativism, sovereignty vs. accountability) and **case-based learning** for enforcement mechanisms. The field is inherently **interdisciplinary** (law, politics, history, ethics), so draw connections across domains and highlight competing perspectives rather than presenting one "right" answer.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "International law is just aspirational—states ignore it whenever convenient"
**Why this is wrong:** While compliance isn't perfect, most states follow most international law most of the time. Violations are newsworthy precisely because they're exceptional. States comply due to reciprocity (they want others to comply too), reputation costs (being seen as a "rogue state"), and domestic legal incorporation.

**How to correct:** Use data showing high compliance rates (e.g., most treaties are honored most of the time). Discuss WHY states comply even without a world government (reciprocity, iteration, reputational costs). Compare to domestic law—people also violate national laws, but we don't say domestic law is "just aspirational."

### 2. "The UN creates and enforces international law"
**Why this is wrong:** States create international law through treaties and customary practice. The UN facilitates treaty-making, provides a forum for negotiation, and hosts some judicial/quasi-judicial bodies, but the UN itself doesn't legislate or enforce like a domestic government.

**How to correct:** Distinguish between (a) the UN as a forum/facilitator, (b) treaties negotiated through the UN system, and (c) UN organs with specific functions (ICJ, Security Council, treaty bodies). Emphasize that sovereignty and consent remain central—states bind themselves.

### 3. "Human rights are a Western concept imposed on the Global South"
**Why this is wrong:** While Enlightenment ideas influenced human rights discourse, the UDHR was drafted by a diverse committee including non-Western members (Charles Malik of Lebanon, P.C. Chang of China, Hansa Mehta of India). Non-Western states and activists have been central to human rights development (Latin American contributions to asylum law, African contributions to self-determination, Asian contributions to right to development).

**How to correct:** Highlight diverse origins of human rights ideas (Islamic law on charity and justice, Confucian ideas on reciprocal obligations, Ubuntu philosophy, Latin American asylum traditions). Acknowledge legitimate debates about priorities and implementation without dismissing universalism entirely. Distinguish between **universal norms** and **culturally specific applications**.

### 4. "The International Criminal Court can prosecute any world leader for atrocities"
**Why this is wrong:** The ICC has limited jurisdiction—it can only prosecute crimes committed (a) on the territory of a member state, (b) by nationals of a member state, or (c) when the Security Council refers a situation. Major powers (US, Russia, China, India) are not members, limiting the court's reach.

**How to correct:** Explain the **complementarity principle** (ICC only acts when national courts won't or can't) and **jurisdictional limits** (treaty-based, so only binds parties). Discuss the political constraints (Security Council veto power, non-cooperation by non-parties). Frame as both a **design choice** (states wouldn't have accepted broader jurisdiction) and an **ongoing challenge** for international justice.

### 5. "Customary international law is just what states repeatedly do"
**Why this is wrong:** Custom requires TWO elements: (1) consistent state practice AND (2) opinio juris (belief that the practice is legally required). Repeated action alone isn't enough—states must believe they're legally obligated.

**How to correct:** Use examples where repeated practice doesn't create custom (diplomatic gifts are customary but not legally required) vs. where it does (diplomatic immunity is both practiced and considered legally obligatory). Emphasize that opinio juris is what distinguishes legal obligation from mere habit or courtesy.

### 6. "All human rights must be immediately realized"
**Why this is wrong:** While civil and political rights generally require immediate compliance (states can't gradually stop torturing), economic, social, and cultural rights are subject to **progressive realization**—states must take steps to the maximum of available resources, but full realization may take time.

**How to correct:** Distinguish between (a) **negative obligations** (don't torture, don't censor) that are immediate, and (b) **positive obligations** (provide healthcare, education) that require resources and infrastructure. Emphasize that even ESC rights have a **minimum core** that must be satisfied immediately (e.g., basic food security, primary education).

### 7. "International humanitarian law permits war"
**Why this is wrong:** IHL regulates **how** armed conflict is conducted (protecting civilians, limiting means and methods of warfare) but doesn't address **whether** going to war is lawful. That's governed by the UN Charter and the law on the use of force (jus ad bellum vs. jus in bello).

**How to correct:** Clarify that IHL is **neutral**—it applies equally to all parties in a conflict regardless of who started it or whose cause is "just." This is a design feature: protecting civilians and wounded soldiers shouldn't depend on who's "right." Separate the ethics/legality of war itself from the rules governing wartime conduct.

### 8. "Refugees are just economic migrants seeking a better life"
**Why this is wrong:** Refugees have a specific legal definition under the 1951 Refugee Convention: someone outside their country with a **well-founded fear of persecution** based on race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. Economic migrants don't qualify.

**How to correct:** Explain the **refugee definition** and the principle of **non-refoulement** (can't return someone to a place where they face persecution). Acknowledge that mixed flows exist (both refugees and economic migrants may travel together) but emphasize the legal distinction and protection obligations. Discuss "asylum shopping" concerns vs. genuine protection needs.

### 9. "State sovereignty means states can do whatever they want inside their borders"
**Why this is wrong:** While sovereignty remains a core principle, international human rights law and the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine have established that states have obligations to their populations. Sovereignty is increasingly framed as **responsibility**, not absolute freedom from external scrutiny.

**How to correct:** Trace the evolution from Westphalian sovereignty (1648) to post-WWII human rights order to R2P (2005). Emphasize that states still have primary authority, but **egregious violations** (genocide, crimes against humanity) can trigger international concern. Frame as a tension to be managed, not a binary (sovereignty vs. intervention).

### 10. "International law is only about states—individuals don't matter"
**Why this is wrong:** While states remain the primary subjects, individuals have gained increasing legal personality in international law: they can bring complaints to human rights treaty bodies and regional courts, and they can be prosecuted before international criminal tribunals.

**How to correct:** Trace the evolution of individual standing (Nuremberg trials onward). Distinguish between **rights** (individuals increasingly hold them) and **enforcement capacity** (still mostly state-driven, but growing individual access). Highlight regional courts (ECHR, Inter-American Court) where individuals have direct access.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate Level (This Curriculum)
- **Assume:** basic legal literacy (understanding of jurisdiction, liability, remedies), awareness of global governance structures, familiarity with major historical events (WWII, decolonization, Cold War)
- **Emphasize:** case law, treaty interpretation, interplay between different regimes (IHL vs. HR law), enforcement mechanisms, contemporary challenges
- **Formalism:** moderate—introduce treaty texts and case excerpts with scaffolding; don't assume ability to parse complex legal language independently
- **Depth:** go beyond black-letter law to explore **tensions and trade-offs** (universalism vs. cultural specificity, sovereignty vs. accountability, ideal vs. feasible)
- **Pedagogy:** mix mini-lessons (doctrinal foundations), case studies (real-world application), Socratic questioning (conceptual tensions), and teach-backs (student synthesis)

### If Adjusting Down (Beginner)
- **Reduce:** technical terminology, treaty text excerpts, complex jurisdictional rules
- **Increase:** historical context, visual aids (timelines, organizational charts), concrete examples, narrative storytelling
- **Simplify:** focus on big ideas (why human rights matter, how international law works) rather than technical details
- **Skip or gloss:** jus cogens, complementarity, progressive realization nuances, universal jurisdiction complexities

### If Adjusting Up (Advanced)
- **Increase:** primary source analysis (full case opinions, treaty travaux préparatoires), critical scholarly debates, theoretical frameworks (legal positivism vs. natural law, realism vs. liberalism)
- **Add:** comparative analysis across legal systems, engagement with academic literature, more complex jurisdictional puzzles
- **Deepen:** philosophical foundations (what grounds human rights?), institutional design critiques, reform proposals
- **Expect:** independent research, synthesis across multiple sources, original argumentation

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections to Drop In)

### 1. The Eichmann Trial and Universal Jurisdiction
**Connection:** When covering universal jurisdiction (Lesson 24), mention Israel's prosecution of Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann in 1961. He was captured in Argentina and tried in Israel for crimes committed in Europe during WWII. Raises questions: Can states prosecute crimes committed elsewhere by non-nationals against non-nationals? What about state sovereignty and kidnapping across borders?

**When to drop:** During enforcement module, when discussing how gaps in territorial/nationality jurisdiction are filled. Pairs well with Pinochet case (Chile's former dictator arrested in UK on Spain's request).

### 2. Lotus Principle ("Everything Not Prohibited Is Permitted")
**Connection:** The 1927 Lotus case (France v. Turkey at the Permanent Court of International Justice) established that states are free to act unless specifically prohibited by international law. This contrasts with domestic legal systems where governments can only do what they're authorized to do. Frames the permissive nature of international law.

**When to drop:** When discussing sovereignty and consent in foundations module. Helps explain why international law looks so different from domestic law.

### 3. Jus Cogens and the Slavery Example
**Connection:** Jus cogens norms are so fundamental they can't be violated by treaty. The prohibition on slavery is the clearest example—no treaty could legitimize slavery, even if all parties consented. This challenges the consent-based foundation of international law.

**When to drop:** When discussing treaty hierarchy (Lesson 9). Creates productive tension: if international law is consent-based, how can some norms be non-derogable?

### 4. The Right to Development Debate
**Connection:** Developing countries (especially in the Non-Aligned Movement) pushed for a "right to development" recognizing that structural inequalities limit realization of other rights. Developed countries resisted, seeing it as a claim to redistribution. Highlights North-South tensions in human rights.

**When to drop:** When discussing universalism vs. cultural relativism (Lesson 10) or ESC rights (Lesson 12). Shows that disagreements aren't just East-West or "liberal vs. traditional," but also about economic justice.

### 5. R2P (Responsibility to Protect) and Libya vs. Syria
**Connection:** R2P doctrine holds that when states fail to protect populations from atrocity crimes, the international community has a responsibility to act. It was invoked to justify intervention in Libya (2011) but not Syria (despite worse atrocities). Why? Security Council politics and the veto.

**When to drop:** When discussing enforcement gaps and state sovereignty (Lessons 4, 21-22). Illustrates both the promise and limits of international accountability.

### 6. The Martens Clause
**Connection:** The Martens Clause (from 1899 Hague Convention, restated in Geneva Conventions) says that even if a situation isn't explicitly covered by treaty, combatants and civilians remain under protection of "the principles of humanity and the dictates of public conscience." It fills gaps in IHL.

**When to drop:** When teaching IHL fundamentals (Lessons 16-17). Shows that law isn't just rules written down—principles and conscience also matter.

### 7. Erga Omnes Obligations
**Connection:** Erga omnes ("owed to all") obligations are so important that every state has a legal interest in their compliance—not just directly affected states. Examples: prohibitions on genocide, slavery, torture. Any state can bring a claim if violated.

**When to drop:** When discussing state responsibility (Lesson 25). Connects to universal jurisdiction and the idea that some violations concern all humanity.

### 8. The Alien Tort Statute and Corporate Accountability
**Connection:** A 1789 US law allowed foreign nationals to sue in US courts for violations of international law. It was used to sue corporations for complicity in human rights abuses abroad (e.g., Shell in Nigeria, Unocal in Myanmar). After Kiobel (2013), the Supreme Court narrowed its scope, but it pioneered business and human rights litigation.

**When to drop:** When discussing business and human rights (Lesson 28). Shows how creative lawyering can open accountability pathways.

### 9. The "Unwilling or Unable" Debate in Self-Defense
**Connection:** When non-state actors (e.g., terrorists) operate from a state's territory, can the victim state use force in self-defense if the territorial state is "unwilling or unable" to stop them? This was invoked for strikes against ISIS in Syria and Taliban in Afghanistan. Controversial—some see it as expanding self-defense, others as violating sovereignty.

**When to drop:** When discussing sovereignty (Lesson 1) or IHL thresholds (Lesson 16). Illustrates how doctrine evolves in response to new threats.

### 10. The International Law Commission (ILC)
**Connection:** The ILC is a UN body of legal experts that drafts model texts to codify and progressively develop international law. Many major treaties (Vienna Convention, Rome Statute provisions) originated as ILC drafts. It's like a "legislature" without legislative power—states still have to adopt its work.

**When to drop:** When discussing sources of law (Lessons 5, 7). Shows the interplay between scholarly expertise and state consent.

## Difficulty Progression

- **Lessons 1-6:** Difficulty 1-3, accessible introduction to foundational concepts (sovereignty, treaties, legal personality). Review at Lesson 6 consolidates basics.
  
- **Lessons 7-13:** Difficulty 2-4, building complexity with custom, jus cogens, and human rights treaties. Peak difficulty at Lesson 9 (treaty conflicts and jus cogens). Review at Lesson 13.

- **Lessons 14-20:** Difficulty 2-4, parallel track through IHL with mid-range complexity. Peak at Lesson 19 (distinguishing war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide). Review at Lesson 20.

- **Lessons 21-25:** Difficulty 3-4, most technically demanding module on enforcement. Peaks at Lessons 22 (ICC jurisdiction and complementarity) and 24 (universal jurisdiction and immunities).

- **Lessons 26-28:** Difficulty 3-4, contemporary challenges require synthesizing earlier learning. No review (end of curriculum), but teach-back format at Lesson 28 reinforces retention.

**Pacing note:** If student struggles during enforcement module (21-25), consider adding a mid-module review or splitting complex lessons. If student breezes through, compress some mini-lessons and add a challenging case study (e.g., ICJ advisory opinion on Nuclear Weapons, ICC Al Bashir arrest warrant saga).
