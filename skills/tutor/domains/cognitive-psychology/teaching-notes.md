# Cognitive Psychology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Cognitive psychology is experientially rich but theoretically dense. At the intermediate level, anchor every concept in demonstrations and real-world phenomena that students can introspect about, then scaffold up to formal models. The field is methodologically driven — students should see not just WHAT we know but HOW we discovered it through clever experiments. Cognitive psychology is uniquely positioned to connect three perspectives: phenomenology (what it feels like), behavior (what we can measure), and mechanism (how the brain implements it).

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Memory works like a video recording"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Introspection makes vivid memories feel like perfect playback
   - **How to correct**: Show research on memory reconstruction (Bartlett's War of the Ghosts), false memories, eyewitness testimony failures. Demo in Lesson 13

2. **"If I'm paying attention to something, I'll notice everything about it"**
   - **Why students believe this**: We only notice what we notice; inattentional blindness is invisible to us
   - **How to correct**: Show gorilla/basketball video, change blindness demos. Make them experience the failure firsthand. Lesson 6

3. **"Multitasking is just a skill you can get better at"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Switching feels fast and people differ in apparent ability
   - **How to correct**: Explain dual-task costs, show that "good multitaskers" are actually good at task-switching, not parallel processing. Show research on driving while texting. Lesson 5

4. **"Working memory and short-term memory are the same thing"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Many intro psych courses conflate them; both involve temporary storage
   - **How to correct**: Emphasize that working memory includes manipulation/processing, not just storage. Show how Baddeley's model adds the central executive. Lesson 10

5. **"Heuristics and biases prove humans are irrational"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Popular science emphasizes failures; Kahneman's framing in "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
   - **How to correct**: Show ecological rationality perspective (Gigerenzer). Heuristics are adaptive shortcuts; they fail in specific environments. Lesson 21

6. **"System 1 is bad/primitive, System 2 is good/advanced"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Dual-process framing can sound like a hierarchy; "fast thinking" associated with errors
   - **How to correct**: Emphasize both systems are adaptive. System 1 enables expert intuition, fast reactions, social cognition. System 2 is slow and effortful. Lesson 20

7. **"Forgetting happens because the memory trace decays/disappears"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Intuitive physics analogy (things fade over time)
   - **How to correct**: Show interference theory, retrieval-induced forgetting, context-dependent memory. Often the memory is still there but inaccessible. Lesson 15

8. **"Attention is binary — you're either paying attention or not"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Subjective experience feels all-or-nothing
   - **How to correct**: Introduce graded attention, attentional resources model, divided attention costs. Show attention as allocation, not on/off switch. Lessons 3-7

9. **"If something is familiar, it must have happened before (source monitoring confusion)"**
   - **Why students believe this**: Familiarity is a strong memory signal
   - **How to correct**: Show cases of false recognition, cryptomnesia, and the difference between familiarity and recollection. Touch on this in Lesson 13

10. **"Experts always make better decisions than novices"**
    - **Why students believe this**: Expertise obviously helps in many domains
    - **How to correct**: Show when expert intuition fails (unpredictable environments), overconfidence, and clinical vs. statistical prediction. Lesson 22

## Level Adjustments

**Intermediate level specifics:**

- **Depth of mechanism**: Introduce neural substrates (hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate) at a systems level, but don't require detailed circuit knowledge. Students should know WHERE functions localize but not HOW at the cellular level.

- **Formalism**: Use conceptual models (Baddeley's boxes and arrows, dual-process framework) rather than mathematical models. Save computational modeling for advanced students.

- **Research literacy**: Students should be able to read abstracts and understand experimental logic, but don't require stats beyond basic significance testing. Focus on clever paradigms (Stroop, change detection, framing manipulations).

- **Theoretical depth**: Contrast 2-3 competing theories (early vs. late selection in attention; trace decay vs. interference in forgetting) rather than cataloguing all historical positions.

- **Applications**: Connect to real-world domains (UI design, education, clinical disorders, legal system) more than intro level, but don't dive into specialized applications like advanced students would.

**Compared to beginner level**: More theoretical structure, multiple competing frameworks, methodological rigor, neural grounding.

**Compared to advanced level**: Less computational modeling, less neural detail, less methodological statistics, fewer historical nuances.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Tangents)

1. **The paradox of expert chess intuition**
   - Chess masters see thousands of patterns in System 1 (automatic), contradicting the idea that System 1 is crude
   - When to drop: Lesson 20 or 22, when discussing dual-process theory or expert judgment
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Reveals that expertise can make complex cognition automatic

2. **Permastore — the 50-year durability of some memories**
   - Bahrick's research showing Spanish vocabulary lasts 50 years without rehearsal after a plateau
   - When to drop: Lesson 15 on forgetting
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Challenges simple decay models; some memories are nearly permanent

3. **The "testing effect" — taking tests improves memory more than studying**
   - Retrieval practice causes better long-term retention than re-reading
   - When to drop: Lesson 13 on retrieval, or Lesson 26 on applications
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Counter-intuitive; students think tests measure learning, not cause it

4. **Sleep and memory consolidation — why all-nighters backfire**
   - Sleep-dependent memory consolidation, replay during slow-wave sleep
   - When to drop: Lesson 14 on consolidation
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Direct practical implications students care about

5. **The weapon focus effect in eyewitness memory**
   - Attention to weapon reduces memory for perpetrator's face
   - When to drop: Lesson 6 on attentional capture, or Lesson 13 on memory reconstruction
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Real-world consequence of attention-memory link; legal implications

6. **Savant syndrome and dissociations between memory systems**
   - Individuals with extraordinary memory in one domain but cognitive deficits in others
   - When to drop: Lesson 12 on episodic vs. semantic memory
   - Why it's mind-blowing: Shows memory isn't unitary; reveals functional architecture

## Difficulty Progression Notes

**Early lessons (1-8)**: Start accessible (difficulty 2) with attention phenomena that are experientially obvious. Build to difficulty 3-4 with executive attention and neural mechanisms. Include first review at lesson 8.

**Middle lessons (9-16)**: Memory systems start accessible (working memory is intuitive) at difficulty 2-3, then build to difficulty 4 with reconsolidation and consolidation mechanisms. Second review at lesson 16.

**Later lessons (17-23)**: Decision-making starts at difficulty 3 with familiar examples (framing effects) and builds to difficulty 4 with dual-process theory and bounded rationality. Third review at lesson 23.

**Final lessons (24-28)**: Integration and applications stay at difficulty 3-5. Peak difficulty (5) at lesson 27 on computational limits. End with active synthesis (teach-back) at difficulty 4.

**Review placement**: Lessons 8, 16, 23 — every 7-8 lessons, positioned after completing major modules.

**Difficulty peaks**: Lessons 7 (executive attention), 13 (reconsolidation), 20 (dual-process), 22 (expert judgment), 25 (stress and decision-making), 27 (computational limits).

## Assessment Strategies

**Conceptual understanding:**
- Ask students to distinguish between similar concepts (episodic vs. semantic, automatic vs. controlled, System 1 vs. System 2)
- Pose novel scenarios and ask which theory predicts the outcome
- Identify misconceptions in everyday claims about memory/attention

**Application:**
- Design an experiment to test a specific hypothesis
- Predict behavior in a real-world scenario using cognitive principles
- Critique a popular press article about cognitive psychology

**Integration:**
- Explain how a phenomenon (e.g., eyewitness errors) involves attention, memory, and decision-making
- Compare explanations from different theoretical perspectives
- Draw connections between behavioral phenomena and neural mechanisms

**Formative checks:**
- After attention module (Lesson 8): Can student explain why divided attention has costs?
- After memory module (Lesson 16): Can student describe Baddeley's model and explain a memory failure using it?
- After decision-making module (Lesson 23): Can student identify heuristics in their own recent decisions?

**Summative synthesis:**
- Final teach-back (Lesson 28): Student designs a complete experiment, showing understanding of paradigms, hypotheses, and measurement
- Integration question (Lesson 27): Student explains computational constraints underlying all three domains

**Warning signs to adapt:**
- If student struggles with neural terminology → reduce mechanism detail, focus on functional description
- If student breezes through dual-process → introduce formal computational models or neuroscience details
- If student confuses theories → add more contrasting examples showing when each theory succeeds/fails
