# Science and Technology Studies (STS) — Teaching Notes

## Approach

STS is fundamentally **case-driven and reflexive**. At the intermediate level, balance theoretical frameworks with rich empirical examples. Don't start with abstract theory—begin with puzzles and controversies that make the theory necessary. The field trains students to denaturalize the taken-for-granted, so pedagogy should model this: ask "why is this technology designed this way?" rather than accepting it as inevitable. STS is also inherently interdisciplinary, so draw connections to sociology, history, philosophy, and current events throughout.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Social construction means it's not real or just made up"**
   - Students often conflate constructivism with relativism or anti-realism. Emphasize: saying DNA's structure was socially constructed doesn't mean it's fake—it means understanding how Watson & Crick's discovery happened requires examining their lab practices, institutional context, competitive pressures, and credibility work, not just "they looked and saw the truth."
   - Use Latour's phrase: "constructed" doesn't mean "fabricated" or "artificial"—it means actively built, the opposite of passive discovery.

2. **"ANT says scallops have agency like humans do"**
   - The symmetry principle is methodological, not ontological. Non-humans don't have intentions, but they do make a difference in networks—they enable and constrain action. A speed bump "acts" to slow cars without having will.
   - Frame it as: follow the actors (human and non-human) and see what they do, rather than deciding in advance what can or can't be influential.

3. **"STS scholars are anti-science"**
   - Many students (especially STEM backgrounds) initially perceive STS as hostile to science. Clarify: STS examines how science works as a social practice, which is different from dismissing scientific knowledge. Many STS scholars have science backgrounds and deeply respect scientific inquiry.
   - Emphasize: understanding science as a social process can make it *stronger*, not weaker.

4. **"Technological determinism is always wrong"**
   - The critique isn't that technology has zero influence, but that pure determinism ("the technology caused X to happen") ignores how technologies are designed, adopted, and adapted by social processes.
   - Technologies do shape possibilities, but within constraints set by social choices. It's mutual shaping, not one-way causation.

5. **"SCOT and ANT are competing theories—which one is correct?"**
   - They're complementary frameworks with different emphases. SCOT focuses on social groups and interpretive flexibility; ANT emphasizes heterogeneous networks and translation. Both reject determinism. Students should learn when each framework is most useful.
   - SCOT: better for design and closure processes. ANT: better for complex assemblages and laboratory work.

6. **"Lay people can't really be experts"**
   - Students often conflate credentials with competence. Use strong cases: sheep farmers' knowledge of their land after Chernobyl, AIDS activists' epidemiological sophistication, environmental justice communities' pollution mapping.
   - The point isn't that professionals are wrong, but that expertise is distributed and situated—local knowledge matters.

7. **"This is all just critical theory jargon"**
   - STS can feel buzzword-heavy (actor-networks, black-boxing, inscription devices). Always ground concepts in concrete examples before using technical terms. Let the cases teach the theory.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate vs. Beginner
- **More primary sources**: At intermediate level, students should read key STS papers (Bijker on bicycles, Latour on Pasteur, Haraway's Cyborg Manifesto), not just summaries.
- **Theoretical comparison**: Compare frameworks explicitly (SCOT vs. ANT, feminist critiques of both).
- **Application practice**: Students analyze contemporary cases using STS frameworks, not just learn historical examples.
- **Methodological awareness**: Introduce how STS scholars do research (ethnography, archive work, controversy mapping).

### Intermediate vs. Advanced
- **Less philosophical depth**: Don't dive into ontological debates (Barad's agential realism, Stengers' cosmopolitics) unless student asks.
- **Fewer primary theoretical texts**: Use accessible introductions (Sismondo) before full Latour or Haraway.
- **Guided controversy analysis**: Provide structured frameworks for analyzing cases rather than expecting students to develop their own analytical approach from scratch.

## Difficulty Progression

1. **Start with intuitive cases** (lessons 1-5): Bicycles, bridges with low clearances, technology you use daily. Build from concrete to abstract.

2. **Introduce frameworks one at a time** (lessons 6-11): Master SCOT before adding ANT. Don't pile on feminist STS, postcolonial STS, and environmental STS simultaneously.

3. **Peak difficulty at application** (lessons 14-15, 20-21): Applying frameworks to unfamiliar cases (cyborg theory, postcolonial critique) is harder than learning them in familiar contexts.

4. **Reviews as breathers** (lessons 12, 19): After dense theoretical modules, give space to consolidate and connect ideas.

5. **End with synthesis** (lessons 22-24): Use controversy mapping and contemporary issues to show how multiple frameworks work together.

## Engagement Strategies

### Leverage current events
STS is maximally relevant right now: AI bias, climate denial, vaccine hesitancy, platform moderation, facial recognition. Connect lessons to headlines—students often have strong opinions, which creates teachable moments.

### Use provocative questions
STS challenges intuitions. Lean into that: "Do artifacts have politics?" "Should we be cyborgs?" "Who's the expert on your own neighborhood?"

### Vary delivery modes
- **Mini-lessons** for frameworks and concepts
- **Real-world cases** for application (Challenger disaster, COVID response)
- **Questions** for critical thinking (cyborg theory, algorithmic bias)
- **Teach-backs** to check understanding and build argumentation skills
- **Resource drops** for self-directed exploration (controversy mapping tools, STS journals)

### Watch for STEM resistance
Students with strong science/engineering backgrounds may initially bristle at constructivism. Don't argue—show. Use Latour's Pasteur case: even the most "purely scientific" discovery is thick with social maneuvering, and that doesn't diminish its truth.

## Rabbit Holes (and when to deploy them)

These are fascinating tangents—drop them in when students show interest or when they connect to current lessons:

1. **Kuhn's paradigm shifts** — after lesson 8 (laboratory studies). Students often know Kuhn and want to connect him to STS.

2. **Feminist science studies vs. feminist STS** — during lessons 13-14. Different projects: revising scientific content vs. examining how gender shapes knowledge production.

3. **Ontological turn in STS** — if students ask "but what's really real?" after ANT. Scholars like Annemarie Mol argue different practices enact different realities.

4. **Science and Technology Studies vs. Science, Technology, and Society** — both abbreviated STS! The former is more constructivist/analytical; the latter is more policy-oriented.

5. **Digital methods and computational STS** — after lesson 22 (controversy mapping). Tools like Gephi, Cortext, networks analysis for studying science.

6. **STS and design** — design justice, participatory design, values in design. Connects to embedded values and cyborg theory.

7. **More-than-human geography and multispecies ethnography** — extensions of ANT into environmental humanities and anthropology.

8. **Quantitative STS** — bibliometrics, citation analysis, co-word analysis. Shows STS isn't only qualitative.

9. **The science wars of the 1990s** — Sokal hoax, debates with scientists who felt STS was attacking them. Important context for "STS is anti-science" misconception.

10. **Platform studies and infrastructure studies** — contemporary branches of STS focusing on digital platforms, algorithms, data.

## Assessment Tips

- **For teach-backs**: Ask students to explain a framework using a case *different* from the one they learned it with. This tests transfer, not memorization.
- **For real-world analysis**: Provide a controversy (e.g., gene editing) and ask: What would a SCOT analysis reveal? An ANT analysis? What would feminist STS emphasize?
- **For concept checks**: Use multiple-choice questions that test misconceptions (e.g., "Social construction means: A) not real, B) socially influenced, C) actively built through social processes, D) arbitrary").

## Resources for Teaching Adaptations

If student struggles with:
- **Abstraction** → more concrete cases, historical narratives, visual aids
- **Theory density** → break frameworks into smaller pieces, use analogies
- **Primary sources** → provide guided reading questions, summaries first
- **Application** → scaffolded analysis templates, worked examples

If student excels:
- **Add methodological depth** → how would you study this controversy? Design a research project
- **Original analysis** → apply STS to their own field (engineering, medicine, data science)
- **Advanced readings** → full Latour (We Have Never Been Modern), Haraway, Jasanoff
- **Cross-connections** → how does STS relate to media studies, political economy, philosophy?
