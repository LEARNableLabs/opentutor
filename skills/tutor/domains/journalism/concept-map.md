## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **Source cultivation** builds on **story planning** and **information gaps** — you can't develop sources without knowing what you're trying to learn
- **Off-the-record protocols** require mastery of **attribution standards** and **trust building** — mishandling attribution destroys trust
- **Request drafting** depends on **FOIA mechanics** and **public records landscape** — effective requests require knowing what exists and how to ask for it
- **Pattern extraction** combines **document organization** and **pattern recognition** — you need both volume management and analytical skill

### Advanced Dependencies
- **Trauma-informed interviewing** builds on **rapport building** and **consent protocols** — technical skill without genuine care causes harm
- **Finding story angles** requires **pattern detection** and **outlier analysis** — you need to see both the forest and the unusual trees
- **Public interest calculus** integrates **risk assessment** and **public interest test** — deciding to publish despite threats requires weighing competing values
- **Fact-checking protocol** synthesizes **corroboration standards**, **data verification**, and **document authentication** — comprehensive verification draws from all evidence types

## Bottleneck Concepts

### Source Protection (Lessons 7-8)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Students must internalize that source safety isn't optional before conducting high-stakes investigations. Failure here ends careers and endangers lives.

**Teaching strategy**: Use real case studies of sources who were exposed. Make the consequences visceral before moving forward.

### FOIA Appeals Process (Lessons 10-11)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Many students give up after initial denials. Persistence with appeals is what separates productive records work from wasted effort.

**Teaching strategy**: Show examples of stories that only happened because of successful appeals. Normalize rejection as part of the process.

### Trauma-Informed Interviewing (Lesson 17)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Students coming from traditional hard-news backgrounds often resist what they see as "soft" interviewing. But investigations increasingly involve vulnerable subjects.

**Teaching strategy**: Frame this as both ethical and practical — re-traumatizing sources kills stories and harms people. Show examples where trauma-informed approaches yielded better journalism.

### Statistical Literacy (Lesson 22)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Many journalists fear math and avoid data stories. But modern investigations often require finding patterns in datasets.

**Teaching strategy**: Start with simple concepts (averages, percentages, trends). Build confidence before introducing more complex analysis. Emphasize that you don't need to be a statistician, just literate enough to spot problems.

### Public Interest Calculus (Lesson 26)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: This is where ethical reasoning meets real-world pressure. Students must develop independent judgment about when to publish despite legal threats, source concerns, or institutional pressure.

**Teaching strategy**: Use case studies with ambiguous answers. No single "right" decision — the goal is developing a defensible framework.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Investigative journalism is about secrets"
**Reality**: Most investigations use public information. The skill is knowing where to look and how to connect dots.

**Correction**: Emphasize FOIA and public records early. Show investigations built entirely from public sources.

### "Good sources volunteer information"
**Reality**: Most valuable sources need cultivation over time. Relationships are built through consistency and demonstrated trustworthiness.

**Correction**: Lessons 5-7 explicitly teach source development as a process, not a transaction.

### "Off the record means confidential"
**Reality**: "Off the record," "on background," and "not for attribution" have specific meanings that must be negotiated clearly.

**Correction**: Lesson 6 drills attribution standards. Emphasize that terms mean nothing without mutual understanding.

### "FOIA guarantees access"
**Reality**: FOIA is a tool that often requires appeals, negotiation, and persistence. Many records are exempt.

**Correction**: Lessons 9-11 teach both the mechanics and the realistic expectations. Show examples of both successes and limits.

### "Hostile interviews require aggression"
**Reality**: The best accountability interviews are firm but fair. Aggression often gives subjects an excuse to shut down or claim mistreatment.

**Correction**: Lesson 16 distinguishes confrontational from aggressive. Model strategic questioning over performative hostility.

### "Data journalism requires coding skills"
**Reality**: Basic spreadsheet analysis and clear thinking solve most data journalism needs. Advanced technical skills help but aren't required.

**Correction**: Lessons 19-20 teach spreadsheet-based analysis. Show students they already have the tools.

### "Ethics slow you down"
**Reality**: Ethical shortcuts create legal exposure and destroy credibility. The fastest way to kill a story is to cut corners on verification.

**Correction**: Lessons 24-27 frame ethics as protective, not restrictive. Ethical rigor is what allows publication.

### "Document authentication is for specialists"
**Reality**: Basic verification techniques (checking metadata, reverse image search, source confirmation) are accessible to all journalists.

**Correction**: Lesson 23 teaches practical verification tools, not advanced forensics.

### "Legal threats mean don't publish"
**Reality**: Many legal threats are bluffs. With proper fact-checking and legal review, important stories can withstand legal pressure.

**Correction**: Lessons 25-26 teach risk assessment and when to proceed despite threats. Build confidence in verified work.

### "Impact happens automatically if the story is good"
**Reality**: Impact requires strategy — timing, audience targeting, partnership with advocacy groups, follow-up coverage.

**Correction**: Lesson 28 explicitly teaches impact planning as part of publication strategy.
