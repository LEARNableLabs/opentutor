# Journalism and Investigative Reporting — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Investigative journalism is fundamentally about accountability, pattern recognition, and persistence. At the intermediate level, we assume students already know how to report a straightforward news story and are now learning to pursue complex, multi-month investigations that uncover systemic issues rather than surface-level incidents.

The teaching approach emphasizes **practical skills over theory**. Every concept is grounded in real investigative work — FOIA requests that took years, source relationships that required months of trust-building, ethical dilemmas with no clean answers. Journalism is learned by doing, so lessons include exercises: drafting FOIA requests, role-playing interviews, analyzing real datasets, working through publication decisions.

This is also a **high-stakes discipline**. Mistakes in investigative journalism can expose sources to retaliation, trigger lawsuits, destroy credibility, or cause harm to vulnerable subjects. The curriculum treats risk management and ethics not as constraints but as essential craft. Students learn that thoroughness and ethical rigor are what enable bold publication.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Investigative journalism is about breaking rules to get the truth"
**Why students believe this**: Popular culture (All the President's Men, Spotlight, etc.) dramatizes rule-breaking and emphasizes the rebellious journalist versus institutional power.

**Why it's wrong**: The best investigations are built on public records, cultivated sources, and rigorous verification — all within legal and ethical bounds. Breaking rules creates legal exposure and undermines credibility.

**How to correct**: Emphasize early (Lessons 2-3, 9) that most investigative breakthroughs come from knowing *where to look* in public information, not from illegal or unethical access. Use examples like ProPublica's investigations built entirely from public datasets and FOIA requests.

### 2. "Good sources are insiders who leak"
**Why students believe this**: High-profile stories often feature whistleblowers, creating the impression that investigations rely on dramatic leaks.

**Why it's wrong**: Most sources are ordinary people who know a piece of the story. Effective source networks include frontline workers, affected community members, retired officials, and public records officers — not just leakers.

**How to correct**: Lessons 5-7 teach source cultivation as relationship-building across diverse source types. Show investigations where the critical information came from persistent records work or community reporting, not dramatic leaks.

### 3. "Off the record means the source controls the story"
**Why students believe this**: Confusion about attribution rules leads students to accept sources' terms without negotiation.

**Why it's wrong**: Attribution terms are negotiated between journalist and source. "Off the record" should be rare and agreed upon in advance. Journalists can push back or decline terms that hamstring the story.

**How to correct**: Lesson 6 drills attribution standards and negotiation. Emphasize that journalists have agency in these conversations and should resist blanket "off the record" demands.

### 4. "FOIA is too slow to be useful"
**Why students believe this**: FOIA requests can take months or years, and many students assume this means they're impractical for daily journalism.

**Why it's wrong**: FOIA is a long game that pays off with exclusive documents. Smart journalists file requests on multiple stories simultaneously and treat them as part of an ongoing records pipeline, not one-off requests.

**How to correct**: Lessons 9-14 teach FOIA as a system, not an isolated request. Encourage students to file early, file often, and build relationships with records officers. Show examples of stories that only exist because a reporter filed a request months earlier.

### 5. "Hostile interviews should be confrontational from the start"
**Why students believe this**: Students conflate "holding power accountable" with "being aggressive." TV journalism often rewards dramatic confrontations.

**Why it's wrong**: Starting hostile gives subjects an excuse to shut down or claim bias. The most effective accountability interviews start with clear, reasonable questions and only escalate if the subject evades or lies.

**How to correct**: Lesson 16 distinguishes strategic accountability questioning from performative aggression. Model interviews that are firm but fair, and emphasize that allowing subjects to speak makes evasion more obvious.

### 6. "Data journalism requires advanced coding skills"
**Why students believe this**: High-profile data journalism often showcases technical sophistication (Python scripts, machine learning, custom visualizations), creating the impression that data work is only for programmers.

**Why it's wrong**: Most data journalism questions can be answered with spreadsheet analysis, sorting, filtering, and basic arithmetic. Advanced skills help but aren't prerequisites for data-driven investigations.

**How to correct**: Lessons 19-23 focus on spreadsheet literacy and practical analysis. Show investigations based on simple techniques (sorting to find outliers, pivot tables to find patterns). Save advanced technical skills for later specialization.

### 7. "Ethics are about avoiding offense"
**Why students believe this**: Students sometimes conflate "ethical journalism" with "not making anyone uncomfortable," leading to timid reporting.

**Why it's wrong**: Journalistic ethics are about truth, accuracy, fairness, and minimizing harm — not about avoiding all discomfort. Investigations often make powerful people very uncomfortable, and that's appropriate.

**How to correct**: Lessons 24-26 frame ethics as protective rigor, not politeness. Distinguish between legitimate discomfort (holding power accountable) and unnecessary harm (exposing irrelevant private details). Use case studies where ethical rigor enabled bold publication.

### 8. "Legal threats mean you did something wrong"
**Why students believe this**: Receiving legal threats is frightening, especially for early-career journalists, and can feel like evidence of error.

**Why it's wrong**: Many legal threats are strategic attempts to intimidate journalists into backing off true stories. Threats are common in investigative work and often indicate that the story hits close to home.

**How to correct**: Lessons 25-26 normalize legal threats and teach threat assessment. Emphasize that well-verified stories withstand legal scrutiny. Show examples of stories published despite threats that resulted in no legal consequences because the reporting was solid.

### 9. "Impact happens if the story is good enough"
**Why students believe this**: The mythology of journalism suggests that good reporting automatically leads to change — corruption exposed, policies reformed, wrongdoers held accountable.

**Why it's wrong**: Impact requires strategy: timing publication with policy windows, partnering with advocates or lawmakers, planning follow-up coverage, engaging affected communities. Stories don't change the world on their own.

**How to correct**: Lesson 28 explicitly teaches impact planning. Show examples where strategic timing and partnerships amplified impact, and examples where strong stories had little impact due to poor planning.

### 10. "Verification is what you do after reporting"
**Why students believe this**: Students often treat fact-checking as a final step before publication rather than an ongoing practice.

**Why it's wrong**: Verification should happen throughout reporting. Waiting until the end means discovering errors when it's too late to fix them and risking publication of misinformation.

**How to correct**: Lessons 7, 13, 22, 23, 27 embed verification as continuous practice. Teach students to verify as they report: confirming documents when obtained, cross-checking source claims in real-time, testing data as soon as acquired.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners (if students arrive under-prepared)
- **Add prerequisite modules** on news writing fundamentals, basic interviewing, and journalism ethics before starting this curriculum
- **Simplify FOIA** — focus on state-level records (often faster and simpler) before federal FOIA
- **Start with simpler datasets** — use clean, published datasets before teaching data acquisition and cleaning
- **Reduce legal complexity** — focus on basic defamation concepts, save nuances (actual malice, public figures) for intermediate work
- **Provide templates** — offer FOIA request templates, interview question frameworks, fact-checking checklists

### For Advanced Students (graduate-level or experienced reporters)
- **Add specialization modules** — financial records analysis (corporate forensics, offshore structures), advanced data techniques (SQL, OSINT tools), investigative project management
- **Emphasize cross-border work** — international FOIA equivalents, multilingual source development, working with overseas reporters
- **Increase ethical complexity** — publication decisions with life-or-death stakes, undercover methods debate, paying sources controversies
- **Add multimedia production** — video storytelling for investigations, interactive data presentation, documentary techniques
- **Focus on investigation as leadership** — mentoring junior reporters, managing multi-person projects, institutional investigative strategy

### For This Intermediate Level
- **Assume** students can write news stories, conduct standard interviews, understand basic journalism ethics, and use basic research tools
- **Build toward** independent execution of multi-month investigations using FOIA, data analysis, source cultivation, and ethical risk assessment
- **Depth of formalism** — teach practical legal concepts (defamation, shield laws) but don't require law-school-level understanding. Focus on "enough to be safe and effective."
- **Technical skill level** — spreadsheet literacy is required; coding is optional. Data work emphasizes analysis over engineering.
- **Ethical sophistication** — move beyond "don't plagiarize" basics to real-world dilemmas (publish despite source requests not to, balancing privacy against public interest, etc.)

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### The Panama Papers and Cross-Border Collaboration
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 9 (public records) or Lesson 26 (publication despite pressure)

**Why it's fascinating**: The Panama Papers investigation involved 400+ journalists across 80 countries working simultaneously on leaked documents. Raises questions about coordination, verification at scale, and how to publish globally sensitive material safely.

**Teaching angle**: Show how collaborative investigation can work, the role of intermediary organizations (ICIJ), and how verification standards apply to leaked material.

### The Role of FOIA in Watergate
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 11 (FOIA denials and appeals)

**Why it's fascinating**: Contrary to popular belief, Deep Throat was not the only source. Woodward and Bernstein used public records, campaign finance filings, and legal documents extensively. FOIA (passed in 1966) didn't directly enable Watergate, but records-based reporting did.

**Teaching angle**: Demystify the "secret source" mythology. Emphasize how records corroborate or contradict human sources.

### ProPublica's Machine Bias Investigation
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 20 (turning datasets into stories) or Lesson 22 (statistical literacy)

**Why it's fascinating**: ProPublica's investigation of criminal risk assessment algorithms showed how data journalism can reveal algorithmic bias. Raises questions about statistical methods, fairness definitions, and when to publish despite technical pushback.

**Teaching angle**: Show how basic statistical analysis (comparing accuracy rates across groups) can reveal systemic problems. Discuss how ProPublica handled criticism from statisticians about methodology.

### The Counted / Fatal Force Projects
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 12 (court records and public data) or Lesson 19 (data journalism fundamentals)

**Why it's fascinating**: The Guardian and Washington Post independently built databases of police shootings because the government didn't collect comprehensive data. Demonstrates crowdsourcing, data verification at scale, and how journalism can fill government data gaps.

**Teaching angle**: Show how to build datasets from scratch using FOIA, court records, news reports, and crowdsourcing. Discuss verification challenges and impact of making data public.

### Bellingcat and Open-Source Investigation
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 23 (digital forensics and verification)

**Why it's fascinating**: Bellingcat investigations (MH17, Skripal poisoning, etc.) use only publicly available information — satellite imagery, social media, leaked databases, flight tracking. Shows how OSINT techniques enable investigative work without traditional access.

**Teaching angle**: Introduce students to geolocation, reverse image search, metadata analysis, and social media investigation. Discuss how to verify OSINT findings before publication.

### The Snowden Revelations and Source Protection
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 7 (source protection) or Lesson 26 (publication despite pressure)

**Why it's fascinating**: The Snowden case raises every major issue in source protection: operational security, legal threats to journalists, government pressure, balancing source safety with public interest, and long-term consequences.

**Teaching angle**: Use as a case study for operational security failures and successes. Discuss how Greenwald, Poitras, and The Guardian balanced Snowden's safety with the need to publish. Don't glorify or condemn — analyze decisions dispassionately.

### The Tampa Bay Times' Poisoned Schools Investigation
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 3 (follow the money) or Lesson 13 (analyzing thousands of documents)

**Why it's fascinating**: This investigation combined property records, environmental testing data, corporate records, and hundreds of interviews to show schools were built on contaminated land. Won the Pulitzer and forced remediation.

**Teaching angle**: Show how combining multiple record types creates a complete picture. Discuss persistence (the investigation took over a year) and how to manage complex document sets.

### The Spotlight Team and Institutional Abuse Coverage
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 17 (trauma-informed interviewing) or Lesson 24 (reporting boundaries)

**Why it's fascinating**: The Boston Globe's Catholic Church abuse investigation required interviewing dozens of trauma survivors while navigating institutional power, legal threats, and ethical questions about survivor privacy.

**Teaching angle**: Discuss how to report on abuse ethically — centering survivor voices, avoiding re-traumatization, handling sealed court records, and publishing despite institutional pressure.

### Investigative Comedy and The Daily Show Effect
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 28 (narrative structure and impact)

**Why it's fascinating**: Shows like The Daily Show and Last Week Tonight combine investigative techniques (FOIA, data analysis, research) with entertainment. Raises questions about whether humor aids or undermines serious journalism, and whether impact requires traditional formats.

**Teaching angle**: Discuss how narrative format affects audience engagement and impact. No single "right" format — the goal is matching form to function.

### The College Rape Hoax and Verification Failures
**When to drop this in**: Lesson 27 (fact-checking protocol) or Lesson 18 (interview sufficiency)

**Why it's fascinating**: Rolling Stone's retracted UVA rape story shows what happens when verification fails. Every rule in Lesson 27 was broken: insufficient corroboration, failure to contact accused, reliance on single source, emotional investment overriding skepticism.

**Teaching angle**: Use as a cautionary tale for verification standards. Discuss how to balance believing survivors with journalistic rigor, and how empathy doesn't mean abandoning verification.

## Difficulty Progression

### Lessons 1-8: Building Foundations (Difficulty 2-3)
**Focus**: Investigative mindset, source development, basic methodology
**Why this difficulty**: Students are transitioning from straightforward reporting to investigative thinking. Concepts are accessible but require mindset shifts.
**Key challenge**: Letting go of the "breaking news" mentality and embracing slower, deeper work.

### Lessons 9-14: Document and Records Mastery (Difficulty 1-4)
**Focus**: FOIA, public records, document analysis
**Why this difficulty**: FOIA mechanics are concrete (difficulty 3), but appeals and document management get complex (difficulty 4). Review at Lesson 14 brings difficulty back to 1.
**Key challenge**: Patience with bureaucracy and persistence through denials.

### Lessons 15-21: Interview and Data Integration (Difficulty 2-4)
**Focus**: Advanced interviewing, data journalism fundamentals, integration
**Why this difficulty**: Trauma-informed interviewing (Lesson 17, difficulty 4) is emotionally and technically demanding. Data analysis requires new technical skills. Review at Lesson 21 consolidates.
**Key challenge**: Balancing empathy with rigor, and overcoming data anxiety.

### Lessons 22-28: Ethics, Verification, and Publication (Difficulty 2-4)
**Focus**: Statistical literacy, verification, legal/ethical frameworks, publication
**Why this difficulty**: Legal risk (Lesson 25, difficulty 4) and publication decisions (Lesson 26, difficulty 4) are high-stakes and complex. Final lessons (27-28, difficulty 2-3) bring skills together in practical checklists.
**Key challenge**: Making publication decisions under uncertainty and pressure.

### Review Placement Strategy
- **Lesson 8** — after source development, before moving to records (consolidates relationship-building skills)
- **Lesson 14** — after FOIA and document work, before interviews (consolidates records research)
- **Lesson 21** — after data and interviews, before ethics/legal (consolidates information gathering)
- No final review — Lessons 27-28 serve as capstone synthesis rather than review

### Difficulty Distribution
- **Level 1 (review)**: 3 lessons (8, 14, 21)
- **Level 2 (accessible)**: 9 lessons (foundation building and capstone)
- **Level 3 (standard)**: 11 lessons (core skill development)
- **Level 4 (challenging)**: 5 lessons (complex techniques and high-stakes decisions)
- **Level 5 (peak)**: 0 lessons (intermediate level doesn't require peak difficulty)

This distribution keeps most work in the "standard" range (difficulty 3) while challenging students periodically with high-stakes concepts (difficulty 4) and providing regular review breaks (difficulty 1-2). The curve builds gradually, peaks with trauma interviewing (17) and legal risk (25-26), and finishes with practical synthesis (27-28) rather than extreme difficulty.
