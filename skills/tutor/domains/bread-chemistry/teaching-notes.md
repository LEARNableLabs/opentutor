# Bread Chemistry — Teaching Notes

## Approach

This topic is mechanism-first and observation-anchored. Every molecular idea should end in something the student can see, feel or taste: stickiness, windowpane, poke-test spring-back, crumb structure, crust color. Use physical models before chemical names. Gliadin is ball bearings or glue, glutenin is long springs joined by disulfide 'bridges', and a bubble wall is a balloon skin that gets stiffer as it stretches. Bring in bond types and molecule names once the picture is in place. The dough's own timeline is the backbone, and troubleshooting a defect is the main way the student applies what they know.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **'Gluten is in flour.'** Flour contains the precursor proteins (gliadin, glutenin). Gluten forms only once water hydrates them, and work or time develops the network. Correct it with the dough-washing experiment, or by asking why a bag of dry flour isn't stretchy.
2. **'Yeast makes bread rise.'** Yeast makes gas, and the network holds it. Rice-flour dough with yeast still ferments but doesn't rise like wheat. Return to this in the gas-retention and gluten-free lessons.
3. **'More kneading is always better.'** Past a certain point, mechanical work and heat break the network down, and time (autolyse, folds, long ferments) can replace much of the kneading. Contrast the windowpane result with outcomes.
4. **'Maillard = caramelization.'** The Maillard reaction needs reducing sugars **and** amino groups and proceeds readily at lower temperatures than caramelization. Caramelization is sugars alone breaking down at higher heat. The baking-soda/pretzel example shows pH changing Maillard speed.
5. **'Stale bread has dried out.'** Staling is mainly amylopectin recrystallizing (retrogradation). Bread stales faster in the fridge than on the counter even though the fridge doesn't dry it faster, and reheating partly reverses staling. This makes a strong predict-then-reveal exercise.
6. **'Sourdough sourness comes from the yeast.'** Lactic acid bacteria make the lactic and acetic acid. Wild yeast mostly supply the gas.
7. **'Salt just adds flavor.'** Salt changes the network's strength and slows fermentation. Adding it late gives a dough that feels different, which the student can test.
8. **'The crust is done, so the loaf is done.'** A loaf can brown well and still be gummy inside if the interior never fully set, or if it was cut while still hot and steaming.

## Level Adjustments

Intermediate means high-school chemistry with no biochemistry. Name bond types (disulfide, hydrogen, ionic) and the main molecules (gliadin, glutenin, amylose, amylopectin, maltose, lactic acid), but skip reaction mechanisms, kinetics equations and protein subunit genetics (HMW/LMW glutenin subunits can appear once as a curiosity). Rheology stays conceptual, with no moduli or equations. Use temperature ranges, not exact thresholds, because gelatinization and setting temperatures shift with water and sugar content. Baker's percentages are the only required arithmetic. Treat the Shewry and Collins papers as optional deep dives for a keen student, not required reading. If Module 2 goes badly, add a lesson on protein structure basics (amino acids, folding, cysteine and its -SH group) before Lesson 7. Kitchen experiments are always optional, and every lesson must work without them.

## Rabbit Holes

- **Gluten as natural rubber**: the polymer physics of elastomers (crosslinked chains, entropy-driven elasticity) parallels the glutenin network. Drop it in Lesson 19.
- **14,000-year-old bread**: charred flatbread remains at the Natufian site of Shubayqa 1 in Jordan predate farming (Arranz-Otaegui et al., 2018, PNAS, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1801071115). Drop it in Lesson 1 or 5.
- **Starter ecology**: sourdough starters are studied as model microbial communities, a living example of competition and niche formation. Drop it in Lesson 16.
- **Maillard everywhere**: the same chemistry browns coffee, seared steak and toasted marshmallows. Drop it in Lesson 25.
- **Pretzels and lye**: the high pH from a lye bath is why pretzels turn deep mahogany. It also works as a kitchen experiment with a baking-soda solution. Drop it in Lesson 25.
- **Industrial bread**: the Chorleywood process (1961) swapped long fermentation for intense mechanical mixing plus oxidants, a real-world test of 'time vs. work'. Drop it in Lesson 31.
