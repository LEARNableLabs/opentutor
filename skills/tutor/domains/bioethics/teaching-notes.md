# Bioethics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Bioethics at the intermediate level is fundamentally about **wrestling with genuine dilemmas**—not solving them cleanly, but reasoning through them rigorously. Unlike math or physics where problems have solutions, bioethics cases often have competing legitimate values with no obviously correct answer. The pedagogical goal is to help students:

1. **Identify the stakeholders** and their interests (patient, family, doctor, insurer, society, future generations)
2. **Apply multiple ethical frameworks** systematically (consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, care ethics, justice frameworks)
3. **Recognize where frameworks conflict** and articulate the tradeoffs
4. **Construct defensible arguments** even when certain of uncertainty

This topic is **case-driven** and **discussion-based**. Present real-world controversies early and often. Avoid the trap of teaching frameworks in isolation for weeks before applying them—students learn frameworks best by using them to analyze messy cases. Balance classic thought experiments (trolley problem, violinist argument) with contemporary cases (He Jiankui, COVID ventilator allocation, dermatology AI bias).

Students at this level should have basic familiarity with consequentialism and deontology from intro philosophy. The work here is applying those frameworks to biomedical contexts where the stakes are life, death, identity, and human flourishing. Expect students to arrive with strong intuitions (often from political/religious backgrounds); the goal is not to change their views but to help them **reason more carefully** and **understand opposing perspectives charitably**.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Bioethics is just medical ethics"
**Why students get this wrong**: Popular media focuses on doctor-patient dilemmas; "bioethics" sounds medical.

**How to correct it**: Distinguish three nested circles from the start:
- Clinical ethics (doctor-patient, bedside decisions)
- Biomedical ethics (research, innovation, industry)
- Public health ethics (population-level policy, resource allocation)

Show how the same technology (e.g., CRISPR) raises questions across all three: clinical (should this patient get gene therapy?), biomedical (should we edit embryos?), public health (how do we prevent a genetic divide?). Use lesson 3 to make this distinction stick.

### 2. "There are right answers to ethical dilemmas"
**Why students get this wrong**: Science classes reward finding the answer; students want certainty.

**How to correct it**: Explicitly frame bioethics as navigating **reasonable disagreement**. Introduce the concept of "moral pluralism"—multiple frameworks that can't be reduced to a single master theory. When presenting cases (lessons 4, 13, 19, 29), show how intelligent people using different frameworks reach opposite conclusions. The goal is not consensus but **informed disagreement**. Grade on argument quality (premises, logic, evidence), not on which position they take.

### 3. "Autonomy always trumps other values"
**Why students get this wrong**: Western liberal emphasis on individual freedom; "my body, my choice" rhetoric.

**How to correct it**: Present cases where autonomy conflicts with other principles:
- Autonomy vs harm to others (refusing vaccines, dual-use research)
- Autonomy vs justice (buying organs, enhancement access)
- Autonomy vs future persons (germline editing)
- Autonomy undermined by structural forces (poverty, coercion, information asymmetry)

Use lessons 2, 12, 25, 26 to show consent's limits. Introduce communitarian and relational autonomy perspectives to balance individualist framing.

### 4. "Natural is good / technology is bad" (or the reverse)
**Why students get this wrong**: Naturalistic fallacy + tech optimism/pessimism biases.

**How to correct it**: Challenge both directions:
- "Natural" = smallpox, childhood mortality, short brutal lives (lesson 20)
- "Technological" = eyeglasses, vaccines, sanitation (also lesson 7)

The relevant question is not natural vs artificial but **effects on human flourishing**. Use disability rights perspectives (lesson 21) to show how both "fix everyone" (tech imperative) and "accept everything" (naturalism) can miss the mark. The goal is **reflective equilibrium** between technological capabilities and human values.

### 5. "Technology is neutral; only use matters"
**Why students get this wrong**: Common engineering framing; sounds balanced.

**How to correct it**: Show how **design encodes values**:
- Algorithmic fairness (lesson 15): training data choices determine outcomes
- AI triage (lesson 18): code embeds what counts as "fair"
- Genetic screening (lesson 11): what's testable shapes what's selected
- Consent interfaces (lesson 25): dark patterns manipulate choices

Technology is not a neutral tool picked up for good or ill—it channels action toward certain ends. Use Value-Sensitive Design literature. This is especially important for students from tech backgrounds.

### 6. "Informed consent solves everything"
**Why students get this wrong**: Consent is foundational in medical ethics; seems like the answer.

**How to correct it**: Show consent's limits throughout:
- Complexity undermines understanding (lesson 25: can you read a 50-page EULA?)
- Power asymmetries constrain choice (lesson 12: surrogacy; lesson 10: IVF access)
- Structural coercion (lesson 22: enhancement as arms race)
- Future persons can't consent (lesson 9: germline editing)
- Collective action problems (vaccines, dual-use research)

Consent is **necessary but not sufficient**. Introduce "meaningful consent" vs nominal consent. When is a yes not really a yes?

### 7. "The slippery slope is always a fallacy"
**Why students get this wrong**: Intro logic classes teach slippery slope as a fallacy; students overgeneralize.

**How to correct it**: Distinguish **fallacious** from **legitimate** slippery slope arguments:
- Fallacious: "If we allow X, Y will inevitably follow" with no mechanism or evidence
- Legitimate: "X changes incentives/norms/precedents such that Y becomes more likely" with evidence

Examples of real slopes:
- Embryo research timeline: permitted for 14 days → proposals to extend to 28 days (lesson 11)
- Assisted dying: initially terminal illness → proposed for mental suffering (lesson 30)
- Genetic testing: medical necessity → lifestyle traits (lesson 7)

The question is not "Is this a slope?" but "How steep is it, and how slippery?" Use lessons 7, 13, 30 to practice evaluating slope arguments carefully.

### 8. "Bias is a bug that can be fixed"
**Why students get this wrong**: Tech framing treats bias as a technical problem with technical solutions.

**How to correct it**: Distinguish:
- Statistical bias (systematic errors—fixable with better data)
- Value bias (encoded priorities—requires normative judgment)
- Structural bias (reflects existing inequalities—requires social change)

Examples from lessons 15, 17, 18: Dermatology AI trained on light skin (statistical—fixable). Triage algorithm prioritizing years-of-life over quality-of-life (value—need to decide what we want). Health disparities in training data reflecting historical discrimination (structural—tech can't fix alone).

**Fairness is not a technical specification**—it's a contested moral concept. Introduce impossibility results (can't simultaneously optimize all fairness criteria). The work is deciding which fairness we want, not "debugging" bias.

### 9. "Disability is obviously bad"
**Why students get this wrong**: Medical model dominance; personal fear of disability.

**How to correct it**: Introduce disability studies perspectives early (lesson 21):
- Medical model: disability = defect to fix
- Social model: disability = mismatch between body and environment
- Disability as identity and community, not just deficit

Challenge uncritical enhancement assumptions:
- Cochlear implants and Deaf culture (lesson 21)
- Autism self-advocacy against "cure" rhetoric
- Wheelchair users who oppose walking research

The question is not "should we fix disabilities?" but **who decides, in what contexts, with what alternatives**. This is crucial for genetic engineering, enhancement, and PGD discussions.

### 10. "Ethics is just opinions; we can't know anything"
**Why students get this wrong**: Moral relativism; reaction against dogmatism.

**How to correct it**: Distinguish **moral uncertainty** from **"anything goes"**:
- We can know some things: torture is wrong, consent matters, suffering is bad
- Disagreement exists in borderline cases and value conflicts, not across the board
- Reasoned argument is better than unreasoned intuition even when we can't prove it

Use reflective equilibrium model: test intuitions against principles, principles against cases, revise both. Bioethics is not about **proof** (like math) but **justification** (giving reasons that others can evaluate). Some arguments are better than others even when no argument is conclusive.

## Level Adjustments

### Compared to Introductory Level
At intro level, students learn **what the frameworks are**. At intermediate level, students **apply frameworks to messy cases** and recognize their limits. Expectations:

- **More ambiguity**: Intro courses often have clear right answers; intermediate bioethics has defensible positions without consensus.
- **Multiple frameworks per case**: Don't just analyze from one angle—show how consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, and care ethics each illuminate different aspects.
- **Stakeholder complexity**: Intro bioethics focuses on patient vs doctor. Intermediate adds: families, insurers, policymakers, future generations, industry, researchers, society.
- **Policy connection**: Intro stops at "what should we do?"; intermediate asks "how would we implement this? What are the second-order effects?"

### Compared to Advanced Level
Advanced bioethics students read primary philosophy (Rawls on justice, Parfit on identity, Thomson on abortion). Intermediate students read **applied** work and case studies. Differences:

- **Depth**: Intermediate surveys many issues; advanced goes deep on a few.
- **Technical detail**: Intermediate uses simplified versions of arguments (e.g., Thomson's violinist); advanced reads the full papers and scholarly responses.
- **Interdisciplinary**: Advanced might pair philosophy with law, policy, or empirical bioethics; intermediate stays more self-contained.

### Formalism
Intermediate bioethics uses **minimal formal apparatus**:
- Logic symbols for argument structure (if helpful, not required)
- Decision matrices for comparing options
- Stakeholder maps
- No formal modal logic, probability theory, or game theory (those are advanced tools)

Keep the focus on **reasoning clearly** rather than technical sophistication.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### 1. Personal Identity and Life Extension (lesson 20)
Drop this when discussing longevity: If you replace your cells, memories, values over 200 years, are you still you? Derek Parfit's reductionist view challenges intuitions about death's badness. Connect to Theseus's Ship paradox. This blows students' minds.

### 2. Future Persons and Non-Identity Problem (lessons 7, 13)
When discussing germline editing or reproductive choices: If we change who will exist, have we harmed anyone? Parfit's non-identity problem shows we can make the future worse without harming any specific person. Useful for climate ethics too.

### 3. Moral Status Gradients (lesson 27)
Don't just ask "when does life begin?" Ask: Does moral status come in degrees (embryo < fetus < infant < adult)? Or is it binary? Connects brain death debates to abortion, animal ethics, AI rights. Students who think binary status is obvious will struggle productively.

### 4. Effective Altruism and Triage (lesson 18)
When discussing resource allocation: Introduce EA frameworks (QALYs, DALYs, cost-effectiveness). Some students love the rigor; others recoil at "putting a price on life." Both reactions are useful. Connect to broader EA debates about global health priorities.

### 5. Disability Paradox (lesson 21)
Empirical finding: people with significant disabilities report quality of life as high as able-bodied people, contradicting medical predictions. Why? Hedonic adaptation, values shift, social model. Challenges utilitarian assumptions about maximizing ability.

### 6. Algorithmic Fairness Impossibility (lessons 15, 17)
Technical result: you cannot simultaneously optimize multiple fairness criteria (equal false positive rates across groups, equal predictive value, equal selection rates). **Fairness is not a math problem**—it's a values choice about which unfairness to accept. Mind-blowing for CS students.

### 7. Care Ethics vs Justice (throughout)
Justice ethics focuses on rights, principles, impartiality. Care ethics focuses on relationships, context, responsibilities. Feminist ethics critique: justice is gendered (masculine), ignoring caregiving. Use for case studies where both matter (lesson 12: surrogacy, lesson 28: family decisions).

### 8. Transhumanism vs Bioconservatism (lessons 19-22)
Frame enhancement debates as a spectrum:
- Bioconservatives (Kass, Sandel): some things shouldn't be engineered; preserve "the given"
- Moderate: enhance to treat, not for advantage
- Transhumanists (Bostrom, More): moral obligation to enhance; reduce suffering, expand capabilities

Students often haven't considered the pro-enhancement position seriously. Challenge both sides.

### 9. Relational Autonomy (lessons 2, 12, 25)
Standard autonomy = individual self-determination. Relational autonomy = we're embedded in relationships that shape choices. Feminist bioethics contribution. Helps analyze coercion, consent under constraint, surrogacy, caregiving decisions.

### 10. One-Thought-Too-Many Problem (lesson 4)
Bernard Williams: If you save your spouse from drowning because it maximizes utility, you've had "one thought too many." Some actions should flow from love/loyalty, not moral calculation. Challenges consequentialism. Use when discussing care ethics, virtue ethics.

## Difficulty Progression

### Modules 1-2 (Lessons 1-9): Building Foundations
**Difficulty**: 1-2 (accessible)

Start gently. Introduce frameworks with clear cases where intuitions align (Lesson 1: Hippocratic Oath basics; Lesson 2: paradigm consent cases). Build to first controversy (Lesson 6: He Jiankui) only after frameworks are in place. First review at Lesson 9.

### Modules 3-4 (Lessons 10-18): Applying Frameworks
**Difficulty**: 2-3 (standard), peaks at 4 (lessons 13, 18)

Students now **use** frameworks independently. Cases get messier. Lesson 13 (maternal-fetal conflict) and Lesson 18 (pandemic triage) are the first difficulty 4 peaks—these require juggling multiple values with no clean resolution. Second review at Lesson 16 before the hardest cases.

### Modules 5-6 (Lessons 19-26): Synthesis and Extension
**Difficulty**: 3 (standard), peak at 4 (lesson 20)

Students should now analyze new domains (enhancement, digital health) without as much scaffolding. Lesson 20 (life extension) touches personal identity—philosophically deep. Third review at Lesson 24 consolidates enhancement and data ethics.

### Modules 7-8 (Lessons 27-34): Advanced Applications
**Difficulty**: 2-4 (variable)

End-of-life starts accessible (lesson 27: death definition) but builds to hard cases (lesson 29: active vs passive euthanasia = difficulty 4). Emerging frontiers (lessons 32-34) are difficulty 3-4 because they require transferring all prior learning to novel contexts. Final review at Lesson 31 before the synthesis.

### Review Placement
Reviews every 5-7 lessons at difficulty 1-2:
- Lesson 9: Genetic engineering
- Lesson 16: Applications and limits
- Lesson 24: Enhancement and data
- Lesson 31: End-of-life and synthesis

Use reviews for **spaced repetition**, not just summarizing. Ask students to apply old concepts to new cases. Example: Lesson 24 review could ask "How does informed consent work differently in genetic testing vs wearable apps?"

## Engagement Strategies

1. **Start with a case, not a theory**: Lesson 1 opens with "Why Hippocratic Oath in the age of gene editing?" not "What is autonomy?"
2. **Use real controversies**: He Jiankui (lesson 6), COVID triage (lesson 18), dermatology AI (lesson 15)—these are in the news
3. **Vary delivery**: Mix mini-lessons, questions, resource-drops, teach-backs, real-world cases—monotony kills engagement
4. **Embrace disagreement**: Create space for students to argue opposing positions respectfully
5. **Connect to their lives**: Cognitive enhancers (lesson 19) for students; data privacy (lesson 23) for everyone; end-of-life (lesson 27) for anyone with aging family
6. **Use thought experiments**: Trolley problem (lesson 4), violinist argument (lesson 13), utility monster (lesson 18)—memorable and clarifying
7. **Challenge their intuitions**: Find the case where their framework gives a counterintuitive answer. Growth happens at discomfort.
