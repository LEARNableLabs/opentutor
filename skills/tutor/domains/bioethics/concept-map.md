# Bioethics — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Four principles framework** — autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice as organizing structure
2. **Informed consent** — voluntary, informed, competent decision-making. Depends on: autonomy
3. **Distributive justice** — fair allocation of resources and burdens. Depends on: justice principle
4. **Clinical vs public health ethics** — individual vs population focus. Depends on: beneficence, justice
5. **Consequentialism vs deontology** — outcome-focused vs duty-focused moral reasoning
6. **CRISPR mechanism** — gene editing technology basics
7. **Somatic vs germline editing** — non-heritable vs heritable genetic changes. Depends on: CRISPR mechanism
8. **Therapy vs enhancement** — treating disease vs augmenting normal function. Depends on: somatic vs germline editing
9. **Designer babies** — intentional genetic selection/modification of offspring. Depends on: therapy vs enhancement, germline editing
10. **Genetic privacy** — control over genetic information. Depends on: autonomy, informed consent
11. **Reproductive justice** — equitable access to reproductive technologies. Depends on: distributive justice
12. **Preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)** — embryo screening before implantation. Depends on: genetic privacy, reproductive justice
13. **Commodification** — treating bodies/capacities as market goods. Depends on: autonomy
14. **Maternal-fetal conflict** — competing interests between pregnant person and fetus. Depends on: autonomy, beneficence
15. **Personhood** — moral status and when it begins. Depends on: maternal-fetal conflict
16. **Algorithmic bias** — systematic errors in AI systems. Depends on: distributive justice
17. **AI diagnostics** — machine learning for disease detection. Depends on: algorithmic bias
18. **Liability and transparency** — accountability for AI decisions. Depends on: AI diagnostics
19. **Resource allocation algorithms** — AI-driven triage and rationing. Depends on: distributive justice, AI diagnostics
20. **Cognitive enhancement** — improving mental capacities beyond normal. Depends on: therapy vs enhancement
21. **Life extension** — radical longevity interventions. Depends on: therapy vs enhancement
22. **Disability identity** — disability as difference vs deficit. Depends on: therapy vs enhancement
23. **Medical vs social model of disability** — locating disability in bodies vs environments. Depends on: disability identity
24. **Data ownership** — who controls health information. Depends on: genetic privacy, autonomy
25. **Digital consent** — meaningful agreement in complex systems. Depends on: informed consent, data ownership
26. **Surveillance** — continuous monitoring and tracking. Depends on: data ownership, autonomy
27. **Brain death** — irreversible loss of brain function as death criterion
28. **Medical futility** — when treatment cannot achieve goals. Depends on: beneficence
29. **Active vs passive euthanasia** — killing vs letting die distinction. Depends on: non-maleficence, autonomy
30. **Physician-assisted death** — doctor-facilitated voluntary death. Depends on: active vs passive euthanasia, autonomy
31. **Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs)** — direct brain-technology communication. Depends on: enhancement, privacy
32. **Dual-use research** — science with beneficial and harmful applications. Depends on: consequentialism, beneficence
33. **Synthetic biology** — engineering biological systems. Depends on: CRISPR, dual-use research

## Dependencies

### Foundation Building
- **Informed consent** requires understanding **autonomy** because consent is autonomy's practical expression in medical contexts
- **Distributive justice** builds on general **justice principle** by specifying how to allocate scarce resources fairly
- **Clinical vs public health ethics** requires **beneficence and justice** because it highlights the tension between individual good and population welfare

### Genetic Technologies Chain
- **Somatic vs germline editing** requires understanding **CRISPR mechanism** because the ethical stakes differ based on technical capabilities
- **Therapy vs enhancement** builds on **somatic vs germline** because heritability amplifies enhancement concerns
- **Designer babies** requires **therapy vs enhancement + germline editing** because it combines intentional trait selection with heritable changes
- **Genetic privacy** depends on **autonomy and informed consent** because controlling genetic data is a form of self-determination

### Reproductive Ethics Dependencies
- **Reproductive justice** requires **distributive justice** because it applies justice frameworks to reproduction specifically
- **PGD ethics** requires **genetic privacy + reproductive justice** because embryo screening raises both information control and access equity issues
- **Maternal-fetal conflict** requires **autonomy + beneficence** because it pits one agent's autonomy against potential obligations to another
- **Personhood** emerges from **maternal-fetal conflict** debates because moral status determines whose interests count

### AI & Healthcare Chain
- **AI diagnostics** requires understanding **algorithmic bias** because biased training data produces biased clinical tools
- **Liability and transparency** builds on **AI diagnostics** because black-box systems complicate accountability
- **Resource allocation algorithms** requires **distributive justice + AI diagnostics** because automated triage embeds value judgments in code

### Enhancement Cluster
- **Cognitive enhancement** applies **therapy vs enhancement** distinction to mental capacities specifically
- **Life extension** applies **therapy vs enhancement** to mortality itself
- **Disability identity** emerges from **therapy vs enhancement** debates because "fixing" disabilities assumes deficit model
- **Medical vs social model** builds on **disability identity** by relocating the "problem" from bodies to social structures

### Digital Health Dependencies
- **Data ownership** extends **genetic privacy + autonomy** to broader health data ecosystems
- **Digital consent** builds on **informed consent + data ownership** because complexity undermines meaningful agreement
- **Surveillance** requires **data ownership + autonomy** because monitoring threatens both control and self-determination

### End-of-Life Chain
- **Medical futility** requires **beneficence** because it asks when treatment no longer serves patient welfare
- **Active vs passive euthanasia** requires **non-maleficence + autonomy** because it distinguishes killing from letting die
- **Physician-assisted death** builds on **active vs passive + autonomy** because it asks if autonomy can override non-maleficence

### Emerging Frontiers
- **BCIs** require **enhancement + privacy** because they augment cognition while threatening mental privacy
- **Dual-use research** requires **consequentialism + beneficence** because it weighs knowledge benefits against misuse risks
- **Synthetic biology** builds on **CRISPR + dual-use** because it enables both beneficial and catastrophic applications

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic philosophical reasoning** — needed for framework application, argument construction, identifying fallacies
- **Consequentialism and deontology** — needed throughout for analyzing cases from multiple perspectives
- **High school biology** — needed for genetic engineering, CRISPR, PGD, brain death concepts
- **Healthcare system basics** — helpful for understanding insurance, access, policy debates (not required)

## Conceptual Bottlenecks

Students commonly get stuck on:

1. **Therapy vs enhancement distinction** — seems clear at extremes but blurs in the middle (what about treating short stature? ADHD?). This distinction is foundational for genetic, cognitive, and life extension debates.

2. **Autonomy limits** — students grasp autonomy easily but struggle with when it should be constrained (public health, harm to others, structural coercion). This affects consent, enhancement, and end-of-life debates.

3. **Individual vs population ethics** — clinical ethics trains individual focus; public health requires population thinking. Students often try to scale individual frameworks to populations without adjustment.

4. **Bias vs fairness in algorithms** — students see bias as a fixable bug rather than a fundamental challenge of encoding values. This bottleneck affects AI healthcare understanding.

5. **Active vs passive distinction** — intuitively appealing but philosophically contested. Students often accept it uncritically, then struggle when challenged (is withholding treatment really passive?).

## Learning Pathways

### Fast Track (for students with strong philosophy background)
Can move quickly through lessons 1-4 (foundations), spend more time on applied dilemmas (lessons 11-13, 18, 29, 33)

### Visual/Case-Driven Path (for students who learn from examples)
Lead with real-world cases (lessons 6, 12, 15, 18, 28), then extract frameworks. Connect abstractions back to cases throughout.

### Tech-First Path (for CS/engineering students)
Start with AI & genetic technologies (modules 2, 4, 6), circle back to foundational frameworks as needed. More emphasis on dual-use, algorithmic fairness, privacy.

### Policy-Oriented Path (for students interested in governance)
Emphasize justice, resource allocation, regulation (lessons 3, 8, 10, 18, 23, 30, 33). Connect to actual policy debates and legislative frameworks.

## Common Misconceptions

These are addressed in teaching-notes.md but flagged here for instructor awareness:

1. "Bioethics is just medical ethics" — misses public health, research ethics, policy dimensions
2. "There are right answers" — students want clear rules; bioethics is often about reasoned disagreement
3. "Autonomy always wins" — overweights autonomy at expense of other principles
4. "Natural = good" — naturalistic fallacy appears in enhancement, genetic engineering debates
5. "Technology is neutral" — ignores how design encodes values (especially in AI)
6. "Informed consent solves everything" — ignores structural constraints, power asymmetries
7. "Slippery slope arguments are always fallacious" — sometimes slopes are real (need to distinguish)
