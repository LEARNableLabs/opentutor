## Domain Hooks
Drop these in when students are engaged and ready for a wild card:

1. **Insect succession as a window into climate history**  
   **When:** After lesson 16 on regional/seasonal variation  
   **Hook:** Forensic entomology databases are becoming climate archives. As species ranges shift northward and phenology changes (earlier emergence, extended seasons), historical case data documents these changes. Some cold-case reinvestigations must account for warmer climates in re-analysis.

2. **The microbial dimension — bacteria as co-clocks**  
   **When:** After lesson 3 on decomposition ecology  
   **Hook:** Microbes (bacteria, fungi) decompose tissues and produce the VOCs that attract insects. Recent work sequences microbial communities on remains to estimate PMI independently of insects. The "necrobiome" is predictable, trainable with machine learning, and works when insects are absent (e.g., buried/submerged bodies). Could microbial forensics replace entomology someday?

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"Forensic entomology is just about time of death"**  
   **Why:** Media dramatizations focus on single-number PMI estimates  
   **How to correct:** Emphasize the range of applications: victim identification (DNA from gut contents), toxicology (drug detection in larvae), body movement (succession disruption), season of death (species composition), neglect cases (living victims), contraband (stored product pests). PMI is one tool among many.

2. **"Insects arrive instantly when someone dies"**  
   **Why:** Misconception about insect detection abilities and access  
   **How to correct:** Explain the **pre-colonization interval** — insects must find the body, which depends on VOC dispersal, barriers (indoor vs. outdoor, buried, wrapped), weather (rain suppresses flight), and seasonality. This unknown delay is why we estimate *minimum* PMI, not exact time of death.
