# Tidal Pool Ecology — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Tidal cycles** — gravitational forcing creates predictable rise and fall of ocean water levels
2. **Emersion time** — duration organisms spend exposed to air varies with vertical position
3. **Exposure gradients** — vertical gradients in physical stress from high to low intertidal
4. **Temperature extremes** — organisms face thermal stress from solar heating and evaporative cooling
5. **Salinity fluctuations** — evaporation and rainfall create osmotic stress in isolated pools
6. **Vertical zonation** — distinct bands of organisms arranged by elevation
7. **Indicator species** — characteristic organisms that define zone boundaries
8. **Microhabitat heterogeneity** — small-scale variation in physical conditions creates refuges
9. **Desiccation resistance** — physiological and behavioral mechanisms to prevent water loss
10. **Thermal tolerance** — cellular and organismal mechanisms to cope with extreme temperatures
11. **Osmoregulation** — physiological control of internal salt and water balance. Depends on: salinity fluctuations
12. **Wave force resistance** — morphological and behavioral adaptations to withstand hydrodynamic stress
13. **Fundamental vs realized niche** — potential vs actual distribution of a species. Depends on: exposure gradients
14. **Interspecific competition** — species compete for limiting resources (space, food). Depends on: fundamental vs realized niche
15. **Competitive exclusion** — superior competitors exclude inferior ones from preferred habitats. Depends on: interspecific competition
16. **Predation pressure** — top-down control by predators shapes prey distributions
17. **Keystone species** — species whose impact on community is disproportionate to abundance. Depends on: predation pressure
18. **Trophic cascades** — predator effects cascade down through multiple trophic levels. Depends on: keystone species
19. **Facilitation** — positive interactions where one species benefits another. Depends on: microhabitat heterogeneity
20. **Ecosystem engineers** — organisms that modify habitat structure for others. Depends on: facilitation
21. **Food webs** — network of trophic interactions connecting producers, consumers, decomposers
22. **Top-down control** — predators/herbivores regulate prey/plant populations. Depends on: food webs
23. **Intermediate disturbance hypothesis** — moderate disturbance maintains diversity. Depends on: competitive exclusion
24. **Patch dynamics** — spatial mosaic of successional stages created by disturbance. Depends on: intermediate disturbance hypothesis
25. **Succession** — predictable sequence of community change following disturbance
26. **Recruitment variation** — temporal variability in larval settlement affects population dynamics
27. **Larval supply** — oceanographic processes deliver propagules to recruitment sites. Depends on: recruitment variation
28. **Climate change impacts** — warming, heatwaves, and ocean acidification alter species distributions and interactions
29. **Marine protected areas** — spatial management tool for conservation

## Key Dependencies

### Physical Environment → Biological Patterns
- **Exposure gradients** drive **vertical zonation** because organisms differ in tolerance to emersion time
- **Temperature extremes** and **salinity fluctuations** require **physiological adaptations** (thermal tolerance, osmoregulation)
- **Microhabitat heterogeneity** enables **facilitation** by creating refuges from physical stress

### Competition → Zonation
- **Interspecific competition** determines **realized niches**, explaining why species don't occupy their full potential range
- **Competitive exclusion** by mussels and barnacles drives sharp zone boundaries
- Competition effects are strongest where physical stress is lowest (low intertidal)

### Predation → Community Structure
- **Keystone species** prevent competitive exclusion by controlling dominant competitors
- **Trophic cascades** amplify predator effects throughout the food web
- Predation pressure decreases with increasing physical stress (high intertidal)

### Disturbance → Diversity
- **Intermediate disturbance** prevents competitive exclusion while not destroying the community
- **Patch dynamics** create spatial heterogeneity in successional stage
- Disturbance interacts with recruitment to determine community composition

### Multiple Controls
- **Zonation patterns** result from interaction of physical stress, competition, and predation
- Environmental stress model: predation dominates in benign environments, physical stress dominates in harsh environments, competition operates at intermediate stress

## Conceptual Bottlenecks

### 1. Niche vs. Habitat
Students often confuse physical location (habitat) with functional role (niche). Clarify: barnacles occupy the high intertidal *habitat* but their *niche* includes feeding mode, predators, competitors, etc.

### 2. Multiple Causation in Zonation
Zonation appears simple (vertical bands) but results from complex interaction of tolerance limits, competition, and predation. Students may oversimplify to single cause.

### 3. Facilitation vs. Competition
Students expect all interactions to be negative. Positive interactions (facilitation) are common but counterintuitive — algal canopy competes for light but facilitates understory animals.

### 4. Scale-Dependent Patterns
Processes operating at different scales (microhabitat refuges, zone-scale gradients, regional recruitment) interact. Students may focus on one scale and miss cross-scale dynamics.

### 5. Disturbance as Creative Force
Intuition says disturbance reduces diversity. Intermediate disturbance maintaining diversity is counterintuitive until mechanism (preventing competitive exclusion) is understood.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Zonation is fixed and absolute"
**Reality**: Zone boundaries shift with wave exposure, season, recruitment, climate. Zones are dynamic equilibria, not painted lines.

### 2. "Organisms live where they prefer"
**Reality**: Most organisms occupy *realized* niches constrained by competition/predation, not their full *fundamental* niche. Barnacles at zone tops don't prefer it — they're excluded from below.

### 3. "Only the fittest survive"
**Reality**: Environmental variation, disturbance, and dispersal limitation prevent competitively dominant species from excluding all others. Diversity persists through non-equilibrium processes.

### 4. "Temperature is the main stressor"
**Reality**: Multiple stressors interact — desiccation, salinity, oxygen, UV. Temperature is obvious but often not limiting.

### 5. "Removing predators helps prey populations"
**Reality**: Removing keystone predators can trigger trophic cascades that *reduce* overall diversity by allowing competitive dominants to exclude others (Paine's *Pisaster* removal).

### 6. "Climate change just shifts zones upward"
**Reality**: Species differ in thermal tolerance, dispersal ability, and interaction strengths. Range shifts are idiosyncratic, creating novel communities and disrupting evolved interactions.

### 7. "Protected areas freeze ecosystems in place"
**Reality**: Protection changes dynamics (allows recovery from harvesting, trampling) but doesn't stop natural disturbance, recruitment variation, or climate change.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic ecology** — needed for competition, predation, trophic levels, energy flow
- **Cell biology and physiology** — needed for understanding osmotic stress, thermal tolerance, cellular adaptations
- **Scientific method** — needed for interpreting field experiments (Connell, Paine)
- **Graph interpretation** — needed for tide charts, stress gradients, population dynamics

## Connections to Broader Ecological Theory

- **Environmental stress gradient model** — general theory of how physical stress, competition, and predation trade off across gradients
- **Intermediate disturbance hypothesis** — applies broadly (forests, coral reefs, grasslands)
- **Keystone species concept** — originated in rocky intertidal, now applied ecosystem-wide
- **Facilitation and positive interactions** — increasingly recognized across ecosystems
- **Metacommunity ecology** — rocky intertidal as model system for dispersal-limited communities
- **Climate change ecology** — intertidal as sentinel system for warming impacts

## Learning Sequence Rationale

1. **Start with physical drivers** (lessons 1-4) — establish the stage before introducing actors
2. **Add biological patterns** (lessons 5-8) — what zonation looks like before why it happens
3. **Build mechanistic understanding** (lessons 9-13) — physiological adaptations explain tolerance limits
4. **Layer in interactions** (lessons 14-19) — competition and predation refine distributions
5. **Introduce dynamics** (lessons 20-22) — disturbance, recruitment, succession add temporal dimension
6. **Apply to global change** (lessons 23-24) — climate change and conservation as synthesis

This sequence follows classic pedagogy: observation → pattern → mechanism → dynamics → application.
