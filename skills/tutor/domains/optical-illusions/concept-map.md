# Optical Illusions — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Lateral inhibition** — neurons inhibit their neighbors, enhancing edges and contrast
2. **Center-surround receptive fields** — retinal ganglion cells respond to local contrast, not absolute brightness
3. **Mach bands** — exaggerated edge perception from lateral inhibition
4. **Hermann grid** — spurious dark spots at intersections from receptive field interactions
5. **Orientation selectivity** — V1 neurons respond to edges at specific angles
6. **Illusory contours** — brain constructs edges where none exist physically
7. **Photoreceptor adaptation** — light-sensitive cells desensitize after prolonged exposure
8. **Opponent processing** — color coded as red-green, blue-yellow, black-white pairs
9. **Negative aftereffects** — complementary colors/motions appear after adaptation
10. **Gestalt grouping laws** — proximity, similarity, continuity govern perceptual organization
11. **Figure-ground segregation** — visual scene parsed into foreground objects and background
12. **Bistable perception** — ambiguous images flip between interpretations (Rubin's vase, Necker cube)
13. **Perceptual completion** — brain fills in missing contours and surfaces (amodal/modal)
14. **Kanizsa triangle** — illusory brightness and edges from strategic gaps
15. **Peripheral drift** — stationary patterns appear to move in periphery (Kitaoka illusions)
16. **Motion aftereffect** — stationary objects appear to move opposite to adapted direction
17. **Apparent motion** — discrete flashes perceived as continuous movement (phi phenomenon)
18. **Müller-Lyer illusion** — same-length lines appear different due to arrowhead direction
19. **Size constancy** — perceived size accounts for distance cues
20. **Misapplied depth cues** — 2D images trigger 3D interpretation mechanisms (Gregory's theory)
21. **Impossible figures** — locally coherent but globally inconsistent objects (Penrose, Escher)
22. **Forced perspective** — 3D space designed to create specific 2D projection (Ames room)
23. **Lightness constancy** — perceived surface reflectance stays stable despite illumination changes
24. **Intrinsic vs extrinsic properties** — separating surface color from lighting (checkerboard shadow)
25. **Color constancy** — perceived hue stays stable under different illuminants (The Dress)
26. **Empirical approach** — perception reflects statistics of natural environments (Purves)
27. **Predictive coding** — brain generates predictions, compares to sensory input, updates mismatches
28. **Bayesian inference** — perception combines priors (expectations) with likelihoods (sensory data)
29. **Top-down modulation** — higher-level knowledge shapes low-level processing
30. **Inattentional blindness** — unexpected stimuli go unnoticed when attention is focused elsewhere
31. **Change blindness** — large scene changes missed without directed attention
32. **Multisensory integration** — vision, hearing, touch combined into unified percept (McGurk effect)

## Dependencies

### Early Processing Dependencies
- **Mach bands** require **lateral inhibition** and **center-surround receptive fields** — the neural circuitry creates the edge enhancement
- **Illusory contours** require **orientation selectivity** — V1 edge detectors respond even without luminance boundaries
- **Negative aftereffects** require **photoreceptor adaptation** and **opponent processing** — prolonged stimulation shifts the balance point

### Mid-Level Dependencies
- **Perceptual completion** requires **Gestalt grouping laws** and **figure-ground segregation** — organization precedes filling-in
- **Kanizsa triangle** requires **illusory contours** and **perceptual completion** — combines edge construction with surface interpolation
- **Bistable perception** requires **figure-ground segregation** — the ambiguity is which region is figure vs ground

### Motion & Depth Dependencies
- **Apparent motion** builds on **motion aftereffect** mechanisms — both involve directional motion detectors
- **Müller-Lyer** and **forced perspective** both involve **misapplied depth cues** and **size constancy** — 2D triggers 3D heuristics
- **Impossible figures** require understanding **local vs global consistency** — each part is valid but the whole is contradictory

### Color Dependencies
- **Color constancy** and **lightness constancy** share mechanisms — both involve separating intrinsic from extrinsic properties
- **Empirical approach** provides a framework for understanding **lightness constancy**, **color constancy**, and even **Müller-Lyer** — all reflect natural statistics

### Top-Down Dependencies
- **Predictive coding** and **Bayesian inference** formalize how **top-down modulation** works — predictions flow down, errors flow up
- **Inattentional blindness** and **change blindness** demonstrate **attention limits** — related to what predictions the brain is currently testing
- **Multisensory integration** shows predictions span modalities — the McGurk effect reveals cross-modal priors

## Bottlenecks

### Critical Concepts That Gate Later Understanding
1. **Center-surround receptive fields** — fundamental to all contrast/edge illusions; must be solid before moving on
2. **Figure-ground segregation** — underpins ambiguous figures, completion, and organization
3. **Size constancy** — essential for understanding geometrical illusions (Müller-Lyer, Ponzo, Ames room)
4. **Predictive coding** — unifying framework for top-down effects; challenging but crucial for synthesis

## Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: "Illusions mean your eyes are broken"
**Why students think this:** Illusions feel like errors or malfunctions

**Reality:** Illusions reveal adaptive strategies. Lateral inhibition enhances edges (useful for object recognition), size constancy compensates for distance (usually helpful), predictive coding speeds processing (efficient). Illusions are side effects of normally useful mechanisms.

### Misconception 2: "All illusions happen in the retina"
**Why students think this:** Early examples (Hermann grid, Mach bands) are retinal

**Reality:** Illusions span the entire visual hierarchy. Lateral inhibition is retinal, illusory contours are V1/V2, motion aftereffects are MT/V5, impossible figures require high-level interpretation, predictive coding involves feedback across all levels. The progression from low-level to high-level is the pedagogical arc.

### Misconception 3: "Knowing the mechanism makes the illusion disappear"
**Why students think this:** Understanding should override perception

**Reality:** Most illusions persist even with full knowledge. This demonstrates that perception is not under voluntary control — it's an automatic, encapsulated process. The separation of perception from cognition is itself a key insight.

### Misconception 4: "3D depth cues directly cause geometrical illusions"
**Why students think this:** Gregory's misapplied depth cue theory is intuitive

**Reality:** The depth cue theory is contested. Purves' empirical approach argues illusions reflect scene statistics, not depth processing. Howe & Purves (2005) show Müller-Lyer correlates with typical retinal image statistics. Present both theories; don't oversimplify to one explanation.

### Misconception 5: "Predictive coding explains everything"
**Why students think this:** It's fashionable and unifying

**Reality:** Predictive coding is a powerful framework but not a complete theory. Low-level illusions (Mach bands) are well-explained by lateral inhibition without invoking prediction. Don't retrofit old explanations into new terminology just for consistency — use the right tool for each illusion.

### Misconception 6: "Color constancy always works"
**Why students think this:** The brain usually recovers surface color correctly

**Reality:** The Dress shows color constancy can fail spectacularly, and individuals differ. This reveals the inference problem — the brain must guess the illuminant based on limited information. When the scene is ambiguous (The Dress has cues consistent with both blue and yellow lighting), people make different inferences and see different colors.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic neuroscience** — needed for neurons, synapses, receptive fields, cortical areas (V1, MT)
- **Light and optics** — needed for wavelength, reflection, luminance, retinal projection
- **Probability/statistics (light touch)** — needed for Bayesian inference, natural scene statistics (empirical approach)

## Learning Path Notes

### Recommended Sequence
1. **Retina → V1** (lessons 1-5) — concrete, measurable, builds confidence with clear explanations
2. **Gestalt** (lessons 7-11) — shift to computational principles, less directly tied to single neurons
3. **Motion & Depth** (lessons 13-18) — domain-specific pathways, mix of low-level (motion adaptation) and high-level (impossible objects)
4. **Color** (lessons 20-23) — revisit constancy from a new angle, introduce empirical approach
5. **Top-down** (lessons 25-28) — abstract, theoretical, still actively debated

### Why This Order?
- **Physiological → Computational → Cognitive** — moves from well-established to cutting-edge
- **Single explanations → Competing theories** — build confidence before introducing controversy
- **Concrete → Abstract** — Mach bands are easy to see and explain; predictive coding requires philosophical shift

### Review Placement
- **After lesson 6** — consolidate retinal/V1 mechanisms
- **After lesson 12** — consolidate Gestalt before motion
- **After lesson 19** — consolidate motion/depth before color
- **After lesson 24** — consolidate color before top-down theory

These are strategic breaks before conceptual shifts.
