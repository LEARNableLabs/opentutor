# Behavioral Economics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Behavioral economics is best taught through experiments and real-world examples, not formalism. Every concept should be introduced with a decision scenario the student can try themselves before learning the theory. This creates the "aha" moment when they realize they fell for the exact bias being taught.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Biases mean people are stupid"** — biases are adaptive heuristics that usually work. They fail in specific, predictable contexts. Frame as "when shortcuts mislead" not "why humans are broken."
2. **"Knowing about biases fixes them"** — awareness reduces but doesn't eliminate biases. This is well-documented (bias blind spot).
3. **"Prospect theory replaces expected utility"** — prospect theory describes behavior, expected utility prescribes optimal choice. They answer different questions.
4. **"Nudges are manipulation"** — distinction between libertarian paternalism (preserving choice) and manipulation (removing it). Important ethical nuance.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner
Focus on relatable examples (shopping, social media, investing). Skip mathematical formulations of prospect theory. Use lots of "try this" experiments.

### Intermediate (this curriculum)
Include the key experiments (Kahneman & Tversky's original studies). Introduce prospect theory's value function qualitatively. Discuss policy applications.

### Advanced
Full mathematical treatment of prospect theory. Experimental design. Critiques and limitations of the field. Replication crisis implications.

## Rabbit Holes

- **Neuroeconomics** — brain imaging during economic decisions
- **Behavioral finance** — how biases affect markets (bubbles, crashes)
- **Choice architecture in UX** — dark patterns vs. ethical design
