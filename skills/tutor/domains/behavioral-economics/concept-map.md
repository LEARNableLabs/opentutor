# Behavioral Economics — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Homo economicus** — the benchmark agent: consistent preferences, rational beliefs, self-interest, utility maximization.
2. **Expected utility and risk aversion** — choosing among gambles by probability-weighted utility; a concave utility curve produces risk aversion. Depends on: 1
3. **Exponential discounting** — a constant discount factor per period, which makes plans time-consistent. Depends on: 1
4. **Bounded rationality and dual-process thinking** — limited attention and computation lead to satisficing and heuristics (System 1 / System 2). Depends on: 1
5. **Heuristics and biases** — anchoring, availability, representativeness (conjunction fallacy, base-rate neglect), overconfidence, confirmation bias. Depends on: 4
6. **Framing** — logically equivalent descriptions produce different choices. Depends on: 2, 5
7. **Allais and Ellsberg paradoxes** — systematic violations of the expected-utility axioms (certainty effect, ambiguity aversion). Depends on: 2
8. **Reference dependence and loss aversion** — outcomes are coded as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and losses loom larger (λ roughly 2). Depends on: 2, 6
9. **Endowment effect and status quo bias** — owning something shifts the reference point, so willingness to accept exceeds willingness to pay. Depends on: 8
10. **Probability weighting and the fourfold pattern** — small probabilities are overweighted and near-certainties underweighted, giving four risk attitudes. Depends on: 7, 8
11. **Mental accounting** — money is sorted into non-fungible accounts; includes sunk costs and narrow bracketing. Depends on: 8
12. **Hyperbolic discounting and present bias** — impatience is strongest for the immediate present, which causes preference reversals. Depends on: 3
13. **Beta-delta model** — a two-parameter model of present bias. Depends on: 3, 12
14. **Naivete, sophistication and commitment** — whether agents foresee their own future bias decides whether they demand commitment devices. Depends on: 13
15. **Social preferences** — fairness, altruism, trust and reciprocity, shown in the ultimatum, dictator, trust and public-goods games. Depends on: 1
16. **Inequity aversion (Fehr-Schmidt)** — utility falls with disadvantageous inequality (α) and advantageous inequality (β). Depends on: 15
17. **Motivation crowding out** — external incentives can displace intrinsic or social motivation. Depends on: 15
18. **Choice architecture: defaults and nudges** — how options are presented changes behavior without changing incentives. Depends on: 9, 12, 14
19. **Nudge design, sludge and ethics** — Save More Tomorrow, frictions that harm, libertarian paternalism and its critics. Depends on: 8, 13, 18
20. **Evidence quality** — lab versus field, effect sizes, publication bias, replication. Depends on: all earlier findings (applied throughout, formalized in lesson 29)
21. **Behavioral finance** — disposition effect, myopic loss aversion, limits to arbitrage, bubbles. Depends on: 5, 8, 11
22. **Behavioral industrial organization** — firms exploiting naive consumers through shrouded fees, teaser rates and subscription traps. Depends on: 10, 14, 18

## Dependencies

- Every anomaly (5–17) needs the benchmark (1–3), because a bias is only defined relative to what the rational model predicts.
- Framing (6) leads into reference dependence (8): the Asian disease problem works because the frame moves the reference point.
- Loss aversion (8) underlies the endowment effect (9), mental accounting (11), the disposition effect (21) and the design of Save More Tomorrow (19).
- Present bias (12–13) plus naivete (14) explains why defaults and commitment work (18–19) and why firms can profit from flat-fee contracts (22).
- Social preferences (15–16) are needed to see why crowding out (17) happens: a fine turns a social obligation into a price.
- Limits to arbitrage (21) explains why individual biases can move market prices instead of being competed away.
- Evidence quality (20) cuts across everything. Robust findings (defaults, present bias, loss aversion in many settings) should be told apart from fragile ones (priming, ego depletion, inflated nudge effects).

## The Three Pillars

- **Rational beliefs** → challenged in Module 2 (heuristics and biases)
- **Rational preferences over risk and time** → challenged in Modules 3 and 4
- **Self-interest** → challenged in Module 5
- **Applications** (Modules 6–7) use all three.

## Prerequisite Topics

- Basic probability and expected value — needed for concepts 2, 7, 8, 10
- Percentages and compounding/interest — needed for concepts 3, 12, 13
- High-school algebra — needed for concepts 8, 13, 16 (value function, beta-delta, Fehr-Schmidt)
- Supply, demand and utility maximization — needed for concepts 1, 21, 22
