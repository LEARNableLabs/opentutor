# Foraging — Research Summary

## Domain Overview

Wild edible foraging combines botany, ecology, ethnobotany, and practical field skills. At the intermediate level, students move beyond picture-matching identification to understanding plant families, ecological patterns, and the "why" behind safe foraging practices.

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Botanical Identification Beyond Pictures
- Plant family characteristics (Apiaceae, Rosaceae, Brassicaceae, etc.)
- Morphological features: leaf arrangement, flower structure, root systems
- Seasonal variation and phenology
- Taxonomic keys vs. field marks

### 2. Foraging Ecology
- Habitat associations (disturbed ground, forest edges, wetlands, meadows)
- Indicator species and plant communities
- Seasonal availability and harvest windows
- Climate and regional variation

### 3. Safety and Risk Management
- The "deadly dozen" and lookalike awareness
- Toxin types (alkaloids, glycosides, oxalates)
- Dose-dependent toxicity and preparation methods
- Environmental contamination (lead, pesticides, pollution)

### 4. Sustainable Practices
- Population impact assessment
- Ethical harvest guidelines (1-in-20 rule, root vs. leaf harvest)
- Invasive species as foraging targets
- Land access and legal considerations

### 5. Processing and Preparation
- Proper identification confirmation before consumption
- Detoxification methods (leaching, fermentation, cooking)
- Preservation techniques
- Nutritional content and limitations

## Key Sources

### Academic & Educational
- **Botany in a Day** (Thomas Elpel) — pattern-based family recognition
- **Edible Wild Plants: Wild Foods From Dirt To Plate** (John Kallas) — rigorous scientific approach
- **The Forager's Harvest** and **Nature's Garden** (Samuel Thayer) — detailed species monographs
- **Stalking the Wild Asparagus** (Euell Gibbons) — classic foundational text

## Resource Types Available

- **Field guides**: Regional and family-specific
- **Video content**: Seasonal ID walkthroughs, preparation techniques
- **Interactive tools**: iNaturalist, plant family flowcharts, phenology calendars
- **Community**: Local foraging groups, mycological societies
- **Safety databases**: Poison control references, lookalike comparisons

## Pedagogical Approach

Intermediate foraging education emphasizes:
1. **Pattern recognition over memorization** — teach plant families, not just species
2. **Negative identification** — knowing what something is NOT (ruling out toxic lookalikes)
3. **Ecological context** — where and when to look, not just what to look for
4. **Hands-on practice** — field time is essential; this curriculum supports but doesn't replace it
5. **Progressive exposure** — start with easy, distinctive species; build to challenging groups
6. **Risk calibration** — understanding uncertainty and the stakes of misidentification

## Knowledge Gaps and Extensions

- **Mushroom foraging** — separate domain due to complexity and risk
- **Medicinal use** — excluded to maintain food-safety focus
- **Regional specialization** — curriculum covers general principles; students should supplement with regional guides
- **Advanced processing** — acorn leaching, cattail starch extraction, etc. touched on but not exhaustive
