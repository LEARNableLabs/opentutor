## Domain Hooks
- **The Sheridan style lineage** — Drop this when discussing carving styles (Lesson 8). The Sheridan style has a fascinating origin story involving saddle-makers in Wyoming and specific tool innovations. Connect to western US history and ranching culture.

- **Leather tanning chemistry** — Mention when discussing why only veg-tan takes tooling (Lesson 1). The chemical difference between vegetable (tannin-based) and chrome (chromium-salt-based) tanning explains fiber behavior. Great for science-minded students.

- **Historical dye sources** — Drop during dye chemistry (Lesson 13). Before synthetic aniline dyes, leatherworkers used cochineal (insects), logwood (trees), and iron salts. Connect to medieval manuscript illumination and historical craft guilds.

- **Sacred geometry in tooling** — Mention during pattern design (Lesson 22). Traditional leather tooling often incorporates mathematical ratios (golden ratio, fibonacci) in border design. Connect to Islamic geometric art and Art Nouv

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"Wetter is better for casing"** — Students over-saturate leather thinking it will create deeper impressions. In reality, over-cased leather produces muddy, indistinct tooling and risks mold growth. Correct case is damp to the touch, not dripping. Fix: Teach the "wait and watch" method — case leather, wait 10 minutes, check if the surface has returned to original color (too dry) or still shows water beads (too wet). Proper case shows even darkening with no surface moisture.

2. **"Harder strikes make better impressions"** — Beginners often think power equals quality. Excessive force causes tool bounce, inconsistent depth, and fatigue. Controlled, moderate strikes with proper tool angle produce cleaner results. Fix: Practice on scrap with graduated strike intensity — show how light-medium-heavy strikes compare. Most students discover medium pressure gives best control.

3. **"Dye color on wet leather = final color"** — Dyes shift significantly during drying, and finishes alter them
