# Leathercraft Research — Tooling, Dyeing, and Pattern Design

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Tooling Techniques
- **Carving** — beveling, pear shading, backgrounding, undercutting
- **Stamping** — geometric stamps, figure carving stamps, border stamps
- **Embossing** — wet molding, 3D forming, relief work
- **Tool selection** — swivel knives, bevelers, shaders, backgrounders, veiners

### 2. Dyeing & Finishing
- **Dye types** — spirit-based (alcohol), water-based, oil-based
- **Application methods** — daubers, airbrush, immersion, resist techniques
- **Color theory** — layering, antiquing, gradient effects
- **Surface preparation** — case hardening, moisture control, deglazing
- **Top coats** — resolene, wax, oil finishes

### 3. Pattern Design
- **Drafting fundamentals** — pattern layout, seam allowances, grain direction
- **Adaptation** — scaling patterns, fitting adjustments
- **Traditional motifs** — Sheridan style, basketweave, floral patterns
- **Modern design** — geometric, abstract, custom illustration
- **Digital tools** — CAD software, vector design, pattern libraries

### 4. Advanced Construction
- **Edge work** — beveling, burnishing, edge paint
- **Hardware installation** — rivets, snaps, buckles, Chicago screws
- **Stitching techniques** — saddle stitch variations, decorative stitching
- **Lining and reinforcement** — interfacing, structural support

## Research Notes

Leathercraft at the intermediate level assumes competency with basic hand-sewing, leather selection, and simple project completion. The three focus areas (tooling, dyeing, pattern design) represent distinct skill branches that often develop in parallel.

**Tooling** is the most technically demanding — it requires understanding leather case preparation, tool angles, strike pressure, and sequencing. The learning curve is steep but offers immediate visual feedback.

**Dyeing** involves chemistry (pH, absorption rates, fixatives) and artistic judgment (color mixing, fade control). Water-based dyes are beginner-friendly; spirit dyes require ventilation and technique refinement.

**Pattern design** bridges craft and engineering — students must understand structural requirements (where to reinforce, how to distribute stress) alongside aesthetic principles.

The curriculum should integrate these three areas rather than treating them sequentially, as real projects demand all three skill sets simultaneously.
