# Leathercraft — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Case hardening** — moisture-controlled preparation that makes vegetable-tanned leather hold tooled impressions
2. **Fiber compression** — physical deformation of leather fibers under tooling pressure. Depends on: case hardening
3. **Swivel knife technique** — clean cutting of leather surface to create carved line work. Depends on: case hardening
4. **Beveling** — angled compression along carved edges to create depth illusion. Depends on: swivel knife technique, fiber compression
5. **Tool angles** — precise positioning of stamps and bevelers for consistent impressions. Depends on: beveling
6. **Backgrounding** — textured stamping of negative space to create figure-ground contrast. Depends on: beveling, tool angles
7. **Pear shader technique** — graduated shading using rounded tool for organic forms. Depends on: beveling, tool angles
8. **Carving styles** — design languages (Sheridan, floral, geometric) with distinct motif conventions. Depends on: beveling, backgrounding
9. **Stamp categories** — classification of tools by function (bevelers, shaders, backgrounders, veiners, geometrics)
10. **Workflow planning** — sequencing of tooling operations for efficiency and quality. Depends on: all tooling concepts
11. **Dye chemistry** — how alcohol, water, and oil carriers affect absorption and color behavior
12. **Resist techniques** — methods to control dye penetration in carved areas. Depends on: dye chemistry, beveling
13. **Gradient technique** — smooth color transitions using dauber or airbrush. Depends on: dye chemistry
14. **Antiquing** — applying dark dye to recessed areas and wiping highlights. Depends on: dye chemistry, backgrounding, beveling
15. **Seam allowances** — extra material needed for stitching and edge finishing
16. **Grain direction** — orientation of leather fibers affecting strength and stretch. Depends on: understanding leather structure
17. **Pattern scaling** — mathematical adjustment for different sizes or thicknesses. Depends on: seam allowances
18. **Design placement** — strategic positioning of tooling on functional pieces. Depends on: carving styles, pattern scaling
19. **Project integration** — synthesis of tooling, dyeing, and pattern design. Depends on: all prior concepts

## Dependencies

- **Beveling requires swivel knife technique** because the beveler follows the cut line created by the swivel knife — without a clean cut, the beveler has no edge to compress against
- **Backgrounding depends on beveling** because the beveled edges define the boundary between figure and ground — backgrounding without beveling creates flat, lifeless designs
- **Pear shader technique builds on beveling and tool angles** because shading creates graduated depth within already-beveled forms — it's a refinement layer that requires understanding of how light reads on angled surfaces
- **Workflow planning requires all tooling concepts** because efficient sequencing demands knowing when to cut (swivel), when to bevel, when to shade, and when to background — out-of-order work can damage previous impressions
- **Resist techniques depend on dye chemistry and beveling** because resist methods exploit how different dye carriers interact with compressed vs. uncompressed leather fibers
- **Antiquing depends on dye chemistry, backgrounding, and beveling** because antiquing requires recessed areas (created by backgrounding) and raised areas (created by beveling) to trap and release dye differentially
- **Design placement depends on carving styles and pattern scaling** because decorative tooling must respect structural stress points identified in pattern design while following aesthetic conventions of the chosen carving style
- **Project integration depends on all prior concepts** because a finished piece requires correct sequencing (workflow), moisture control (case), tooling execution (carving), color application (dye), and structural soundness (pattern)

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Leather types and characteristics** — needed for understanding which leathers accept tooling (vegetable-tanned only) and how grain affects dyeing
- **Basic hand-sewing techniques** — needed for pattern design (understanding seam allowances) and project assembly
- **Tool safety fundamentals** — needed for using sharp swivel knives, strike tools, and cutting implements
- **Simple project completion** — needed for understanding workflow, assembly sequence, and finishing standards

## Bottleneck Concepts

### Beveling (Lesson 3-4)
Beveling is the first major skill gate. Students who don't achieve consistent beveling will struggle with all downstream carving and stamping. It requires muscle memory for angle, pressure, and strike consistency — purely intellectual understanding is insufficient.

### Dye chemistry (Lesson 13)
Understanding how different dye carriers behave is essential for all subsequent dyeing work. Students who skip or skim this will encounter frustrating failures in gradients, antiquing, and resist work.

### Workflow planning (Lesson 11)
This metacognitive skill determines whether students can execute complex designs. Without it, they may know individual techniques but produce inconsistent results because they work out of sequence or damage previous steps.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Wetter is better for casing"** — Over-cased leather produces muddy impressions and can mold. Proper case is damp to the touch but not saturated.

2. **"Harder strikes make deeper impressions"** — Excessive force causes tool bounce and inconsistent depth. Controlled, moderate strikes with proper angles produce cleaner results.

3. **"Dye color on wet leather = final color"** — Most dyes darken significantly as they dry and are affected by final finish (wax darkens, resolene lightens). Test scraps are essential.

4. **"Seam allowances are optional on leather"** — Unlike fabric, leather doesn't fray, leading students to omit allowances. This causes weak, poorly finished seams and inconsistent edge finishing.

5. **"You can tool any leather"** — Only vegetable-tanned leather accepts tooling. Chrome-tanned leather (most garment/upholstery leather) will not hold impressions.

6. **"Background first, then bevel"** — This backwards sequence crushes beveled edges. Always cut, bevel, shade, then background.

7. **"Antiquing covers carving mistakes"** — Poor carving with inconsistent depth reads as amateur even with antiquing. Antiquing enhances good work but exposes weak work.
