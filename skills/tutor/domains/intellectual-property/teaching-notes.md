# Intellectual Property — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Intellectual property law sits at the intersection of legal doctrine, economic policy, and technological change. At the intermediate level, the teaching approach should emphasize **legal reasoning through cases and statutes** rather than pure memorization, **policy tradeoffs** rather than bright-line rules, and **strategic thinking** rather than abstract theory. IP law is fundamentally about balancing competing interests (creators vs. users, disclosure vs. secrecy, innovation vs. access), so every doctrine should be taught with its policy rationale and limitations. Use real-world examples liberally—students connect much better to "why can't I patent my app idea?" than to abstract discussions of subject matter eligibility. The field is highly statutory, so comfort reading and interpreting statutory text is essential.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Intellectual property protects ideas"
**Why students get this wrong:** Everyday language ("I have an idea for a patent") conflates ideas with protectable embodiments.

**How to correct it:** Emphasize the idea-expression dichotomy early and repeatedly. Use contrasts: "The idea of a boy wizard attending school is not copyrightable, but Harry Potter's specific expression is." Show how patent claims must specify concrete implementations, not abstract concepts. Connect to constitutional limits—if ideas were ownable, speech and science would be impossible.

### 2. "If it's publicly accessible online, it's public domain"
**Why students get this wrong:** Confusion between "public access" and "public domain." The internet makes copying trivial, leading to assumption of permission.

**How to correct it:** Distinguish public domain (rights expired or never existed) from public accessibility (anyone can view but rights still exist). Use examples: YouTube videos are publicly accessible but copyrighted; expired patents are actually public domain. Explain that ease of copying ≠ legality of copying.

### 3. "Trade secrets are forever"
**Why students get this wrong:** Unlike patents and copyrights, trade secrets have no statutory expiration date.

**How to correct it:** Emphasize that trade secrets last only "while they remain secret." Once disclosed (whether through reverse engineering, independent discovery, or breach), protection evaporates. Compare Coca-Cola (still secret after 130+ years) with KFC recipe (reverse engineered and published). The lack of time limit is offset by fragility.

### 4. "Fair use means I can use 10% (or 30 seconds, etc.)"
**Why students get this wrong:** Desire for bright-line rules in an inherently contextual doctrine. Internet myths perpetuate percentage-based rules.

**How to correct it:** Explain that fair use is a four-factor balancing test with no mechanical thresholds. Show cases where tiny uses were infringing (snippet of song in commercial use) and massive uses were fair (entire work in transformative search engine). Emphasize that transformative purpose matters more than amount used.

### 5. "Patents are always better protection than trade secrets"
**Why students get this wrong:** Patents seem more official, prestigious, and legally robust.

**How to correct it:** Walk through the strategic tradeoff. Patents require public disclosure (teaching competitors), cost money, expire in 20 years, and only protect against independent development. Trade secrets can last indefinitely, cost nothing to maintain, and protect against reverse engineering (if it's hard enough). Use Coca-Cola vs. pharmaceutical examples. Show how easily reverse-engineered tech (software) might favor patents while hard-to-reverse-engineer tech (manufacturing processes, formulas) might favor trade secrets.

### 6. "Obviousness means obvious to anyone"
**Why students get this wrong:** Natural language meaning of "obvious" suggests layperson perspective.

**How to correct it:** Introduce the PHOSITA (Person Having Ordinary Skill In The Art) construct and explain it's a legal fiction. Show how inventions that seem simple in hindsight were non-obvious at the time to skilled practitioners. Use examples like the Graham factors and teaching, suggestion, or motivation test. Emphasize that genius-level innovation isn't required, but trivial combinations of prior art aren't enough.

### 7. "Small changes to a work avoid copyright infringement"
**Why students get this wrong:** Belief that changing 30% or making it "different enough" creates a safe harbor.

**How to correct it:** Explain substantial similarity test looks at overall protected expression, not line-by-line comparison. Show cases where significant changes still infringed (melody transposed to different key, character details altered). Also show reverse: where large copying was transformative fair use. The question is not "how different is it?" but "does it appropriate the original's protected creative expression?"

### 8. "You need to register or use symbols to have IP rights"
**Why students get this wrong:** Patents and trademark registration requirements create assumption this applies to all IP.

**How to correct it:** Distinguish by type: trade secrets have no registration; copyright exists upon fixation without any formalities (though registration aids enforcement); patents and trademarks require registration. Explain that © and ™ symbols provide notice and benefits but aren't required for protection to exist. Show how this varies internationally.

## Level Adjustments

### From Beginner to Intermediate
At beginner level, this topic would focus on "what is IP and why it exists" with broad overviews of each type. Intermediate level (this curriculum) assumes students can handle:
- **Statutory interpretation** — Reading actual statute text and applying it to fact patterns
- **Case analysis** — Understanding how courts apply doctrines and create precedent
- **Multi-factor balancing tests** — No bright-line rules; judgment required
- **Policy arguments** — Critiquing doctrines based on utilitarian and rights-based theories

Emphasize that at intermediate level, the goal is not just knowing "copyright protects original works" but understanding "how original is original enough, and why did the Court draw the line there?"

### From Intermediate to Advanced
Advanced courses would add:
- **Litigation strategy and procedure** — Discovery, claim construction hearings, Markman hearings, appellate standards
- **International and comparative IP** — PCT, Madrid Protocol, Berne Convention, TRIPS, differences in EU/UK/Asian systems
- **Cutting-edge areas** — AI authorship and inventorship, 3D printing, blockchain, genomic patents, open source licensing
- **Transactional practice** — Drafting licenses, conducting due diligence, patent prosecution strategy, portfolio management
- **Empirical IP** — Economic studies of IP incentives, patent thickets, copyright's effect on creativity

## Rabbit Holes

### 1. The Software Patent Wars (1980s-present)
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 17 (software patents)

The evolution of software patentability is a microcosm of how IP doctrine struggles with technological change. In the 1970s, software was considered unpatentable math/algorithms. The 1980s saw gradual acceptance. The 1990s-2000s were the Wild West of software patents (one-click ordering, anyone?). Then Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank (2014) created the abstract idea test, invalidating many software patents. Students find it fascinating that the same computer program might be copyrightable but not patentable, or vice versa—and that the rules keep shifting as courts and USPTO grapple with the "what is software, really?" question.

### 2. The Mickey Mouse Curve (Copyright Term Extension)
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 3 (constitutional limits) or Lesson 19 (copyright scope)

The Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, derisively called the "Mickey Mouse Protection Act," extended copyright from life+50 to life+70 years, conveniently preventing Mickey Mouse from entering the public domain. This leads to great discussions: Is repeated term extension consistent with the "limited Times" constitutional requirement? Does the public domain bargain break down if nothing ever enters the public domain? Why do we even have copyright terms (incentive to create doesn't require 70-year-post-death protection)? This rabbit hole connects constitutional law, economic policy, and the power of the Disney lobby.

### 3. The Reasonable Measures Mystery
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 7 (reasonable measures for trade secrets)

There's no clear standard for what "reasonable measures" are required to protect trade secrets—courts evaluate case-by-case. Some require Fort Knox-level security; others accept minimal precautions. This creates a fascinating strategic dilemma: over-protect and you burden your business operations; under-protect and you lose rights. Students can explore real cases where companies lost trade secret protection because they didn't mark documents "confidential" or didn't have enough NDAs. The rabbit hole: how do you balance security with operational efficiency?

### 4. Fair Use as Free Speech Safety Valve
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 21 (fair use)

Fair use doctrine evolved in part to avoid First Amendment conflicts—without fair use, copyright could chill speech by preventing quotation, criticism, and commentary. This rabbit hole explores: What if there were no fair use? Could you quote a book in a review? Could news organizations show clips? The Sony Betamax case (time-shifting TV) and Google Books (mass digitization) show how fair use adapts copyright to technological and social change. Students love debating whether AI training on copyrighted data is transformative fair use—the cutting edge happening now.

### 5. The Patent Troll Phenomenon
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 15-16 (patent infringement and defense)

Non-practicing entities (NPEs) that buy patents and sue for infringement without making products are called "patent trolls" by critics and "efficient patent aggregators" by supporters. This rabbit hole explores the economics: Are trolls parasites gaming the system, or do they provide liquidity to the patent market and let inventors monetize? The America Invents Act created IPR (inter partes review) to fight low-quality patents, but trolls adapted. Students enjoy the strategic cat-and-mouse game and debating whether the patent system is fundamentally broken.

### 6. Trade Secret vs. FOIA/Transparency (Government Context)
**When to deploy:** During Lesson 8 (trade secret misappropriation)

When companies contract with government, their trade secrets may clash with Freedom of Information Act requests and transparency norms. Should taxpayers get to see the formulas/processes their money funded? Should companies lose trade secret protection by working with government? This rabbit hole explores public/private boundaries and whether trade secret law needs special rules for government contexts. Particularly relevant for students interested in administrative law or public interest.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

The curriculum follows a deliberate arc:

**Lessons 1-4 (Difficulty 1-2):** Gentle entry focusing on policy rationales and framework. Students build conceptual scaffolding before diving into doctrine.

**Lessons 5-9 (Difficulty 2-3):** Trade secrets are doctrinally simpler (three-part test, common law) but introduce case-by-case balancing (reasonable measures has no bright-line rule).

**Lessons 10-14 (Difficulty 2-4):** Patent law ramps up difficulty. Patentability requirements are complex, multi-factored, and heavily case-dependent. Non-obviousness (Lesson 12, difficulty 4) is the first peak—it requires understanding Graham factors, secondary considerations, and the PHOSITA construct.

**Lessons 15-18 (Difficulty 2-4):** Patent infringement hits another peak (Lesson 15, difficulty 4) with literal infringement and claim construction. Software patents (Lesson 17, difficulty 4) are the most conceptually challenging part of the entire curriculum due to the abstract idea test's ambiguity.

**Lessons 19-26 (Difficulty 1-4):** Copyright starts simpler (copyrightable subject matter, Lesson 19) then builds. Fair use (Lesson 21, difficulty 4) is the third peak—four factors, all context-dependent, no clear answers. This is intentionally placed after students have built stamina from patent law.

**Lessons 27-28 (Difficulty 3):** Strategic integration at moderate difficulty. Students synthesize by applying what they've learned to portfolio decisions and future challenges.

**Review lessons (4, 9, 14, 18, 23, 26):** Deliberately placed every 5-7 lessons to reinforce before moving forward. Difficulty 1-2 to give students breathing room.

## Assessment Strategies

### Formative Assessment (During Lessons)

1. **Hypothetical Fact Patterns** — Present a scenario and ask: "Would this be a trade secret? Why or why not?" Forces students to apply multi-factor tests.

2. **Claim Drafting Exercises** — Have students draft a simple patent claim for an everyday object (paperclip, coffee mug). Reveals whether they understand claim structure and scope.

3. **Fair Use Analysis** — Give a real case (or make one up) and have students argue both sides of the four factors. Develops comfort with balancing tests.

4. **Strategic Choice Questions** — "You invented a new chemical process. Patent or trade secret? Explain your reasoning." Assesses understanding of tradeoffs.

5. **Spot the Misconception** — Present common student errors and ask them to correct. E.g., "My friend says if I change 20% of a song, I can use it without permission. Is that right?"

### Summative Assessment (Curriculum Completion)

1. **IP Portfolio Capstone** — Present a startup with multiple innovations (software, branding, secret algorithm, creative content). Student must design comprehensive IP strategy, identifying what to patent, copyright, keep secret, and trademark, with justifications.

2. **Case Brief and Analysis** — Select a landmark case (Alice, Feist, Campbell) and have student brief it, then explain its impact on doctrine and policy implications.

3. **Prosecution Simulation** — Give a patent application and office action. Student must respond with claim amendments and arguments.

4. **Infringement Memo** — Provide patent claims and accused product specs. Student writes analysis of whether infringement exists and what defenses might apply.

5. **Fair Use Audit** — Student identifies a real-world use (YouTube clip, course pack, parody) and conducts full four-factor fair use analysis.

### Diagnostic Questions to Identify Struggles

- **If student struggles with novelty/obviousness:** May need to review prior art searching and technical reading skills
- **If student struggles with fair use:** May need more practice with balancing tests and comfort with ambiguity
- **If student struggles with claim construction:** May need to slow down and work through more claim diagrams
- **If student struggles with trade secret reasonable measures:** May need more examples of real-world business practices

### Engagement Indicators

- **High engagement:** Student asks "but what if..." hypotheticals, brings in current news (AI copyright, patent trolls), debates policy implications
- **Medium engagement:** Completes exercises but doesn't extend beyond prompts
- **Low engagement:** Focuses on memorizing rules without understanding rationales, struggles with application

Adjust difficulty and pacing based on engagement level. High-engagement students should get rabbit holes and cutting-edge materials. Low-engagement students may need more scaffolding and concrete examples.
