# Lockpicking — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Lockpicking is fundamentally a **tactile skill** learned through hands-on practice, not reading. Teaching must balance mechanical theory (understanding what's happening inside) with practice guidance (how to feel what's happening). Use visual aids heavily—cutaway locks, diagrams, and video demonstrations are essential. At intermediate level, emphasize the *why* behind techniques: why pins bind in sequence, why security pins create false sets, why tension control matters. Students should develop both picker skills (sport focus) and security mindset (professional focus), understanding locks from both attacker and defender perspectives.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"I need to buy expensive tools to start"** — Students often think professional-grade picks are necessary. Reality: basic hooks and tension wrenches suffice; skill matters far more than tool quality. Corrective: Emphasize that BosnianBill and others often use homemade tools. Practice with basic gear first.

2. **"Lockpicking is about force/speed"** — Beginners over-tension and rake frantically. Reality: Light tension and patient feedback recognition beat aggressive approaches. Corrective: Demonstrate how heavy tension prevents pins from setting. Use the "barely enough to bind one pin" rule.

3. **"All locks work the same way"** — Students learn pin tumblers and assume all locks are variations. Reality: Wafer, disc detainer, lever, and combination locks require completely different mental models and techniques. Corrective: Early exposure to lock diversity prevents overgeneralization.

4. **"Security pins make locks unpickable"** — Intermediate students hit spool pins and think they've found an impossible lock. Reality: Security pins increase difficulty but remain vulnerable with technique adjustment. Corrective: Teach recognition of counter-rotation and false sets; show that SPP works with patience.

5. **"Lockpicking is illegal"** — Fear prevents practice and community engagement. Reality: Legal in most places for owned locks; locksport community emphasizes ethics. Corrective: Share TOOOL resources, explain "two rules" (don't pick locks you don't own; don't own locks you're currently picking).

6. **"Expensive locks are secure"** — Price-equals-security fallacy. Reality: Master Lock #3 costs $8 and opens in seconds; installation quality and attack surface often matter more than lock quality. Corrective: Demonstrate cheap lock vulnerabilities and expensive lock bypasses. Teach threat modeling.

7. **"I need to feel every pin individually"** — Students expect discrete "click" feedback for each pin. Reality: Feedback is subtle; some pins give minimal indication; some locks have tight tolerances. Corrective: Practice with progressively harder locks; explain that expertise is recognizing subtle feedback, not needing obvious clicks.

8. **"Bumping/bypassing is 'cheating'"** — Sport mindset sees non-SPP techniques as inferior. Reality: Security assessment requires knowing all attack vectors; real attackers use the easiest method. Corrective: Frame techniques as tools in a toolbox; context determines the right approach.

9. **"Digital/smart locks are automatically more secure"** — Technology faith. Reality: Often combines weak physical mechanisms (cheap pin tumbler cores) with mediocre digital security. Corrective: Show examples like LockPickingLawyer videos demonstrating smart lock vulnerabilities.

10. **"If I can pick it, anyone can"** — Imposter syndrome after developing skill. Reality: Lockpicking requires practice; most people never develop the skill. Corrective: Contextualize skill development timeline; emphasize that security is about raising attacker cost, not achieving impossibility.

11. **"Rake first, SPP if that fails"** — Efficiency mindset. Reality: True for quick entry, but SPP practice builds foundational skills. Corrective: Encourage SPP practice even when raking would work; explain skill development strategy.

12. **"Locksport competitions are about speed only"** — Limited understanding of competitive formats. Reality: Competitions include speed, difficulty tiers, specific lock challenges, and team formats. Corrective: Share LockCon and Dutch Open competition structures; emphasize variety.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner vs. Intermediate

Beginners need heavy emphasis on basic pin tumbler theory, tool familiarity, and simple SPP on low-security locks (Master Lock, cheap padlocks). Intermediate students should already have basic SPP competence and are ready for:

- **Security pins** — Spools, serrated pins, false sets
- **Advanced mechanisms** — Disc detainers, high-security designs (Medeco, ASSA)
- **Alternative techniques** — Bumping, impressioning, decoding
- **Security mindset** — Vulnerability assessment, threat modeling, defense recommendations
- **Competitive awareness** — Locksport culture, competition formats, community standards

Intermediate curriculum reduces hand-holding on basic techniques and increases challenge lock complexity, attack vector diversity, and security analysis depth.

### Intermediate vs. Advanced

Advanced students would move beyond this curriculum to:

- **Automotive locks** — Sidebars, wafer systems, high-security car locks
- **Safes** — Manipulation, drilling, bypass techniques
- **Electronic hybrids** — Attacking electronic and physical components
- **Tool fabrication** — Custom pick/decoder creation for exotic locks
- **Competitive mastery** — Speed-picking optimization, tournament strategies
- **Security consulting** — Professional vulnerability assessment, client reporting

## Rabbit Holes

1. **Lock manufacturing and economics** — Why do cheap locks dominate the market? What's the cost-security tradeoff? How do manufacturers balance pick resistance with key durability and manufacturing cost? *Drop during lesson 6 (manufacturing flaws) or lesson 22 (security ratings).*

2. **Lockpicking in popular media** — How do movies get it hilariously wrong? Why does Hollywood show instant picking with one tool? What real techniques actually look like vs. cinematic versions. *Drop during lesson 8 or 9 when students are struggling with SPP difficulty—validate that it's supposed to be hard.*

3. **Historical lock evolution** — Ancient Egyptian pin tumblers, medieval warded locks, Bramah challenge lock (unpicked for 67 years), race between lock designers and pickers. *Drop during lesson 17 (high-security locks) to contextualize modern arms race.*

4. **Covert entry and forensics** — How can locksmiths/forensics determine if a lock was picked vs. forced? What traces do techniques leave? How do criminals avoid detection? *Drop during lesson 23 (vulnerabilities) for security-minded students.*

5. **Locksport culture and community** — TOOOL history, locksport code of conduct, how the community self-regulates, relationship with locksmith industry, DEF CON's lockpick village. *Drop during lesson 24-26 (locksport module) or when student expresses interest in competitions.*

6. **Physical security integration** — Locks are just one component: door material, frame quality, hinge placement, gap exploitation, security cameras. A $500 lock in a hollow door is theater. *Drop during lesson 20 (security assessment) to shift thinking beyond lock-centric view.*

7. **Vulnerability disclosure ethics** — Should you publish lock vulnerabilities? How do manufacturers respond? History of disclosure conflicts (Medeco lawsuit, etc.). Responsible disclosure process. *Drop during lesson 24 (ethics) or when student discovers a new vulnerability.*

8. **Lock patents and innovation** — How do patents reveal lock mechanisms? Using patent databases for research. Recent innovations in high-security design. *Drop for technically-minded students during lesson 17 (Medeco) or lesson 26 (future directions).*

## Difficulty Progression Notes

The curriculum has three distinct difficulty peaks:

**First peak (lessons 11-13)**: Bumping and tension control represent the shift from pure theory to tactile skill. Students need hands-on practice here; theory alone won't carry them through. Expect frustration. Provide encouragement and emphasize that SPP competence takes weeks of practice.

**Second peak (lessons 15-18)**: Security pins and advanced mechanisms. This is where intermediate students earn the "intermediate" label. Spool pins create false sets that violate beginners' mental models. Disc detainers and Medeco locks show there's no universal technique. Students must adapt and think like both engineer and attacker.

**Third peak (lesson 23)**: Security vulnerabilities synthesis. Students must integrate all prior knowledge to assess real-world security. This is a conceptual rather than technical challenge—shifting from "can I pick this?" to "what's the realistic threat model?"

**Review placements** at lessons 7, 14, and 21 provide consolidation before each new challenge tier. These aren't passive reviews—students should teach back concepts, compare techniques, and build mental maps of the knowledge domain.

## Assessment Strategies

**Hands-on demonstration** is the gold standard. Can the student SPP a standard pin tumbler? Recognize a false set from a spool pin? Identify when to bypass rather than pick?

**Teach-backs** (lessons 9, 13) reveal mental model quality. Can students explain *why* something works, not just *how* to do it?

**Security assessments** (lesson 20) test synthesis. Given a lock installation, can students identify vulnerabilities across all attack vectors (picking, bumping, bypassing, social engineering)?

**Video analysis** — Show LockPickingLawyer or BosnianBill videos and ask students to identify the technique, predict the outcome, and explain the vulnerability.

**Lock identification** — Present photos of lock keyways/mechanisms and ask students to identify type, likely difficulty, and appropriate technique.

**Ethical scenarios** — Present situations (found keys, locked-out neighbor, security research) and assess understanding of legal/ethical boundaries.

**Avoid pure knowledge recall** — Don't quiz on "what year was the MIT guide published?" Focus on applied understanding: "Given this lock and this scenario, what's your approach?"
