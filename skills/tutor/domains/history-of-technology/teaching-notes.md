# History and Philosophy of Technology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

This topic combines historical narrative with philosophical analysis, requiring constant movement between concrete cases and abstract frameworks. At the intermediate level, emphasize **application over memorization**: students should be able to use philosophical frameworks to analyze contemporary technology, not just recite definitions. The sweet spot is using historical examples to ground philosophy, then applying those frameworks to technologies students encounter daily. This is primarily a **conceptual and argumentative** field rather than technical or mathematical, so focus on critical thinking, comparing perspectives, and constructing well-reasoned positions.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Technology is neutral, only its uses are good or bad"**  
   Students often start with this instrumental view. Counter it early by introducing Winner's "politics of artifacts" and showing how design choices embed values. Use concrete examples: highway overpasses, building accessibility, social media algorithms.

2. **"Technological progress is linear and inevitable"**  
   Challenge this with the "Greeks and steam power" question and path dependence examples (QWERTY keyboard). Emphasize historical contingency — things could have gone differently.

3. **"Determinism and construction are opposing theories you must choose between"**  
   Many students want a clear "right answer." Resist this. Show how both offer insights. Technology constrains possibilities (determinism) while society shapes which possibilities are realized (construction). The dialectical view is most sophisticated.

4. **"Heidegger is saying 'technology is bad, return to nature'"**  
   This is a persistent misreading. Heidegger critiques the essence of modern technology (enframing) as a particular way of revealing, not technology per se. He distinguishes modern technology from ancient techne. Emphasize the phenomenological dimension, not moralistic rejection.

5. **"Ellul and Heidegger are saying the same thing"**  
   Both are critical, but Ellul focuses on autonomy and totality of the "technological system" while Heidegger focuses on ontology and modes of revealing. Different diagnoses, different concerns.

6. **"Philosophy of technology is just applied ethics"**  
   Ethics is important, but this field includes ontology (what is technology?), epistemology (how does it shape knowledge?), political philosophy (who has power?), and phenomenology (how do we experience it?). Don't reduce everything to "is it good or bad?"

7. **"Social construction means technology is arbitrary or 'just a social construct'"**  
   SCOT shows how social factors influence development, but technological artifacts still have material constraints and affordances. Social construction doesn't mean "anything goes" — it means we should examine how social choices shape technological trajectories.

8. **"Old philosophers like Heidegger and Ellul aren't relevant to modern tech like AI"**  
   Show continuities. Enframing applies to data-driven optimization. Technique applies to algorithmic governance. Classic frameworks still illuminate contemporary issues, though they may need updating.

9. **"The Industrial Revolution was about steam engines"**  
   It was about reorganizing labor, capital, time, and social relations. The steam engine was one element in a broader transformation. Students often focus too much on specific inventions and miss systemic changes.

10. **"Posthumanism/transhumanism is about becoming cyborgs"**  
    It's about questioning human/technology boundaries and anthropocentrism. Haraway's cyborg is a metaphor and political project, not just a literal prediction. Focus on the conceptual work, not sci-fi imagery.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students (Current Level)

**Emphasize:**
- Comparing and contrasting philosophical frameworks (determinism vs. construction, Heidegger vs. Ellul vs. Feenberg)
- Applying concepts to familiar technologies (smartphones, social media, AI assistants)
- Reading excerpts from primary sources (not just secondary summaries)
- Constructing arguments and taking positions (with justification)
- Historical context for philosophical ideas (why did these thinkers write when they did?)

**De-emphasize:**
- Exhaustive historical timelines (focus on key transitions, not every invention)
- Technical details of how technologies work (unless relevant to philosophical point)
- Scholarly debates within philosophy of technology (save for advanced level)
- Overly dense prose from primary sources (curate excerpts carefully)

**Appropriate depth:**
- Read Heidegger's "The Question Concerning Technology" in excerpt form with guidance
- Understand SCOT framework and apply it to one case study
- Compare 2-3 philosophical positions on a single question
- Trace one technology through multiple historical periods
- Engage with contemporary debates using classical frameworks

### Compared to Beginner Level

Beginners would focus more on storytelling and less on philosophical abstraction. They'd encounter ideas like determinism and construction in simpler form ("Do technologies change society or does society change technologies?") without the theoretical depth. Primary sources would be minimal or avoided entirely.

### Compared to Advanced Level

Advanced students would engage with scholarly debates (critical theory vs. empirical turn, ANT vs. SCOT), read full primary texts, conduct original historical research or philosophical analysis, and grapple with more difficult thinkers (Simondon, Stiegler, Hui). They'd be expected to synthesize across multiple frameworks and develop their own positions.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

- **Lewis Mumford's megamachine** — Drop in when discussing Ellul or industrial organization. Mumford argues that large-scale social organization (Egyptian pyramids, modern bureaucracy) is itself a technology. Blows students' minds about what counts as "technology."

- **Technological momentum** — Thomas Hughes' concept that technologies gain momentum over time, becoming harder to redirect. Bridges determinism and construction. Great for path dependence discussions.

- **Frankenstein and the Romantic critique** — When covering 19th-century industrialization, mention that Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) is an early philosophical critique of unconstrained technological creation. Literature as philosophy of technology.

- **Appropriate technology movement** — E.F. Schumacher's "Small is Beautiful" and the 1970s movement for human-scale, democratic technology. Historical alternative to both technophobia and techno-optimism. Relevant for lesson 26 on future directions.

- **Chinese science and technology** — Joseph Needham's question: why did China, despite centuries of technological leadership, not develop modern science and industry? Rich discussion of cultural, institutional, and historical factors. Complicates Euro-centric narratives.

- **Technological scripts and affordances** — Madeleine Akrich and Don Norman. How technologies "script" user behavior and embed assumptions. Great for analyzing everyday objects (doors, interfaces, transit systems).

- **Care ethics and technology** — Feminist philosophy of technology (beyond Haraway). How do technologies distribute care work? Who does maintenance? Invisible labor in technological systems.

- **Technological singularity** — Vernor Vinge and Ray Kurzweil's predictions of AI superintelligence. Some students will ask about this when covering AI. Good opportunity to apply Ellul (autonomous technology) and discuss limits of prediction.

- **The Jevons Paradox** — Efficiency improvements can increase consumption rather than decrease it. Relevant for environmental technology discussions. Named for 1865 observation about coal.

- **Non-Western philosophies of technology** — Most canonical work is European/American. Yuk Hui's work on Chinese cosmotechnics opens alternative frameworks. Good for questioning universality of "technology" concept.

## Pacing and Difficulty Progression

**Week 1-2 (Lessons 1-6):** Gentle introduction. Build conceptual foundation and historical background. Difficulty stays at 1-2.

**Week 2-3 (Lessons 7-11):** Introduce complexity with Industrial Revolution debates and competing explanations. First review day at lesson 10. Difficulty reaches 3.

**Week 3-4 (Lessons 12-16):** Hardest section. Core philosophical frameworks, primary source engagement. Heidegger and Ellul are dense. Students may struggle. Consider slowing down if needed. Difficulty peaks at 4.

**Week 4-5 (Lessons 17-20):** Apply philosophy to society. Second review day at lesson 20 helps consolidate the philosophical frameworks before moving to contemporary debates. Difficulty moderates to 2-3.

**Week 5-6 (Lessons 21-27):** Synthesis and application. Contemporary debates let students use frameworks they've learned. Final review at lesson 27. Mix of 3-4 (analysis) and 1-2 (reflection).

## Engagement Strategies

- **Current events tie-ins:** Every lesson can connect to recent news (AI regulation, climate tech, data privacy, automation). Students engage more when they see relevance.

- **"What would X say about Y?" exercises:** "What would Heidegger say about TikTok?" "What would Ellul say about ChatGPT?" Encourages applying frameworks creatively.

- **Case study debates:** Take a controversial technology (facial recognition, gene editing, social media algorithms) and have students argue from different philosophical positions.

- **Personal technology audit:** Ask students to examine their own technology use through philosophical lenses. Makes abstract concepts concrete.

- **Historical counterfactuals:** "What if the printing press had never been invented?" "What if the steam engine had emerged in China first?" Develops contingency thinking.

## Resources for Further Depth

If a student wants to go deeper on specific areas:

- **Critical theory of technology:** Andrew Feenberg's "Transforming Technology" or "Questioning Technology"
- **Postphenomenology:** Peter-Paul Verbeek's "Moralizing Technology"
- **Feminist philosophy of technology:** Judy Wajcman's "TechnoFeminism"
- **Actor-Network Theory:** Bruno Latour's "Reassembling the Social"
- **History of technology:** David Edgerton's "The Shock of the Old" (challenges innovation narratives)
- **Contemporary AI ethics:** Kate Crawford's "Atlas of AI"
