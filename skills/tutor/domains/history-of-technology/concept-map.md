# History and Philosophy of Technology — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Technology definition** — what counts as technology, artifacts vs processes vs systems
2. **Philosophy vs history** — normative vs descriptive approaches to studying technology
3. **Human intentionality** — what distinguishes human technology from animal tool use
4. **Neolithic revolution** — agriculture as foundational technological transformation
5. **Social context of invention** — how society enables or constrains technological development
6. **Printing press** — knowledge dissemination and standardization
7. **Great Divergence** — why the Industrial Revolution emerged in specific contexts
8. **Mechanization** — factory system and reorganization of work
9. **Electrification** — second industrial revolution and power distribution
10. **Information technology** — computing as general-purpose technology
11. **Technological determinism** — technology as autonomous driver of social change. Depends on: 4, 7, 8
12. **Social construction** — society shapes technology's form and meaning. Depends on: 5
13. **Heidegger's enframing** — technology as way of revealing reality. Depends on: 1, 2
14. **Ellul's technique** — autonomous technological system. Depends on: 11
15. **Technological mediation** — how technologies shape perception and action. Depends on: 13
16. **Postphenomenology** — human-technology relations (Ihde). Depends on: 15
17. **Politics of artifacts** — values embedded in technological design (Winner). Depends on: 12
18. **SCOT framework** — interpretive flexibility and closure. Depends on: 12
19. **Path dependence** — historical contingency in technological development. Depends on: 5, 18
20. **Posthumanism** — cyborg identity and human-technology boundaries. Depends on: 15, 16
21. **AI and automation** — contemporary debates on artificial intelligence. Depends on: 10, 11, 14
22. **Anthropocene** — technology's planetary-scale impact. Depends on: 4, 8, 9
23. **Surveillance capitalism** — data extraction and platform power. Depends on: 10, 17
24. **Democratic technology** — participatory design and appropriate technology. Depends on: 17, 18

## Critical Dependencies

### From History to Philosophy
- **Social context of invention** (5) enables understanding **social construction** (12) — you can't understand SCOT without seeing how ancient Greeks failed to industrialize
- **Mechanization** (8) and **electrification** (9) provide concrete cases for debating **technological determinism** (11) vs **social construction** (12)
- **Information technology** (10) is necessary context for **AI debates** (21) and **surveillance capitalism** (23)

### Within Philosophy
- **Heidegger's enframing** (13) must come before **technological mediation** (15) — mediation theory responds to and extends Heidegger
- **Technological determinism** (11) and **social construction** (12) must both be understood before **SCOT** (18) makes sense — SCOT is a specific constructivist framework
- **Politics of artifacts** (17) requires both **social construction** (12) and concrete historical cases (5, 7)

### Philosophy to Contemporary
- **Ellul's technique** (14) provides framework for analyzing **AI and automation** (21)
- **Posthumanism** (20) synthesizes **technological mediation** (15) and **postphenomenology** (16)
- **Democratic technology** (24) responds to critiques in **Heidegger** (13), **Ellul** (14), and **Winner** (17)

## Bottleneck Concepts

These concepts are crucial — if a student doesn't grasp them, later material becomes much harder:

1. **Technological determinism vs social construction** (11-12) — the central philosophical debate, needed for nearly all subsequent lessons
2. **Social context of invention** (5) — historical foundation for understanding construction
3. **Technological mediation** (15) — unlocks contemporary phenomenological approaches
4. **Politics of artifacts** (17) — bridges theory and practice for contemporary applications

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Determinism = bad, construction = good"** — Both frameworks have insights; it's not a binary choice. Real understanding comes from seeing their dialectical relationship.

2. **"Philosophy of technology is just ethics"** — Ethics is one dimension, but ontology (what technology is) and epistemology (how technology shapes knowledge) are equally important.

3. **"Ancient people were less technologically sophisticated"** — Different, not less. Ancient technologies were often highly sophisticated for their contexts.

4. **"The Industrial Revolution was inevitable progress"** — Historical contingency matters. China could have industrialized first; Britain's path wasn't predetermined.

5. **"Heidegger thinks all technology is bad"** — Heidegger critiques the essence of modern technology (enframing) but doesn't reject technology entirely. He distinguishes modern technology from ancient techne.

6. **"Social construction means technology is arbitrary"** — SCOT shows how social factors shape development, but this doesn't make outcomes random or unconstrained.

7. **"Postphenomenology rejects Heidegger"** — It extends and specifies Heidegger's insights, focusing on particular human-technology relations rather than rejecting his work.

8. **"AI is fundamentally different from all previous technologies"** — Yes and no. AI has unique properties (learning, autonomy) but also shares characteristics with earlier general-purpose technologies (steam, electricity, computing).

## Prerequisite Topics

- **World history survey** — needed for lessons 4-11 (historical transitions)
- **Basic logic and argumentation** — needed for lessons 12-16 (philosophical frameworks)
- **Contemporary social awareness** — needed for lessons 21-26 (current debates)

## Vertical Integration

The curriculum alternates between historical and philosophical modes:

- **Lessons 1-3:** Conceptual foundations
- **Lessons 4-11:** Historical survey (with philosophical questions embedded)
- **Lessons 12-16:** Core philosophical frameworks
- **Lessons 17-20:** Philosophy applied to society
- **Lessons 21-27:** Contemporary synthesis

This structure allows students to ground abstract philosophy in concrete historical cases, then apply frameworks to current debates.
