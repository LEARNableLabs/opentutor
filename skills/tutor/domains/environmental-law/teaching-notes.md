# Environmental Law and Regulation — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Environmental law is unique among legal subjects because it requires integrating **legal doctrine, scientific principles, policy trade-offs, and real-world implementation**. At the intermediate level, students already have foundational legal knowledge (constitutional law, administrative law basics), so the focus should be on building **statutory literacy** — the ability to navigate complex regulatory schemes — and **cross-cutting themes** like cooperative federalism, technology-forcing, and environmental justice.

**Pedagogical strategies:**
- **Start procedural, then substantive**: NEPA is a gentle entry point (easier to grasp procedural requirements than substantive standards). Use it to introduce administrative law concepts (notice-and-comment, judicial review, standing) that recur throughout.
- **Use a statute-by-statute spiral**: Teach each major statute (CAA, CWA, RCRA, CERCLA, ESA) as a discrete module, then integrate them in synthesis lessons. This builds statutory literacy before demanding cross-statute analysis.
- **Ground in real cases and current events**: Environmental law is intensely practical. Every lesson should connect to a real case, a recent EPA rulemaking, or a current controversy (e.g., WOTUS rule revisions, climate litigation). This keeps the material from feeling abstract.
- **Address policy trade-offs explicitly**: Students often enter with strong normative priors (pro-regulation or anti-regulation). Push them to grapple with competing values: environmental protection vs. economic development, centralized enforcement vs. state flexibility, precaution vs. cost-benefit analysis.
- **Emphasize structure over memorization**: The goal is not to memorize every CAA provision but to understand the statute's architecture (NAAQS → SIPs → enforcement) and apply it to new problems.

This is a **mixed visual-textual** subject: statutory text is critical, but diagrams (permit flowcharts, jurisdictional maps, compliance timelines) help immensely. Encourage students to sketch processes (e.g., Section 7 consultation) to internalize the steps.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **NEPA requires the best environmental outcome** — Students often think NEPA is substantive. Emphasize: NEPA is "look before you leap," not "choose the greenest path." Agencies must disclose and consider, but can choose any alternative if they explain why. This is counterintuitive and requires repeated reinforcement.

2. **EPA is an all-powerful regulator** — In reality, EPA shares power with states (cooperative federalism), is constrained by statutory text (e.g., CAA doesn't explicitly mention CO2 until Massachusetts v. EPA interpreted it), and faces judicial review. Highlight the **fragmented, multi-actor** nature of environmental governance.

3. **Environmental law is mostly about pollution permits** — True for point sources, but misses huge swaths: nonpoint source pollution (largely unregulated), species protection (ESA), land use (wetlands, NEPA), toxic substances (TSCA), climate law (evolving). Correct this early to set accurate expectations.

4. **Strict liability under CERCLA is unfair** — Students trained in tort law expect liability to follow fault. CERCLA's strict, joint, and several liability shocks them. Address this directly: explain the policy rationale (cleanup urgency, polluter pays, spreading costs), acknowledge the harshness, and show how defenses (innocent landowner, de minimis settlements) and contribution actions temper it.

5. **The Clean Water Act regulates all water pollution** — False. CWA only regulates **point sources** via NPDES permits. Nonpoint source pollution (agricultural runoff, stormwater in many contexts) falls through the cracks. This is a critical gap; use it to discuss the limits of the regulatory framework.

6. **Endangered species protection is absolute** — Students often think ESA bans all harm to listed species. In reality, Section 7 consultations can lead to "reasonable and prudent alternatives" that allow some harm, and incidental take permits (Section 10) explicitly authorize take if mitigated. The statute is strong but not absolute.

7. **Climate change law is a separate field** — Climate law is **built on CAA's existing structure** (Massachusetts v. EPA interpreted CAA to cover GHGs; endangerment findings trigger regulation under existing CAA provisions). Treating climate law as separate obscures this doctrinal continuity. Integrate climate throughout, culminating in lessons 21-24.

8. **Environmental justice is just a policy preference** — EJ has legal teeth: Executive Order 12898 (federal agencies must address EJ in NEPA analyses), Title VI disparate impact regulations (EPA can block discriminatory permits), and state EJ statutes (e.g., California). It's not just "nice to have" but a compliance consideration.

9. **Wetlands must be wet year-round to be jurisdictional** — Jurisdictional wetlands are defined by **hydrology, hydric soils, and hydrophytic vegetation**, not just surface water. Seasonal and ephemeral wetlands can be jurisdictional. Use examples to show the breadth of the definition.

10. **Standing is easy to establish** — Students underestimate standing as a barrier. Emphasize: injury must be **concrete and particularized**, causation must be **fairly traceable**, and redressability must be **likely**. Use Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife and Massachusetts v. EPA to show how standing doctrine shapes environmental litigation.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Level (this curriculum):
- **Assume**: basic legal knowledge (case law, statutory interpretation, constitutional law, administrative law concepts)
- **Emphasize**: statutory structure, regulatory implementation, real-world compliance. Less time on abstract policy debates, more on "how does this work in practice?"
- **Depth of formalism**: use statutory text and regulations; don't oversimplify. Students should read actual CAA or CWA provisions, not just summaries. But avoid deep dives into arcane regulatory details unless directly relevant (e.g., NAAQS implementation is important; specific PM2.5 measurement protocols are not).
- **Case law**: use landmark cases (Massachusetts v. EPA, Rapanos, TVA v. Hill, Chevron) to illustrate doctrine, but don't require memorizing every circuit split. Focus on Supreme Court decisions and major administrative law precedents.
- **Interdisciplinary content**: introduce scientific concepts (criteria pollutants, hydrology, species biology) at a level sufficient to understand regulatory triggers, but don't require deep scientific literacy. This isn't an environmental science course.

### If Adapting for Beginner Level:
- **Add**: more background on U.S. legal system, administrative agencies, how regulations are made and enforced
- **Simplify**: focus on a few key statutes (CAA, CWA, ESA) and skip RCRA, CERCLA, or climate law entirely
- **Reduce**: fewer cases, less statutory text, more narrative explanations
- **Skip**: standing doctrine in depth, complex jurisdictional battles (WOTUS), multi-statute synthesis

### If Adapting for Advanced Level:
- **Add**: in-depth regulatory analysis (reading Federal Register notices, writing mock comments), circuit splits, state-level variations, comparative environmental law (EU, international treaties)
- **Increase**: policy analysis (cost-benefit analysis under Executive Orders, precautionary principle, environmental economics)
- **Deepen**: federalism tensions (dormant Commerce Clause, preemption), takings challenges to environmental regulation, statutory interpretation debates (Chevron, major questions doctrine)
- **Expand**: climate law (state initiatives, corporate climate litigation, international climate finance), emerging issues (PFAS regulation, plastics, biodiversity loss)

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

- **The "major questions doctrine" and climate law** — West Virginia v. EPA (2022) limited EPA's authority to regulate GHG emissions from power plants, invoking the major questions doctrine (agencies can't decide major policy questions without clear congressional authorization). This is a seismic shift in administrative law. Drop this in after lesson 21 (Massachusetts v. EPA) to show how doctrine evolves.

- **Takings and wetlands regulation** — Can wetlands regulation be a Fifth Amendment taking requiring compensation? This connects environmental law to constitutional property rights. Mention it in lesson 19 (Section 404) as a rabbit hole for students interested in property law.

- **The Flint water crisis and environmental justice** — Flint is a case study in EJ failure: predominantly Black city, cost-cutting decision to switch water source, lead contamination, slow government response. Use it in lesson 23 to make environmental justice visceral and immediate.

- **Love Canal and the birth of CERCLA** — Love Canal (1970s toxic waste disaster in Niagara Falls, NY) spurred CERCLA's passage. This is environmental law's origin story for Superfund. Drop it in lesson 14 to give historical context.

- **The ozone hole and the Montreal Protocol** — The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out ozone-depleting substances and is the most successful international environmental treaty. It's a model for climate law. Mention it in lesson 24 (international climate agreements) as a contrast to the Paris Agreement's voluntary structure.

- **ESA and the snail darter (TVA v. Hill)** — Supreme Court case holding that ESA's plain language required halting a nearly completed dam to protect a small fish. Illustrates statutory textualism and the strength of ESA. Drop it in lesson 16-18 to show ESA's teeth.

- **Environmental DNA (eDNA) and species monitoring** — New scientific techniques use DNA from water or soil samples to detect species presence without direct observation. This is revolutionizing ESA monitoring and enforcement. Mention it in lesson 16 as a "wild card" — science changing how law is implemented.

- **The Green New Deal and the limits of environmental law** — The Green New Deal (2019 proposal) framed climate action as inseparable from economic justice and job creation. It failed legislatively but reshaped the policy debate. Use it in lesson 21 or 24 to explore the boundaries between environmental law and broader progressive policy.

- **Chevron deference and its potential demise** — Chevron (1984) held that courts defer to agencies' reasonable statutory interpretations. Recent Supreme Court cases have limited Chevron, and some justices want to overturn it. This could reshape environmental law (less deference to EPA). Drop this in after lesson 2 (administrative procedure) for students interested in administrative law.

- **Plastic pollution and the Basel Convention** — International trade in plastic waste is governed by the Basel Convention, recently amended to include plastics. This connects environmental law to international trade law and waste management. Mention it in lesson 12-13 as an international dimension to RCRA.

## Difficulty Progression

### Early Lessons (1-4): Foundations — Difficulty 2-3
Start with **NEPA and administrative law** (procedural, less dense than substantive statutes). Difficulty 2 for NEPA (conceptually straightforward), 3 for standing and Commerce Clause (more abstract, constitutional dimensions). Goal: build foundational concepts that recur throughout.

### Mid-Early Lessons (5-11): CAA and CWA — Difficulty 2-4
**CAA** is relatively accessible (NAAQS → SIPs → enforcement is a clear progression), so start at difficulty 2-3. **CWA** introduces more complexity: NPDES permits (difficulty 3) are manageable, but **WOTUS** (difficulty 4, lesson 11) is the first major bottleneck. Lesson 8 is a review to consolidate before moving to CWA.

### Middle Lessons (12-15): Waste and Cleanup — Difficulty 2-4
**RCRA** (lessons 12-13) is moderate difficulty (2-3) — hazardous waste definitions are detailed but not conceptually hard. **CERCLA** (lesson 14) spikes to difficulty 4 because liability is complex and counterintuitive (strict, joint, and several liability; causation issues). Lesson 15 is a review to consolidate before moving to ESA.

### Mid-Late Lessons (16-20): ESA and Wetlands — Difficulty 2-4
**ESA** starts accessible (listing process, difficulty 2) but builds to difficulty 3-4. Section 7 consultation (lesson 17, difficulty 3) and Section 9 take prohibition (lesson 18, difficulty 4) require integrating procedural and substantive requirements. Lesson 20 is a teach-back to ensure mastery before emerging issues.

### Late Lessons (21-24): Emerging Issues — Difficulty 1-4
**Climate law** (lesson 21) is difficulty 4 — it requires integrating CAA, standing (Massachusetts v. EPA), and policy debates. Lesson 22 is a review to consolidate. **Environmental justice** (lesson 23, difficulty 3) is conceptually accessible but requires normative engagement. **International climate law** (lesson 24, difficulty 3) builds on climate law foundations.

### Synthesis Lessons (25-26): Integration — Difficulty 3-4
Lessons 25-26 require **multi-statute analysis** (difficulty 4 and 3, respectively). These are capstone lessons where students apply everything they've learned to realistic scenarios (siting a factory, enforcing a violation). Difficulty 3 for lesson 26 because enforcement is more bounded than the open-ended compliance analysis in lesson 25.

### Review Lessons (8, 15, 22): Difficulty 1
Reviews are difficulty 1 — low cognitive load, focused on consolidation and spaced repetition. These should feel like a breather, not a challenge.

## Pacing Notes

- **Lessons 1-4**: Move quickly. NEPA and admin law are setup for the real content (statutes). Don't linger.
- **Lessons 5-11**: This is the core. CAA and CWA are the most important statutes for intermediate learners. Spend time here.
- **Lesson 11 (WOTUS)**: Bottleneck. Students will struggle with Rapanos. Be prepared to re-explain or provide supplemental resources.
- **Lesson 14 (CERCLA liability)**: Another bottleneck. Plan to address fairness objections and clarify how liability works in practice.
- **Lessons 21-24**: These are the "payoff" lessons — climate and EJ are why many students care about environmental law. Let students explore and debate.
- **Lessons 25-26**: Capstone. Give students time to work through these — they're synthesizing 25 lessons of material.

## Engagement Strategies

- **Use real cases**: Don't just cite cases — tell the story. Massachusetts v. EPA: states suing EPA to force climate regulation. Rapanos: Michigan developer vs. Army Corps over a wetland. TVA v. Hill: a small fish stopping a dam. Stories stick.
- **Link to current events**: What's EPA doing right now? What climate litigation is pending? What new WOTUS rule is being challenged? This makes the material feel urgent.
- **Invite normative debate**: Should EPA be able to regulate CO2? Is CERCLA liability fair? Should species protection trump economic development? Let students wrestle with these — environmental law is value-laden.
- **Vary delivery formats**: mini-lessons for new content, questions for retrieval practice, resource-drops for exploration, teach-backs for consolidation, real-world scenarios for application. Don't default to mini-lessons every time.
- **Use visuals**: flowcharts for permit processes, maps for jurisdictional waters, timelines for rulemaking. Environmental law is procedurally complex; visuals help.
