# Environmental Law and Regulation — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **NEPA (National Environmental Policy Act)** — procedural statute requiring environmental impact assessment before major federal actions
2. **Environmental impact assessment** — structured process for identifying and disclosing environmental consequences
3. **Notice-and-comment rulemaking** — administrative procedure requiring agencies to solicit and respond to public input
4. **Arbitrary and capricious review** — judicial standard for evaluating agency decisions (low bar, deferential)
5. **Standing** — constitutional and prudential requirements for bringing suit (injury-in-fact, causation, redressability). Depends on: basic constitutional law
6. **Commerce Clause** — constitutional basis for federal environmental regulation. Depends on: constitutional law fundamentals
7. **Cooperative federalism** — regulatory framework where federal government sets standards and states implement. Depends on: Commerce Clause, federalism concepts
8. **Criteria pollutants** — six common air pollutants EPA regulates under CAA (ozone, PM, CO, lead, SO2, NOx)
9. **NAAQS (National Ambient Air Quality Standards)** — health-based and welfare-based air quality targets. Depends on: criteria pollutants
10. **State Implementation Plans (SIPs)** — state-level plans for achieving NAAQS. Depends on: NAAQS, cooperative federalism
11. **Mobile source regulation** — vehicle emissions standards under CAA. Depends on: criteria pollutants, technology-forcing
12. **Technology-forcing standards** — regulations requiring development of new pollution control technology
13. **Water quality standards** — state-defined goals for designated uses of water bodies (swimmable, fishable, etc.)
14. **Point source** — discrete conveyance of pollutants (pipe, ditch) vs nonpoint source (runoff)
15. **NPDES (National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System) permits** — permits required for point source discharges. Depends on: point source, cooperative federalism
16. **Effluent limitations** — technology-based and water quality-based discharge limits. Depends on: NPDES, water quality standards
17. **Waters of the United States (WOTUS)** — jurisdictional scope of CWA. Depends on: Commerce Clause, federalism
18. **Significant nexus test** — Rapanos standard for determining CWA jurisdiction. Depends on: WOTUS, Commerce Clause
19. **RCRA (Resource Conservation and Recovery Act)** — hazardous waste management from cradle to grave
20. **Hazardous waste definition** — EPA's listed and characteristic waste criteria. Depends on: RCRA framework
21. **Land disposal restrictions** — RCRA limits on burying untreated hazardous waste. Depends on: hazardous waste definition
22. **CERCLA (Superfund)** — statute for cleaning up contaminated sites and assigning liability
23. **Strict, joint, and several liability** — CERCLA's expansive liability scheme. Depends on: basic tort concepts
24. **Potentially Responsible Parties (PRPs)** — entities liable under CERCLA (owners, operators, transporters, arrangers). Depends on: CERCLA liability
25. **ESA (Endangered Species Act)** — statute protecting threatened and endangered species
26. **Listing process** — procedure for designating species as threatened or endangered. Depends on: ESA framework, best available science
27. **Critical habitat** — areas essential to species conservation. Depends on: listing process
28. **Section 7 consultation** — requirement for federal agencies to consult on actions that may affect listed species. Depends on: ESA, listing, critical habitat
29. **Jeopardy standard** — test for whether federal action threatens species survival. Depends on: Section 7 consultation
30. **Section 9 take prohibition** — prohibition on killing, harming, or harassing listed species. Depends on: ESA
31. **Incidental take permits** — permits for otherwise lawful activities that may harm listed species. Depends on: Section 9, Section 7
32. **Habitat conservation plans** — mitigation plans required for incidental take permits. Depends on: incidental take permits
33. **Section 404 permits** — Army Corps permits for filling wetlands. Depends on: CWA, WOTUS
34. **Wetlands mitigation** — compensating for wetland loss through restoration or creation. Depends on: Section 404
35. **Massachusetts v. EPA** — Supreme Court case holding EPA can regulate greenhouse gases under CAA. Depends on: CAA, standing
36. **Endangerment finding** — EPA's determination that GHGs endanger public health and welfare. Depends on: Massachusetts v. EPA
37. **Environmental justice** — fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people in environmental decisions. Depends on: NEPA, disparate impact concepts
38. **EJ screening tools** — geospatial tools for identifying environmental justice communities (e.g., EJScreen). Depends on: environmental justice
39. **Paris Agreement** — international climate accord with voluntary national commitments. Depends on: international law basics
40. **Multi-statute compliance** — navigating overlapping requirements of CAA, CWA, RCRA, ESA, NEPA. Depends on: all major statutes
41. **Citizen suits** — provisions allowing private enforcement of environmental laws. Depends on: standing, notice requirements

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **Administrative law** underpins all environmental statutes. Understanding notice-and-comment rulemaking, judicial review standards (arbitrary and capricious, substantial evidence), and standing is essential before diving into substantive statutes.
- **Constitutional law** (Commerce Clause, federalism, due process, takings) provides the framework for federal environmental authority and constraints.
- **NEPA** is the procedural backbone — it applies across agencies and projects, so learning it first makes sense. It's also conceptually simpler (procedural only, not substantive).

### Statute-Specific Dependencies
- **Cooperative federalism** appears in CAA, CWA, and other statutes — understanding it once helps across the board.
- **CAA structure** (NAAQS → SIPs → enforcement) is a template for understanding CWA and other statutes.
- **Point source vs. nonpoint source** is critical for CWA — without this distinction, NPDES permits make no sense.
- **WOTUS** is the jurisdictional foundation for both CWA Section 404 (wetlands) and other CWA programs — settling this early clarifies scope.
- **CERCLA liability** builds on basic tort concepts (strict liability, joint and several liability), but the statutory liability scheme is unique and must be taught explicitly.
- **ESA Sections 7 and 9** work together — Section 7 applies to federal actions, Section 9 to all persons. Teaching them together shows the complementary structure.

### Cross-Cutting Dependencies
- **Climate law** (Massachusetts v. EPA, endangerment finding) depends on understanding CAA's statutory structure and standing doctrine.
- **Environmental justice** overlaps with NEPA (impact assessment), disparate impact analysis, and all substantive statutes (where facilities are sited, who is affected).
- **Multi-statute compliance** is the synthesis — students need to understand individual statutes before they can analyze how they interact.

## Bottlenecks

- **Standing** — this is a barrier for many students. Without a solid grasp of injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability, cases don't make sense. Plan to revisit standing in different contexts (NEPA, CAA, ESA) to reinforce.
- **Cooperative federalism** — the division of federal/state roles is counterintuitive (why doesn't EPA just enforce directly?). This is a recurring theme across CAA, CWA, and ESA, so mastering it early pays dividends.
- **WOTUS / jurisdictional waters** — this is the hardest concept in the curriculum (difficulty 4). Students struggle with the Rapanos plurality/concurrence split and the "significant nexus" test. Use concrete examples (ditches, wetlands, ephemeral streams) and revisit after students have more statutory context.
- **CERCLA liability** — strict, joint, and several liability is harsh and students often resist it as "unfair." Address this head-on by explaining the policy rationale (cleanup urgency, polluter pays) and showing how defenses and settlements work in practice.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **NEPA requires agencies to choose the environmentally preferable alternative** — FALSE. NEPA is procedural only; agencies must disclose impacts but can choose any alternative, even the most harmful, as long as they explain why.

2. **EPA writes all environmental regulations** — FALSE. Under cooperative federalism, states write SIPs (CAA), set water quality standards (CWA), and often run permitting programs. EPA sets floors and backstops.

3. **The Clean Water Act regulates all water pollution** — FALSE. CWA regulates point sources via permits; nonpoint source pollution (agricultural runoff, etc.) is largely unregulated at the federal level.

4. **CERCLA only applies to current owners of contaminated sites** — FALSE. PRPs include past owners, operators, transporters, and arrangers. Liability is retroactive.

5. **Endangered species can't be harmed at all** — FALSE. Incidental take permits and Section 7 consultations allow some harm if mitigated. Section 9's "take" prohibition is broad but not absolute.

6. **Wetlands are only protected if they're wet year-round** — FALSE. Wetlands jurisdiction depends on hydrology, vegetation, and soils. Seasonal or ephemeral wetlands can be jurisdictional.

7. **Climate law is entirely separate from traditional pollution control law** — FALSE. Climate law uses CAA's existing structure (endangerment findings, NAAQS-like standards, SIPs). Understanding CAA is prerequisite to understanding climate law.

8. **Environmental justice is just about fairness** — TRUE but incomplete. EJ also involves legal requirements (NEPA analysis, Title VI disparate impact, Executive Order 12898) and enforcement mechanisms.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **U.S. legal system basics** — needed for understanding federal statutes, regulations, case law, and court structure
- **Constitutional law** — Commerce Clause (for federal authority), federalism (for state/federal roles), due process and takings (for regulatory limits)
- **Administrative law** — notice-and-comment rulemaking, judicial review (APA standards), standing, exhaustion, ripeness, mootness
- **Basic tort law** — helpful for understanding CERCLA liability, nuisance, takings
- **Statutory interpretation** — canons of construction, Chevron deference, legislative history

## Learning Path Notes

- **Start broad, then narrow**: Begin with NEPA (applies everywhere) and administrative law (applies to all statutes), then dive into specific statutes.
- **Pair statutes with cases**: CAA + Massachusetts v. EPA, CWA + Rapanos, ESA + TVA v. Hill, CERCLA + liability cases. Doctrine without application is abstract.
- **Use current events**: EPA's latest WOTUS rule, climate litigation, environmental justice Executive Orders. These make the material feel alive and relevant.
- **Integrate policy throughout**: Environmental law is intensely normative — students should grapple with "why this design?" not just "what does the statute say?"
- **Build to synthesis**: Early lessons are statute-by-statute; late lessons integrate (lesson 25: multi-statute compliance for a new factory).
