# Traditional Joinery — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Grain direction and anisotropy**: wood has very different strength and stiffness along the grain and across it.
2. **Wood movement**: wood shrinks and swells with moisture, mostly across the grain and hardly at all along it. Depends on: 1
3. **Moisture content**: 'seasoned' wood has reached equilibrium with its surroundings. Depends on: 2
4. **End grain vs long grain**: why glue only works well on long-grain surfaces. Depends on: 1
5. **Forces on joints**: tension, compression, shear and racking. Depends on: high-school idea of force
6. **Bending and shear in beams**: one face is in tension, the other in compression, and shear is carried near the supports. Depends on: 5
7. **Holding mechanisms**: glue, pegs, wedges, nails, and mechanical lock. Depends on: 4, 5
8. **Failure modes**: where and how a joint gives way. Depends on: 1, 5, 6, 7
9. **Western core joints**: mortise and tenon (with draw-bore), through and half-blind dovetails, lap, bridle, basic dado, frame-and-panel. Depends on: 2, 7, 8
10. **Tool and layout traditions**: pull and push saws, kanna and bench plane, chisels, marking gauge, sumitsubo, sashigane. Depends on: 9 (as reference vocabulary)
11. **Japanese splices (tsugi)**: koshikake ari tsugi, koshikake kama tsugi, okkake daisen tsugi, kanawa tsugi. Depends on: 6, 8, 10
12. **Japanese connections (shiguchi)**: nagahozo with komisen, watari ago, sampo zashi. Depends on: 8, 11
13. **Cross-tradition comparison**: Depends on: 9, 11, 12
14. **Context and modern practice**: earthquake claims, Ise rebuilding and the UNESCO ICH inscription, computational design (Tsugite). Depends on: 13

## Dependencies

- **Wood movement** needs grain direction, because movement differs by direction. Without it, the student cannot see why a frame-and-panel door or a floating tabletop exists.
- **Failure modes** need forces, grain and holding mechanisms. A joint usually fails where short grain, end grain or a thin section meets the main load.
- **Assembly direction** (dovetail, kama tsugi) depends on the idea of a mechanical lock: a joint that resists pulling in one direction must slide apart in another.
- **Japanese splices** build on bending and shear. Splices often sit in members that bend, so they have to carry tension across the joint.
- **Japanese layout** (sumitsubo, sashigane) comes before the Japanese joints because those joints are laid out from centerlines and reference lines.
- **Shiguchi** build on tsugi. Splices join two members on one axis, while connections join two or more members on several axes.
- **The comparison and the claims lessons** need everything before them. You cannot judge 'traditional joints are better in earthquakes' without knowing loads and failure modes.

## Prerequisite Topics

- High-school idea of force: needed for forces, bending and failure modes (concepts 5–8).
- Basic spatial reasoning or a willingness to build paper or foam models: needed for every 3D joint lesson and for sketching exploded views.
