# Traditional Joinery — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Wood movement** — seasonal expansion/contraction driven by moisture, cross-grain vs long-grain behavior
2. **Grain orientation** — direction of wood fibers determines strength, splitting tendency, and glue surfaces. Depends on: wood movement
3. **Mechanical advantage** — joints that lock through geometry rather than adhesive alone. Depends on: grain orientation
4. **Mortise and tenon** — rectangular peg in rectangular hole, the universal joint of furniture. Depends on: mechanical advantage, grain orientation
5. **Chopping mortises** — waste removal technique using chisel leverage. Depends on: mortise and tenon
6. **Sawing tenons** — shoulder accuracy and cheek parallelism. Depends on: mortise and tenon
7. **Dovetail geometry** — angled pins interlock with tails for pull-out resistance. Depends on: mechanical advantage
8. **Dovetail layout** — spacing, slope angles (1:6 to 1:8), visual balance. Depends on: dovetail geometry
9. **Bridle joint** — open mortise with three-way glue surface. Depends on: mortise and tenon
10. **Japanese philosophy** — reversible assembly, no metal fasteners, wood as living material. Depends on: mechanical advantage
11. **Hozo joint** — Japanese mortise and tenon with wedging and locking variants. Depends on: mortise and tenon, Japanese philosophy
12. **Pull saw mechanics** — cutting on pull stroke with thinner kerf. Depends on: sawing tenons
13. **Shachi-sen** — gooseneck wedge with compound taper for irreversible locking. Depends on: hozo joint, mechanical advantage
14. **Ari joint** — Japanese sliding dovetail, often wedged. Depends on: dovetail geometry, Japanese philosophy
15. **Kumiko** — delicate lattice with precision-fit geometric patterns. Depends on: ari joint, hozo joint
16. **Reversible joinery** — design for disassembly and maintenance access. Depends on: Japanese philosophy, hozo joint
17. **Compound angles** — joints at non-orthogonal intersections. Depends on: dovetail geometry, mortise and tenon
18. **Sliding dovetail** — cross-tradition comparison of Western and Japanese approaches. Depends on: dovetail geometry, ari joint
19. **Joint repair** — dutchman patches, consolidation, minimal intervention. Depends on: wood movement, grain orientation
20. **Technique selection** — matching joint type to structural, aesthetic, and cultural requirements. Depends on: all prior concepts
21. **Furniture history** — how regional styles reflect available tools and joinery knowledge. Depends on: technique selection
22. **Hybrid design** — synthesizing Western and Japanese approaches. Depends on: technique selection, Japanese philosophy

## Dependencies

### Foundational
- **Grain orientation** requires understanding **wood movement** because seasonal expansion happens primarily across the grain, not along it. Joint design must accommodate this.
- **Mechanical advantage** builds on **grain orientation** because the strength of geometric locking depends on long-grain-to-long-grain surfaces resisting shear.

### Western Tradition
- **Mortise and tenon** relies on **mechanical advantage** and **grain orientation** — the long-grain cheeks of the tenon lock into long-grain walls of the mortise.
- **Chopping mortises** and **sawing tenons** are execution techniques that depend on understanding the **mortise and tenon** joint itself.
- **Dovetail geometry** extends **mechanical advantage** by using angled surfaces that resist pulling forces.
- **Dovetail layout** requires mastering **dovetail geometry** to balance structural needs with visual proportion.
- **Bridle joint** is a variant of **mortise and tenon** where one member is open, trading some strength for easier assembly.

### Japanese Tradition
- **Japanese philosophy** stems from **mechanical advantage** taken to an extreme — every joint must work without adhesives.
- **Hozo joint** combines **mortise and tenon** principles with **Japanese philosophy** to create variants with internal wedges and locking mechanisms.
- **Pull saw mechanics** changes the execution of **sawing tenons** by reversing the cutting direction.
- **Shachi-sen** is an advanced application of **hozo joint** and **mechanical advantage**, using compound tapers that tighten under load.
- **Ari joint** merges **dovetail geometry** with **Japanese philosophy**, often adding wedges for extra locking force.
- **Kumiko** requires precision from both **ari joint** and **hozo joint** techniques, scaled down to delicate proportions.

### Advanced & Synthesis
- **Reversible joinery** applies **Japanese philosophy** and **hozo joint** principles to design joints that can be disassembled.
- **Compound angles** challenges both **dovetail geometry** and **mortise and tenon** by requiring non-orthogonal layout.
- **Sliding dovetail** comparison shows how both **dovetail geometry** (Western) and **ari joint** (Japanese) approach the same problem differently.
- **Joint repair** depends on understanding **wood movement** and **grain orientation** to diagnose why joints fail.
- **Technique selection** synthesizes all prior concepts to choose the right joint for context.
- **Furniture history** makes sense only after understanding **technique selection** — styles evolve with joinery knowledge.
- **Hybrid design** requires mastery of **technique selection** and **Japanese philosophy** to intentionally blend traditions.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic hand tools** — needed for all cutting and fitting techniques (chisels, saws, planes, marking tools)
- **Wood identification** — needed for **wood movement** and **grain orientation** concepts
- **Measuring and marking** — needed for **dovetail layout**, **kumiko**, and all precision work
- **Sharpening** — needed for **chopping mortises**, **sawing tenons**, and all cutting operations

## Critical Bottlenecks

1. **Wood movement** — if this isn't internalized early, students will design joints that self-destruct seasonally. Must be reinforced throughout.
2. **Grain orientation** — determines success of every joint. Cutting cross-grain glue surfaces or tenons with short grain across the width leads to failures.
3. **Mechanical advantage** — the conceptual foundation for both traditions. Without this lens, joints become just "shapes to copy" rather than understood systems.
4. **Japanese philosophy** — not just aesthetic preference but a functional constraint that drives technique. Students who skip this see Japanese joints as "fancy versions" of Western joints rather than different design logic.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Tighter is better"** — overly tight joints crack wood, especially across grain. Proper fit allows for wood movement.
- **"Japanese joints are stronger"** — they're mechanically sophisticated but not universally stronger; Western glued joints often exceed strength of wood itself.
- **"You need Japanese tools for Japanese joints"** — the philosophy matters more than the tools; you can cut hozo with Western chisels.
- **"Dovetails are only for drawers"** — they excel wherever you need pull-out resistance: carcase corners, structural frames, etc.
- **"Mortise and tenon is obsolete"** — still the most versatile joint; modern furniture uses it extensively despite machine options.

## Learning Pathways

### Linear Path (Recommended for Beginners)
Foundation → Western Basics → Japanese Basics → Advanced → Synthesis

### Spiral Path (For Experienced Woodworkers)
Alternate Western and Japanese techniques, comparing similar joints directly (mortise/tenon vs hozo, Western dovetail vs ari), then synthesize.

### Project-Based Path
Start with **technique selection** overview, then learn joints on-demand as needed for a specific furniture project. Higher cognitive load but strong motivation.
