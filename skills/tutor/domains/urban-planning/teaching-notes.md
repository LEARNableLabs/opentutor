# Urban Planning and City Science — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Urban planning is fundamentally **observational and interdisciplinary**. Start with what students can see in their own environments — street patterns, public spaces, transportation systems — then build analytical frameworks to understand why cities look and function the way they do. At the intermediate level, balance concrete examples with conceptual frameworks, emphasizing systems thinking and the interconnection of physical, social, economic, and environmental dimensions. Use real-world case studies throughout; planning is a field where theory without practice is hollow, and practice without theory is blind.

This topic uniquely combines **visual/spatial reasoning** (morphology, networks, design), **quantitative analysis** (data, metrics, modeling), and **critical social analysis** (equity, power, justice). Effective teaching weaves these together rather than treating them as separate threads. Students should develop both analytical skills (how to measure accessibility, interpret zoning maps, analyze networks) and critical judgment (whose interests does this serve, what values are embedded in this design, what are the trade-offs).

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Planning is mainly about design/aesthetics"
**Why students believe this:** Visual impact of buildings and spaces is most obvious; planning programs sometimes emphasize design.

**How to correct it:** Emphasize from lesson 1 that urban form is shaped by economics, regulation, social forces, and infrastructure systems, not designer preference alone. Introduce zoning early (lesson 12) to show regulatory determinism. Use data-driven lessons (16-19) to show analytical dimension.

**When it surfaces:** Early lessons when students focus on what they like/dislike about cities rather than understanding causal mechanisms.

### 2. "More capacity solves congestion"
**Why students believe this:** Intuitive engineering logic; if a pipe is full, make it bigger.

**How to correct it:** Dedicate lesson 7 specifically to induced demand with historical examples. Show graph theory perspective: adding edges can worsen performance (Braess's paradox). Connect to economic principle that free goods are overconsumed.

**When it surfaces:** Lesson 6-7 when discussing transportation systems. Will resurface in lesson 25 synthesis.

### 3. "Density is bad for quality of life"
**Why students believe this:** Cultural association of suburban = good, urban = crowded. Conflation of density with poverty or poor building quality.

**How to correct it:** Lesson 3 distinguishes types of density and challenges assumptions. Show examples of low-rise, high-density neighborhoods with excellent quality of life. Connect density to accessibility, walkability, and urban vitality (lessons 2, 6). Address explicitly that density enables amenities and reduces environmental impact.

**When it surfaces:** Very early (lesson 2-3) and throughout. May need repeated addressing.

### 4. "Zoning just organizes uses rationally"
**Why students believe this:** Zoning framed as common-sense separation (don't want factory next to house). Analogy to filing system or categorization.

**How to correct it:** Lessons 12-13 reveal the political history of zoning (explicitly exclusionary origins) and show empirical effects on segregation and affordability. Use specific examples: single-family zoning creating housing shortage, parking minimums driving up costs, use separation preventing walkable mixed-use.

**When it surfaces:** Lesson 12 when introducing zoning. Students may initially defend current system.

### 5. "Data and models are objective/neutral"
**Why students believe this:** Cultural authority of quantification; computational methods seem scientific.

**How to correct it:** Throughout data lessons (16-19), emphasize choices embedded in data collection (what gets measured?), categorization (how is it defined?), and modeling (what assumptions?). Lesson 18 real-world exercise should include critical reflection on data gaps. Connect to equity concerns in lesson 24.

**When it surfaces:** Lessons 16-19 when introducing computational methods.

### 6. "Gentrification is inevitable and/or good"
**Why students believe this:** Either market determinism ("can't stop the market") or improvement framing ("neighborhood getting better").

**How to correct it:** Lesson 22 distinguishes gentrification (change) from displacement (harm). Show anti-displacement strategies that allow improvement without removal. Center impacts on long-term residents. Use data showing policy choices (rent control, land trusts, etc.) affect outcomes.

**When it surfaces:** Lesson 22. Politically charged; expect strong reactions.

### 7. "Community participation means asking for input"
**Why students believe this:** Shallow engagement is common in practice; public meetings called "participation."

**How to correct it:** Lesson 23 introduces Arnstein's ladder of participation, showing spectrum from manipulation to citizen control. Discuss power dynamics explicitly. Lesson 24 teach-back requires designing meaningful process.

**When it surfaces:** Lesson 23. May challenge students' assumptions about democratic planning.

### 8. "Sustainability is mainly about green buildings/technology"
**Why students believe this:** Marketing emphasis on LEED, solar panels, green tech as "sustainability."

**How to correct it:** Lesson 21 frames sustainability as systems property including land use patterns, transportation, social equity, and resilience. Emphasize that urban form (density, mixed-use, transit access) often matters more than building technology.

**When it surfaces:** Lesson 21. Connect back to earlier lessons on density and transit.

### 9. "Planners control what gets built"
**Why students believe this:** Title "planner" suggests control; regulatory power seems authoritative.

**How to correct it:** Throughout, emphasize limits of planning power: market forces, political dynamics, existing property rights, infrastructure constraints. Show planner role as facilitator, analyst, mediator, not dictator. Lessons 14-15 on regulation show both power and limits.

**When it surfaces:** May not surface explicitly but affects understanding throughout. Address when discussing any regulatory topic.

### 10. "Cities in [student's country] work differently"
**Why students believe this:** Real differences in governance, culture, development patterns.

**How to correct it:** Acknowledge real differences but show underlying principles (network effects, accessibility, density, etc.) are universal. Use diverse global examples. Encourage students to analyze their own context using universal frameworks.

**When it surfaces:** Throughout, especially if student is not in US/Europe where many examples originate.

## Level Adjustments

### At Intermediate Level (current)

**Assumed background:**
- Basic familiarity with cities and urban issues from lived experience
- Some exposure to social science or design thinking
- Comfort with data and visualizations (though not necessarily programming)
- Ability to think critically about policy and social systems

**Depth and rigor:**
- Introduce analytical frameworks (graph theory, spatial analysis, modeling) conceptually; application but not derivation
- Discuss research findings and methodologies; students read about studies rather than conducting original research
- Balance is 40% concepts, 30% application, 20% critical analysis, 10% synthesis
- Mathematical content kept to metrics and interpretation, not proofs or complex algorithms
- Policy analysis at "understand mechanisms and trade-offs" level, not legal/regulatory detail

**Formalism:**
- Use precise terminology but define all jargon
- Reference academic concepts (Braess's paradox, induced demand, etc.) but explain with concrete examples
- Cite research to ground claims, but don't require reading full papers
- Diagrams and visualizations central; equations only when illuminating

### If Adjusted to Beginner Level

**Would add:**
- More foundational context on how cities are governed and who makes decisions
- Explicit instruction in map-reading and spatial thinking basics
- Simpler language, more analogies to familiar systems
- More structured exercises with clearer scaffolding
- Focus on observation and pattern recognition before analytical frameworks

**Would remove/simplify:**
- Network topology math (lesson 17) → descriptive only
- Agent-based modeling (lesson 19) → show outputs, don't explain mechanisms
- Dense academic concepts → more intuitive framing
- Assume less background on social systems and policy

### If Adjusted to Advanced Level

**Would add:**
- Original data analysis with GIS/Python tools
- Reading and critiquing primary research papers
- Quantitative methods: regression, spatial statistics, network algorithms
- Deeper regulatory detail: case law, implementation mechanics
- Independent research project synthesizing multiple methods
- Engagement with theoretical debates in planning scholarship

**Would change:**
- Faster pace through foundational concepts (lessons 1-5 condensed)
- More technical depth in computational methods (lessons 16-19)
- Expect students to engage with contradictions in literature
- More emphasis on methodology and epistemology, not just findings
- Balance shifts to 30% concepts, 20% application, 30% critical analysis, 20% synthesis

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### When to deploy

1. **Fractals and power laws in city structure** — Drop after lesson 3 (density) or 16 (urban analytics). Cities show self-similar patterns across scales; population and density often follow power law distributions. Connects to complexity science. Resource: Geoffrey West's "Scale."

2. **The Marchetti constant** — Drop after lesson 6 (accessibility). Humans across cultures/eras spend ~1 hour/day traveling. Cities expand to fill this time budget; faster transport = larger cities but not less travel time. Mind-bending implication for transportation planning.

3. **Jane Jacobs vs. Robert Moses** — Drop after lesson 2 (walkability) or 12 (zoning). Epic 1960s NYC battle between community activist and master builder. Personifies tensions between human-scale urbanism and modernist planning. Highly engaging narrative.

4. **The Bilbao Effect** — Drop after lesson 21 (sustainability) or 25 (synthesis). Museum-led urban transformation; case study in cultural infrastructure as economic development. Questions about authenticity, who benefits, replicability.

5. **Car-free cities (Houten, Netherlands)** — Drop after lesson 8 (bike infrastructure) or 10 (transit). Entire city designed without through-car traffic; how did they do it, could it work elsewhere? Challenges assumptions about necessity of cars.

6. **Kowloon Walled City** — Drop after lesson 3 (density) or 5 (public space). Most dense human settlement in history; self-organized, ungoverned, eventually demolished. Extreme case study in emergent order and regulatory absence.

7. **15-minute city concept** — Drop after lesson 6 (accessibility) or 21 (sustainability). Paris and other cities adopting principle that daily needs should be reachable within 15-minute walk/bike. Synthesizes many course concepts. Currently trendy = high engagement.

8. **Sorting and agglomeration economics** — Drop after lesson 13 (exclusionary zoning) or 22 (gentrification). Economic theory of why similar people/firms cluster. Explains both innovation districts and segregation. Challenges simple normative judgments.

9. **Street names as historical archive** — Drop after lesson 1 (morphology) or 4 (exploration). What streets are named after reveals who had power, which groups were erased. Urban semiotics. Connects to current debates about monuments/memory.

10. **Superblocks (Barcelona)** — Drop after lesson 11 (review) or 25 (synthesis). Bold intervention closing streets to cars, creating pedestrian zones. Resistance and acceptance; measured outcomes. Real-world transformation students can analyze.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

### Arc 1: Observation (Lessons 1-5, Difficulty 2-3)
Start accessible. Build confidence through familiar urban experiences. Difficulty in lesson 3 (density concepts) but still grounded in observable reality.

### Arc 2: Systems (Lessons 6-11, Difficulty 2-4)
Ramp up. Induced demand (lesson 7) and equity metrics (lesson 9) are conceptually challenging (difficulty 4). Review lesson 11 drops back to difficulty 2 for consolidation.

### Arc 3: Regulation (Lessons 12-15, Difficulty 3-4)
Maintain moderate-high difficulty. Exclusionary zoning (lesson 13) is politically complex and conceptually dense (difficulty 4). Real-world application (lesson 15) at difficulty 3 for active learning.

### Arc 4: Computation (Lessons 16-19, Difficulty 3-5)
Peak difficulty. Network topology (lesson 17) and especially agent-based modeling (lesson 19) are abstract and technical. Lesson 19 is maximum difficulty (5) for the entire curriculum. Review lesson 20 critical for recovery.

### Arc 5: Application (Lessons 21-26, Difficulty 1-4)
Varied difficulty but generally more applied than conceptual. Gentrification (lesson 22) and teach-back on equity (lesson 24) are difficult (4) due to complexity and integration required. Final lessons (25-26) at moderate difficulty (3-4) for synthesis rather than new concept load.

### Review Placement
- Lesson 11: After transportation module, before shifting to regulation
- Lesson 20: After computational peak, before application synthesis

Both reviews at difficulty 1-2, focus on connections not new material.

### Pacing Recommendation
If student struggles during lessons 16-19 (computational methods), consider:
- Splitting lesson 17 into two sessions (network basics, then analysis)
- Making lesson 19 (ABM) optional or resource-drop only
- Adding an extra review between 19 and 21
- Reducing difficulty of lesson 17 from 4 → 3 by focusing on concepts over mathematics

If student breezes through early lessons (1-5), consider:
- Combining lessons 4-5 into single session
- Increasing difficulty of lesson 3 to include quantitative density analysis
- Moving faster to lesson 6 to reach challenging content sooner
