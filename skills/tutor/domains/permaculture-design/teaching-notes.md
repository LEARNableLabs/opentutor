# Permaculture Design — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Permaculture design is inherently practical, visual, and systems-oriented. At the intermediate level, focus on **design methodology** over plant lists or specific techniques. The student should learn to *think like a designer* — observing patterns, recognizing relationships, and creating integrated systems. Use real case studies, encourage sketching and mapping, and emphasize that design is iterative. Move from observation to analysis to design to implementation in each major system (water, soil, plants). This is not abstract theory — every concept should connect to "what would you do on this site?"

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Permaculture is just sustainable gardening"
**Why students believe this:** Most permaculture media focuses on food production, and the movement started in agriculture.

**How to correct:** Emphasize permaculture as a **design system** applicable to any human habitat — urban planning, economics, social structures, energy systems. Food production is one application. The 12 principles apply universally. Show examples of permaculture thinking in non-garden contexts.

### 2. "Zones are physical boundaries you draw on a map"
**Why students believe this:** Zone maps look like fixed regions, and beginner resources often present them as static areas.

**How to correct:** Zones are about **frequency of interaction and energy input**, not physical space. A frequently-used herb spiral near the door is Zone 1 even if it's 50 feet from the house. Zones shift based on who's using the land and how. They're planning tools, not property lines.

### 3. "More species = better system"
**Why students believe this:** Permaculture emphasizes biodiversity, so students assume maximum species count is the goal.

**How to correct:** Focus on **functional diversity** and **appropriate stacking**. Random polycultures create competition and confusion. Each plant should serve a purpose in the guild or system. Quality of relationships > quantity of species. A well-designed 7-plant guild beats a chaotic 30-plant mix.

### 4. "Swales go everywhere to catch all the water"
**Why students believe this:** Swales are permaculture's most visible technique and get heavily promoted in videos.

**How to correct:** Swales are for **specific conditions** — gentle slopes (1-10%), appropriate soil, sufficient catchment. Not needed in flat land, counterproductive on steep slopes, wrong tool in sandy soil or high water tables. Teach assessment first, technique second. "When is a swale NOT the answer?"

### 5. "Permaculture means no human intervention after establishment"
**Why students believe this:** Language like "self-maintaining" and "work with nature" suggests passive systems.

**How to correct:** Permaculture designs **reduce** maintenance, but don't eliminate it. Observation and strategic intervention continue. Mature food forests need pruning, thinning, replanting. Animal systems need management. This is "working with nature" not "leaving nature alone." Rewilding ≠ permaculture design.

### 6. "Design happens once, then you implement the plan"
**Why students believe this:** Design presentations show polished final plans, implying a linear process.

**How to correct:** Design is **iterative and adaptive**. Observe → design → implement → observe → adjust. The site teaches you things you couldn't know from analysis alone. Successful designers expect to redesign as systems mature and conditions change. "Start small and slow" means learning by doing, not executing a master plan.

### 7. "Native plants are always better than non-native"
**Why students believe this:** Permaculture's ecological focus overlaps with native plant movements.

**How to correct:** Permaculture prioritizes **function and relationship** over origin. Many food crops are non-native. Some natives are poor functional fits. Consider: invasiveness, function, climate suitability, guild fit, local ecosystem impact. "Right plant, right place, right function" > "native vs. non-native."

### 8. "You need land to do permaculture"
**Why students believe this:** Most examples are rural homesteads or farms.

**How to correct:** Show **urban permaculture** — balcony gardens, community lots, food forests in parks, greywater systems in apartments, neighborhood resilience networks. Design thinking scales from windowsill to bioregion. Principles apply to renters, apartment dwellers, urban contexts.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners (vs. this intermediate curriculum)
- Start with **concrete techniques** (composting, herb spirals, simple guilds) before design theory
- Use more prescriptive guidance ("plant this guild") vs. generative design ("create a guild for this context")
- Limit scope to backyard/garden scale, not whole properties
- Simplify to 5-6 core principles instead of all 12
- Skip scale of permanence, keyline design, complex earthworks

### For Intermediate (this curriculum)
- **Design methodology** is central — teach the process, not just techniques
- Expect students to **analyze and create** original designs for novel contexts
- Cover all 12 principles and show how they interact
- Include water systems, earthworks, and whole-site planning
- Use case studies to show real-world complexity and tradeoffs
- Emphasize troubleshooting and adaptive design

### For Advanced (beyond this curriculum)
- Deep dive into **specific systems** (Keyline, water harvesting, agroforestry, etc.)
- Regional/climate specialization (permaculture in deserts, tropics, cold climates)
- Integrate **regenerative agriculture** and ecosystem restoration
- Cover social permaculture, community design, and bioregional planning
- Professional practice: client work, design presentations, permitting, implementation management
- Advanced water engineering, complex animal integration, perennial crop breeding

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### When covering Observation (Lesson 3)
→ **Phenology and indigenous knowledge** — How traditional cultures tracked seasonal patterns without calendars. Show phenology wheels and animal behavior as seasonal markers.

### When covering Edge Effect (Lesson 10)
→ **Fractals in nature** — Coastlines, branching patterns, romanesco broccoli. How increasing edge through fractal patterns maximizes productivity. Connect to mandala gardens.

### When covering Soil Food Web (Lesson 15)
→ **Mycorrhizal networks** — The "wood wide web" and how fungi connect forest systems. Paul Stamets' work on mushroom cultivation and ecosystem restoration.

### When covering Plant Guilds (Lesson 17)
→ **Three Sisters agriculture** — Indigenous polyculture as sophisticated ecological design. Explore how different cultures developed guild systems (milpa, forest gardens, etc.).

### When covering Scale of Permanence (Lesson 22)
→ **P.A. Yeomans and Keyline Design** — The Australian origins of landscape regeneration at scale. How one farmer's water system thinking influenced global permaculture.

### When covering Pattern Language (Lesson 23)
→ **Christopher Alexander's architecture** — How "A Pattern Language" influenced permaculture design thinking. Cross-pollination between architecture and ecological design.

### Throughout the curriculum
→ **Regenerative agriculture connections** — Joel Salatin, Allan Savory's holistic management, mob grazing, market gardening (Jean-Martin Fortier). Where permaculture overlaps with and differs from other sustainable ag movements.

→ **Climate change adaptation** — How permaculture design principles apply to resilience in changing climate. Water security, perennial systems, polycultures as adaptation strategies.

→ **Indigenous land management** — Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK), fire ecology, terracing, ancient food forests. Recognize permaculture as rediscovering/formalizing practices indigenous cultures have long used.

## Difficulty Progression

### Foundation (Lessons 1-7, Difficulty 1-3)
Start accessible — ethics are intuitive, observation is practical, zones/sectors are visual and concrete. Build confidence with straightforward mapping exercises. Peak at lesson 6 (sector analysis requires integrating multiple external forces) but return to review before principles.

### Principles (Lessons 8-12, Difficulty 2-3)
Principles themselves are digestible, but **application** is harder. "Stacking functions" (lesson 9) and "edge effect" (lesson 10) require systems thinking. Balance harder concepts with mini-lessons. Review at lesson 12 solidifies before moving to technical systems.

### Water & Soil (Lessons 13-16, Difficulty 2-4)
**Peak difficulty at lessons 13-14** (swales and earthworks) — this is where math, site analysis, and hydrology converge. Most students struggle here. Then ease back with soil biology (fascinating but not technically demanding) and sheet mulching (practical and accessible).

### Biological Systems (Lessons 17-21, Difficulty 2-4)
Guilds (lesson 17) are complex — requires integrating plant knowledge, soil understanding, and function stacking. Peak difficulty. Forest gardens (18) build on guilds but are more conceptual. Animal integration and IPM are moderate. Review at 21 consolidates biological thinking.

### Integration & Implementation (Lessons 22-28, Difficulty 2-5)
**Highest peaks** at lessons 24-25 — troubleshooting failed designs (demands diagnostic thinking across all concepts) and whole-site planning (synthesizes everything). These are capstone challenges. Frame them as rewarding summits. Return to easier terrain with phased implementation and maintenance (practical planning, lower cognitive load).

### Review Placement Strategy
- Lesson 7: After observation/analysis, before principles
- Lesson 12: After principles, before technical systems
- Lesson 21: After biological systems, before integration
- Lesson 26: After design challenges, before implementation

This creates **four learning blocks** with consolidation points, preventing overwhelm and reinforcing concepts before building higher.

## Teaching Tips

- **Visual learners thrive here** — encourage sketching, mapping, diagrams. Show photos of real systems.
- **Use the student's climate/location** — make examples relevant to their bioregion and conditions.
- **Start every system with a question** — "What's wrong with this water flow?" before teaching swales.
- **Case studies > abstract theory** — analyze real designs (successful and failed) constantly.
- **Connect to their experience** — if they garden, relate to what they already know; if they don't, use urban examples.
- **Celebrate iteration** — praise students who revise designs based on new understanding; this is the skill.
