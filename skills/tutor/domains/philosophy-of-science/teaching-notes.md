# Philosophy of Science — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Philosophy of science sits at the intersection of abstract reasoning and concrete scientific practice, making it ideal for intermediate learners who can appreciate both. The key pedagogical challenge is balancing conceptual rigor with accessibility—avoiding both oversimplification and technical jargon. Use **case studies from real science** (Einstein vs. Newton, Darwin's theory, quantum mechanics) to ground abstract concepts, then extract the philosophical lessons. At the intermediate level, emphasize **critical engagement**: don't just present views, but show why they emerged, what problems they solve, and where they fail. Encourage students to see philosophy of science as an ongoing conversation, not a settled body of doctrine.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Science proves things true"** — Students often believe scientific theories are proven, when in fact they're supported by evidence but remain fallible. Emphasize that confirmation is always partial and theories remain open to revision. Use historical examples (phlogiston, Newtonian mechanics) to show how even well-supported theories can be overturned.

2. **"The scientific method is a fixed procedure"** — Many students think there's one scientific method (like a recipe). In reality, scientific methodology varies across fields and has evolved historically. Show diversity: experimental physics vs. observational astronomy vs. theoretical mathematics vs. field biology. Kuhn's work is especially helpful here.

3. **"Falsification solves all problems"** — After learning Popper, students may think falsificationism is the final word. Counter with Duhem-Quine: you can't falsify theories in isolation. Use examples like the discovery of Neptune (protecting Newton's theory) vs. the abandonment of the luminiferous ether.

4. **"Observation is pure and objective"** — Students need to overcome naive empiricism. Theory-ladenness doesn't mean observations are worthless, but they're not theory-neutral. Use the duck-rabbit illusion, or better, show how astronomers using different theories (Ptolemaic vs. Copernican) "saw" different things in the same planetary motions.

5. **"Kuhn means science is relativistic and irrational"** — Students often misread Kuhn as saying "anything goes" or that paradigm shifts are purely social. Clarify that Kuhn describes how science works, not whether it's rational. Paradigm shifts have rational elements (anomaly accumulation, problem-solving capacity) even if they're not purely logical.

6. **"Induction and deduction are equally valid"** — Students may think these are just different but equally sound modes of reasoning. Emphasize that deduction preserves truth with logical necessity, while induction is ampliative but not logically valid. Hume's problem targets this asymmetry.

7. **"Philosophy of science is about criticizing science"** — Some students see philosophy of science as hostile to science or as debunking it. Reframe: philosophy of science *clarifies* and *understands* science, helping to identify good reasoning from bad, genuine progress from mere change. Many working scientists engage with philosophy of science precisely to improve their practice.

8. **"Bayesianism solves the problem of induction"** — Students may think Bayesian epistemology fully answers Hume. Clarify that it formalizes how to update beliefs, but doesn't eliminate the need for prior probabilities or justify the uniformity of nature. It's a powerful tool, not a complete solution.

9. **"Laws of nature are just regularities"** — Students may think laws are just observed patterns. Introduce the necessity and counterfactual support of laws: "All gold spheres are less than 1km in diameter" might be true but isn't a law, whereas "No uranium spheres are less than 1km in diameter" reflects a law (critical mass).

10. **"Realism and instrumentalism are about whether science 'works'"** — Students conflate pragmatic success with metaphysical commitment. Both realists and instrumentalists agree science works; they disagree about what that success means. Does successful prediction require that theories describe reality (realism), or is prediction enough (instrumentalism)?

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Learners (Current Level)

- **Formalism**: Introduce logical structure (modus tollens, probability theory) when helpful, but don't get lost in formal details. Use symbolic logic sparingly—only when it clarifies rather than obscures.

- **Historical context**: Provide enough history to show why views emerged (Vienna Circle's response to metaphysics, Kuhn's reaction to logical positivism) but don't drown in historical detail. Focus on philosophical arguments, not biographical minutiae.

- **Primary sources**: Assign short excerpts from Popper, Kuhn, Hempel—not full books. Students at this level can engage with original texts but need guidance on what to focus on.

- **Depth**: Go beyond "Philosophy of science for beginners" overviews. Engage with objections and counter-objections. For example, don't just teach falsificationism; show the auxiliary hypothesis problem and how Lakatos tried to fix it.

- **Breadth**: Cover the major schools (positivism, Popper, Kuhn, Bayesianism) but you don't need to cover every minor variant. Skip Feyerabend's "anything goes" unless the student shows interest in radical positions.

### Adjustments from Beginner

Beginners need more hand-holding with basic distinctions (induction vs. deduction, science vs. pseudoscience) and fewer critiques. At intermediate, assume students can follow "Yes, but..." reasoning: "Popper offers falsificationism as a demarcation criterion, BUT auxiliary hypotheses complicate this, HOWEVER Lakatos tried to save it with research programmes..."

### Adjustments to Advanced

Advanced students would engage with formal confirmation theory (Carnap, Hempel), sophisticated Bayesian decision theory, detailed case studies from history of science (Galileo, Lavoisier, Einstein), philosophy of specific sciences (biology, social sciences), and contemporary debates (structural realism, feminist epistemology, science and values). Keep these as "rabbit holes" for curious intermediate students but don't require them.

## Rabbit Holes (for curious students)

- **Lakatos's research programmes** — A sophisticated middle ground between Popper and Kuhn. Introduces "progressive" vs. "degenerating" research programmes as a criterion for theory choice. Drop this in after lesson 15 if student wants to go deeper on falsificationism.

- **Feyerabend's "anything goes"** — Anarchistic epistemology claiming methodological pluralism. Provocative and fun, but often misunderstood. Good for students who enjoy radical critiques. Introduce after lesson 19 if they want to push Kuhn further.

- **Nancy Cartwright's "dappled world"** — Argues that fundamental laws don't govern the world as we experience it; scientific success comes from models and local regularities, not universal laws. Great for students interested in applied science or engineering. Drop in after lesson 25.

- **Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism** — A middle position between realism and instrumentalism: science aims for empirical adequacy, not truth about unobservables. More nuanced than simple instrumentalism. Introduce after lesson 25 if they're engaged with the realism debate.

- **Social epistemology and values in science** — How do social factors (funding, institutions, gender, race) influence theory choice? Not just Kuhn's sociology of science, but contemporary work on value-free vs. value-laden science. Relevant to students interested in science policy or ethics.

- **Formal philosophy of science** — Probability theory, confirmation theory (Carnap, Hempel), formal learning theory. For mathematically inclined students who want rigor. Can be introduced alongside lesson 21 (Bayesian epistemology).

- **Philosophy of special sciences** — Philosophy of physics (spacetime, quantum mechanics), philosophy of biology (natural selection, teleology), philosophy of social science (interpretation, laws). Deep dives into how general philosophy of science applies to specific fields. Good if student has background in one of these sciences.

- **Inference to the best explanation (IBE)** — Often mentioned but rarely analyzed deeply. The logic of choosing theories based on explanatory virtues (simplicity, unification, coherence). Can be explored after lessons 9 and 24.

- **The pessimistic meta-induction** — Argument against realism: past theories we thought were true (phlogiston, ether) turned out false, so why think current theories are true? A historical argument with philosophical implications. Good counter to realism after lesson 25.

## Difficulty Progression

**Lessons 1-3 (Difficulty 1-2)**: Gentle introduction. Demarcation, basic observation vs. theory, induction. Build confidence with familiar concepts.

**Lessons 4-5 (Difficulty 3)**: First conceptual challenge—theory-ladenness and underdetermination. Students start questioning naive views.

**Lessons 6-8 (Difficulty 3-4)**: Deep engagement with Hume's problem. This is the first major philosophical puzzle. Lesson 8 (Bayesianism) is the peak so far.

**Lesson 9 (Difficulty 1)**: Review. Consolidate before moving to historical schools.

**Lessons 10-12 (Difficulty 2-4)**: Logical positivism. Start accessible, build to Quine's critique (difficult).

**Lessons 13-15 (Difficulty 2-4)**: Falsificationism. Similar arc: accessible start, challenging critiques.

**Lesson 16 (Difficulty 1)**: Review. Prepare for Kuhn.

**Lessons 17-19 (Difficulty 3-4)**: Kuhn is conceptually rich and historically grounded. Incommensurability and the progress question are challenging.

**Lessons 20-22 (Difficulty 3-4)**: Confirmation theory. Hempel's paradoxes and crucial experiments are subtle.

**Lesson 23 (Difficulty 2)**: Review. Lower cognitive load before final module.

**Lessons 24-26 (Difficulty 2-4)**: Explanation, laws, realism. Lesson 25 (realism vs. instrumentalism) is a peak. Lesson 26 (synthesis) is moderate—bring it all together.

**Overall arc**: Start gentle, build to multiple peaks (Hume, Quine, Kuhn, realism debate), with reviews strategically placed to consolidate. The difficulty curve resembles a staircase with periodic plateaus, not a steady climb.
