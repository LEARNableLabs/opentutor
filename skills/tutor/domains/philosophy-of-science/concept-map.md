# Philosophy of Science — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Demarcation problem** — What distinguishes science from non-science or pseudoscience?
2. **Scientific method** — The systematic approach to inquiry and theory-building
3. **Testability** — Whether a claim can be empirically examined. Depends on: demarcation problem
4. **Observation vs. theory** — Relationship between empirical data and theoretical frameworks
5. **Theory-ladenness** — The idea that observation is influenced by theoretical background. Depends on: observation vs. theory
6. **Underdetermination** — When multiple theories fit the same evidence. Depends on: observation vs. theory
7. **Induction** — Reasoning from particular observations to general laws. Depends on: scientific method
8. **Hume's problem of induction** — Why past regularities don't logically guarantee future ones. Depends on: induction
9. **Inference to best explanation** — Choosing theories based on explanatory power. Depends on: Hume's problem, underdetermination
10. **Confirmation theory** — How evidence supports or confirms theories. Depends on: Hume's problem
11. **Bayesian epistemology** — Probabilistic approach to belief updating. Depends on: confirmation theory
12. **Logical positivism** — Movement requiring empirical verification for meaningfulness
13. **Verificationism** — The verification principle of meaning. Depends on: logical positivism
14. **Analytic vs. synthetic** — Distinction between truths of meaning and truths of fact. Depends on: logical positivism
15. **Falsificationism** — Popper's view that science advances by refutation. Depends on: demarcation problem, verificationism
16. **Asymmetry of verification** — Why falsification is logically stronger than confirmation. Depends on: falsificationism
17. **Auxiliary hypotheses** — Background assumptions in theory testing. Depends on: falsificationism, underdetermination
18. **Duhem-Quine thesis** — Theories face evidence holistically, not in isolation. Depends on: auxiliary hypotheses
19. **Paradigms** — Kuhn's framework of shared assumptions and practices. Depends on: theory-ladenness
20. **Normal science** — Puzzle-solving within a paradigm. Depends on: paradigms
21. **Scientific revolutions** — Paradigm shifts in scientific practice. Depends on: paradigms, normal science
22. **Incommensurability** — Inability to fully compare theories across paradigms. Depends on: scientific revolutions
23. **Crisis in science** — Accumulation of anomalies leading to paradigm change. Depends on: normal science, scientific revolutions
24. **Scientific progress** — Whether science moves toward truth or just changes. Depends on: scientific revolutions, incommensurability
25. **Paradoxes of confirmation** — Logical puzzles in confirmation theory (e.g., Hempel's ravens). Depends on: confirmation theory
26. **Crucial experiments** — Tests designed to decide between theories. Depends on: Duhem-Quine thesis, confirmation theory
27. **Scientific explanation** — What it means to explain a phenomenon scientifically
28. **Covering law model** — Hempel's deductive-nomological account of explanation. Depends on: scientific explanation
29. **Laws of nature** — The status and nature of scientific laws. Depends on: scientific explanation
30. **Scientific realism vs. instrumentalism** — Whether theories describe reality or just predict observations. Depends on: laws of nature, scientific progress

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **Theory-ladenness** requires understanding **observation vs. theory** because it challenges the naive view that observations are theory-neutral
- **Underdetermination** requires **observation vs. theory** because it shows how the same observations can support different theoretical interpretations
- **Hume's problem** requires understanding **induction** because it's a fundamental challenge to inductive reasoning itself

### Positivism Chain
- **Verificationism** depends on **logical positivism** as its core principle
- **Analytic vs. synthetic** is central to **logical positivism** because it distinguishes logical truths from empirical claims
- **Falsificationism** emerges partly as a response to problems with **verificationism**

### Popper's Framework
- **Asymmetry of verification** depends on **falsificationism** as its logical foundation
- **Auxiliary hypotheses** become crucial in **falsificationism** because they protect core theories from falsification
- **Duhem-Quine thesis** builds on **auxiliary hypotheses** to challenge naive falsificationism

### Kuhnian Framework
- **Paradigms** depend on **theory-ladenness** because paradigms determine what counts as observation
- **Normal science** operates within **paradigms** as puzzle-solving
- **Scientific revolutions** involve **paradigm** shifts, not just theory replacement
- **Incommensurability** arises from **scientific revolutions** because paradigms define their own standards

### Confirmation & Evidence
- **Confirmation theory** responds to **Hume's problem** by formalizing evidential support
- **Bayesian epistemology** provides a probabilistic version of **confirmation theory**
- **Paradoxes of confirmation** reveal logical puzzles within **confirmation theory**
- **Crucial experiments** relate to both **confirmation theory** and **Duhem-Quine thesis**

### Explanation & Realism
- **Covering law model** provides a specific account of **scientific explanation**
- **Laws of nature** are central to the **covering law model**
- **Scientific realism vs. instrumentalism** debates the metaphysical status of **laws of nature** and theories

## Bottlenecks

### Hume's Problem of Induction
This is a **critical bottleneck**. Many later concepts (confirmation theory, Bayesian epistemology, inference to best explanation) are responses to or refinements of this problem. Students must fully grasp why induction lacks deductive justification before moving to solutions.

### Theory-Ladenness
Understanding that observations aren't theory-neutral is essential for grasping Kuhn's paradigms, the problem of crucial experiments, and the underdetermination thesis. This concept shifts students from naive empiricism to sophisticated philosophy of science.

### Duhem-Quine Thesis
This holistic challenge to testing individual hypotheses is crucial for understanding why both verification and falsification face problems in practice. It's a prerequisite for appreciating Kuhn's work and the complexity of theory choice.

### Paradigms
Kuhn's concept of paradigms integrates many earlier ideas (theory-ladenness, underdetermination, holism) and requires students to think about science as a social practice, not just a logical structure.

## Common Branching Points

### After Hume's Problem
Students can explore:
- Probabilistic responses (Bayesian epistemology)
- Pragmatic justifications
- Naturalized epistemology
- Or move to Popper's attempted solution via falsification

### After Falsificationism
Students can explore:
- Sophisticated versions (Lakatos's research programmes)
- Historical critiques (Kuhn, Feyerabend)
- Or continue to formal confirmation theory

### Realism Debate
After covering the main schools of thought, students can examine:
- Scientific realism (theories aim at truth)
- Instrumentalism (theories are tools for prediction)
- Structural realism (theories capture structure, not essence)
- Social constructivism (theories are social products)

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic logic** — needed for understanding deduction, induction, and logical validity (used throughout)
- **Critical thinking** — needed for analyzing arguments and identifying fallacies (especially in demarcation, verification)
- **Familiarity with scientific concepts** — needed for case studies and examples from physics, biology (used in real-world lessons)
- **Basic probability** (optional) — helpful for Bayesian epistemology but can be introduced as needed
