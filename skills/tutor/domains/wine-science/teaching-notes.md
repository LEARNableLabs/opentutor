# Wine Science — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Wine science sits at the intersection of chemistry, biology, agriculture, and sensory science—making it both highly interdisciplinary and grounded in tangible, sensory experiences. At the intermediate level, the focus should be on **mechanistic understanding** rather than memorization, while maintaining the connection to real wines and winemaking decisions. Use concrete examples from well-known wine regions and varieties (Napa Cabernet, Burgundy Pinot Noir, Champagne) to anchor abstract concepts. Where possible, encourage students to taste wines or watch winemaking process videos to build intuition alongside theory. This topic lends itself to **question-driven learning**—most lessons should present a winemaking puzzle or observation (e.g., "Why does this wine taste vegetal?") and work backward to the science.

The curriculum balances viticulture (field biology and agronomy) with enology (chemistry and microbiology). Students often enter with more intuition about the former, so fermentation science (lessons 12-16) may require slower pacing and more worked examples. The sensory component (lessons 23-27) should integrate throughout rather than being bolted on at the end—regularly reference how chemical changes manifest in aroma and flavor.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. **"Terroir is just marketing talk"**
Students often dismiss terroir as subjective or romantic, or conversely, accept it uncritically without mechanism. Reality: Terroir describes **real, measurable effects** of soil mineral content, water availability, temperature, and sunlight on grape metabolism (e.g., potassium uptake affects pH, cooler nights preserve acidity, shallow soils stress vines and concentrate flavors). The challenge is distinguishing terroir from winemaking technique, vintage variation, and placebo effects. Emphasize measurable chemistry and physiology.

### 2. **"More hang time = better wine"**
Extended ripening is fashionable, but **phenolic ripeness ≠ maximum sugar**. Students often assume that waiting for full physiological ripeness always improves quality, missing that overripeness can lead to raisining, high alcohol, flabby acidity, and loss of aromatic freshness. The best harvest date is **variety-specific, site-specific, and style-dependent**. Teach the tradeoffs.

### 3. **"Sulfur dioxide is bad / unnatural"**
SO₂ is stigmatized, but it's the most versatile winemaking tool: antioxidant, antimicrobial, and found naturally in fermentation. Without it (or extreme care), wines oxidize or develop microbial faults. The "natural wine" movement's rejection of SO₂ is philosophically interesting but technically challenging. Students should understand **why** winemakers use it, what it does chemically, and the tradeoffs of low-sulfite winemaking.

### 4. **"Wild yeast fermentation is always better (or worse)"**
This is tribal: some winemakers swear by native fermentation for "terroir expression," others by inoculation for control and reliability. Reality: **both can make great wine or disaster**. Native fermentation risks stuck fermentation, off-flavors, or volatile acidity, but can produce complexity. Inoculation ensures completion and predictability but may homogenize flavor. Teach the microbial ecology and practical tradeoffs, not dogma.

### 5. **"Oak adds flavor, steel doesn't"**
Students often think stainless steel is neutral and oak is "flavor additives." Better framing: oak allows **micro-oxygenation** (polymerizes tannins, develops tertiary aromas) *and* extracts compounds (vanillin, lactones). Steel prevents oxidation and preserves primary fruit aromatics. Both are active choices shaping wine structure and evolution, not just "flavor on/off."

### 6. **"Acidity is just pH"**
pH and titratable acidity (TA) are distinct and both matter. A wine can have low pH but high TA (lots of strong acid) or high pH but low TA (little acid, weakly buffered). Students often conflate them or think pH alone determines "sharpness." Teach the chemistry: **pH is hydrogen ion activity; TA is total acid concentration**. Sensory perception correlates more with TA, but microbial stability depends on pH.

### 7. **"Tannin comes from oak"**
Oak contributes tannin, but **most tannin in red wine comes from grape skins and seeds**. Students underestimate the importance of extraction technique (maceration length, temperature, cap management) and seed contribution (seeds = harsher, astringent tannins). Oak tannins are softer and integrate with grape tannins during aging.

### 8. **"Organic viticulture = better wine"**
Organic certification restricts synthetic pesticides and fertilizers but doesn't guarantee quality. Some organic vineyards produce exceptional wine; others struggle with disease pressure or nutrient deficiency. Students should understand **IPM (integrated pest management)** as a science-based approach balancing sustainability and vine health, whether certified organic or not. Biodynamics adds another layer (often mystical), but some serious estates practice it—focus on what's empirically testable (compost quality, spray timing) vs. untestable (cosmic rhythms).

### 9. **"Malolactic fermentation always happens"**
MLF is **encouraged** in most reds and some full-bodied whites (e.g., Chardonnay), but often **blocked** in crisp whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling) to preserve malic acid's sharpness. It's not automatic—winemakers inoculate with *Oenococcus oeni* or control temperature/SO₂ to allow or prevent it. Students often assume it's a passive consequence of fermentation.

### 10. **"Sweetness = residual sugar only"**
Perception of sweetness in wine is influenced by **residual sugar, alcohol, glycerol, and fruit ripeness aromatics**. A dry wine (< 2 g/L RS) can taste "sweet" if it has high alcohol (≥15%) and ripe fruit esters. Conversely, a wine with 10 g/L RS can taste balanced if it has high acidity. Teach the **sensory integration** of sugar, acid, alcohol, and tannin.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students (target level)

- **Assume**: basic chemistry (functional groups, pH, redox), basic biology (photosynthesis, microbiology), and some wine familiarity (grape varieties, regions).
- **Emphasize**: mechanisms and decision-making. Why does a winemaker choose technique X over Y? What's the chemistry behind observation Z?
- **Depth**: cover fermentation biochemistry (glycolysis pathway), phenolic chemistry (anthocyanins, tannins, polymerization), and sensory thresholds.
- **Minimize**: rote memorization of regions/vintages, overly detailed taxonomy, or industrial-scale engineering.

### If the Student is More Beginner

- Spend extra time on chemistry fundamentals: redox, acids/bases, esterification.
- Add more visual aids: fermentation curves, phenolic structure diagrams, climate maps.
- Simplify vocabulary: use "tannin" before "proanthocyanidin," "yeast food" before "YAN (yeast assimilable nitrogen)."
- Include more tasting exercises or video walkthroughs of winemaking to build intuition before diving into chemistry.

### If the Student is More Advanced

- Introduce primary literature: research papers on yeast genetics, climate change modeling, or oak compound extraction kinetics.
- Dive deeper into analytical chemistry: HPLC, GC-MS for aroma analysis; spectrophotometry for phenolics.
- Add quantitative problems: calculate alcohol from Brix, predict pH from TA and pKa, model fermentation kinetics.
- Discuss controversial topics: minimal intervention winemaking, "natural" wine definitions, precision viticulture technologies.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Tangents)

### **The phylloxera catastrophe (1860s–1900s)**
A tiny aphid-like insect (*Daktulosphaira vitifoliae*) nearly destroyed European viticulture by attacking vine roots. The solution—grafting European *Vitis vinifera* onto resistant American rootstock—transformed global wine industry structure. Drop this in during Lesson 8 (rootstocks) as an example of **biological crisis driving innovation**. Connects to modern concerns about Pierce's disease, climate-driven pest migration, and genetic diversity.

### **The 1976 Judgment of Paris**
Blind tasting where California wines beat French wines, upending assumptions about Old World supremacy and launching Napa Valley's global reputation. Useful for Lesson 3 (terroir) or Lesson 26 (climate change) to discuss **New World vs. Old World styles** and how site, technique, and climate interact. Sparks debate about whether terroir is destiny or winemaking is king.

### **The "cult wine" phenomenon**
Ultra-expensive, low-production wines (Screaming Eagle, Harlan Estate) often command $500–$5,000/bottle. What's the science vs. marketing? Connects to Lesson 9 (harvest decisions—extreme ripeness, low yields) and Lesson 17 (extraction—maximalist techniques). Prompts critical thinking about **quality vs. scarcity vs. hype**.

### **Orange wine / skin-contact whites**
Ancient technique (especially in Georgia, Italy) experiencing modern revival: white grapes fermented with skins like reds, producing tannic, deeply colored wines. Challenges assumptions about white winemaking (Lesson 17, 21). Discuss phenolic extraction from white grape skins, oxidative aging, and whether "orange wine" is a distinct category or a marketing label.

### **Brettanomyces: character or flaw?**
"Brett" (*Brettanomyces bruxellensis*) produces "barnyard," "leather," or "Band-Aid" aromas via volatile phenols (4-ethylphenol). In tiny amounts, some winemakers (especially in Rhône, older Bordeaux) consider it **complexity**; in larger amounts, it's a **fault**. Use in Lesson 24 (faults) to explore **subjective vs. objective quality** and how sensory thresholds and cultural context shape evaluation.

### **Minimal intervention / "natural" wine**
Philosophical movement toward low-tech winemaking: native yeast, no additives (or minimal SO₂), unfiltered/unfined. Sparks fierce debate about **terroir purity vs. quality risk**. Connect to Lessons 13 (native fermentation), 21 (stabilization), 24 (faults). Excellent for critical discussions about tradeoffs, definitions, and ideology vs. empiricism in winemaking.

### **Climate change and shifting wine regions**
Warming temperatures are pushing viticulture northward (England, Scandinavia emerging; Bordeaux, Napa facing heat stress). Some regions may become unviable for traditional varieties; others will open up. Drop into Lesson 26 but can seed throughout (Lesson 4: climate classification; Lesson 9: harvest timing shifts). Connects wine science to **global environmental change**.

### **Precision viticulture and AI**
Drones, satellite imagery, soil sensors, and machine learning are enabling **site-specific management**: variable pruning, targeted irrigation, selective harvest. Futuristic but increasingly real. Use in Lesson 7 (canopy management) or Lesson 10 (IPM) to show how technology is transforming traditional farming. Could inspire students interested in data science + agriculture.

## Difficulty Progression

The curriculum starts accessible (vine biology, terroir) to build confidence and intuition. **Lessons 9, 14, 17, and 23 are difficulty peaks (level 4)**—these require integrating multiple prior concepts and navigating tradeoffs:

- **Lesson 9 (ripeness)**: Balancing sugar, acid, phenolics—multidimensional optimization.
- **Lesson 14 (fermentation aroma)**: Complex interaction of temperature, nutrients, yeast strain on volatile chemistry.
- **Lesson 17 (extraction)**: Phenolic chemistry and maceration techniques—subtle and consequential.
- **Lesson 23 (aging chemistry)**: Redox reactions and polymerization over time—abstract and dynamic.

**Review lessons (11, 22)** are scheduled after conceptual arcs to consolidate. They should be active (not passive)—ask students to synthesize, compare, or troubleshoot scenarios.

**Lesson 27 (sensory evaluation)** is a "teach-back" capstone: students design a tasting protocol, integrating sensory science (Lesson 25), faults (Lesson 24), terroir (Lesson 3), and winemaking technique (Lessons 17-21). Ends with application, not theory.

## Practical Tips

- **Encourage tastings**: If students can access wines, recommend structured comparisons (e.g., unoaked vs. oaked Chardonnay, cool-climate vs. warm-climate Syrah) to make concepts tangible.
- **Use winemaking videos**: UC Davis, Wine Folly, and winery YouTube channels have excellent process footage (crush, punch-down, riddling).
- **Link to current events**: Wildfires (smoke taint), heatwaves (harvest timing), labor shortages (mechanization) make wine science timely and relevant.
- **Watch for chemistry fatigue**: Fermentation and aging chemistry (Lessons 12-16, 23) can feel dense. Break with real-world applications, tastings, or video demonstrations.
