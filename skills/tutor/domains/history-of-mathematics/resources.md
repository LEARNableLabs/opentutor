# History of Mathematics — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Comprehensive References

- **MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive** (University of St Andrews)
  - https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/
  - Most authoritative online resource. Extensive biographies, topic histories, and timelines.
  - Best for: In-depth exploration of any mathematician or mathematical topic.
  - Level: Intermediate to advanced. Well-written and accessible.

- **The Story of Mathematics**
  - https://www.storyofmathematics.com/
  - Chronological narrative from Sumerian mathematics to modern developments.
  - Best for: Overview, chronological context, connections between periods.
  - Level: Beginner to intermediate. Very accessible prose.

- **A History of Mathematics** by Carl B. Boyer and Uta C. Merzbach
  - Standard textbook, comprehensive and scholarly.
  - Best for: Detailed technical content and historical accuracy.
  - Level: Intermediate to advanced. Requires mathematical maturity.

- **Mathematics and Its History** by John Stillwell
  - Integrates historical development with mathematical exposition.
  - Best for: Understanding how historical problems led to modern concepts.
  - Level: Intermediate with some calculus background helpful.

### Online Course Materials

- **MIT OpenCourseWare**
  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mathematics/
  - Several courses touch on mathematical history and development.
  - Best for: Structured academic perspective, problem sets, lecture notes.
  - Level: Advanced undergraduate.

- **Khan Academy — Math History**
  - https://www.khanacademy.org/
  - Scattered historical context throughout math courses.
  - Best for: Visual explanations, historical motivation for concepts.
  - Level: Beginner to intermediate.

### Historical Documents (Translations)

- **Euclid's Elements** (various translations available online)
  - https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/elements.html (David Joyce's interactive version)
  - Best for: Experiencing Greek mathematical reasoning firsthand.
  - Level: Intermediate. Ancient but remarkably clear.

- **Archive.org Historical Mathematics Texts**
  - https://archive.org/
  - Digitized historical works: Newton's Principia, Descartes' La Géométrie, etc.
  - Best for: Reading original sources (in translation or facsimile).
  - Level: Advanced. Requires historical and mathematical sophistication.

### Books (Beyond Primary Texts)

- **"Men of Mathematics" by E.T. Bell**
  - Biographical sketches of major mathematicians. Engaging but historically imprecise.
  - Best for: Storytelling, inspiration. Take historical details with grain of salt.
  - Level: General audience.

- **"Journey Through Genius" by William Dunham**
  - Great theorems from Euclid to Cantor with historical context.
  - Best for: Seeing both the mathematics and the history.
  - Level: Intermediate.

- **"The Equation That Couldn't Be Solved" by Mario Livio**
  - History of symmetry and group theory through biography and mathematics.
  - Best for: Deep dive into algebraic development.
  - Level: Intermediate.

- **"Prime Obsession" by John Derbyshire**
  - History of the Riemann Hypothesis, alternating chapters on mathematics and biography.
  - Best for: Understanding modern unsolved problems and their historical roots.
  - Level: Intermediate to advanced.

## People to Know (Key Historical Figures)

### Ancient
- **Euclid** (~300 BCE) — Elements, axiomatic geometry
- **Archimedes** (~287-212 BCE) — Early calculus ideas, applied mathematics
- **Pythagoras** (~570-495 BCE) — Number mysticism, geometric algebra
- **Aryabhata** (476-550 CE) — Indian astronomy, zero, decimal system
- **Liu Hui** (~225-295 CE) — Chinese mathematics, approximation methods

### Medieval/Islamic
- **Al-Khwarizmi** (~780-850) — Algebra, algorithms
- **Omar Khayyam** (1048-1131) — Cubic equations, poetry
- **Fibonacci** (Leonardo of Pisa, ~1170-1250) — Introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to Europe

### Renaissance/Early Modern
- **Gerolamo Cardano** (1501-1576) — Cubic formula, probability
- **François Viète** (1540-1603) — Symbolic algebra
- **René Descartes** (1596-1650) — Analytic geometry, philosophy
- **Pierre de Fermat** (1607-1665) — Number theory, probability, early calculus

### Scientific Revolution
- **Isaac Newton** (1643-1727) — Calculus, physics
- **Gottfried Leibniz** (1646-1716) — Calculus, logic, notation
- **Leonhard Euler** (1707-1783) — Most prolific mathematician ever, all areas
- **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777-1855) — Number theory, non-Euclidean geometry (unpublished)

### 19th Century
- **Bernhard Riemann** (1826-1866) — Non-Euclidean geometry, complex analysis, hypothesis
- **Georg Cantor** (1845-1918) — Set theory, transfinite numbers
- **Évariste Galois** (1811-1832) — Group theory, died in duel at 20
- **Srinivasa Ramanujan** (1887-1920) — Number theory, self-taught genius

### 20th Century
- **David Hilbert** (1862-1943) — Formalism, Hilbert's problems
- **Kurt Gödel** (1906-1978) — Incompleteness theorems, logic
- **Emmy Noether** (1882-1935) — Abstract algebra, physics
- **Alan Turing** (1912-1954) — Computability, computer science
- **Alexander Grothendieck** (1928-2014) — Algebraic geometry, abstraction

## Tips for Finding Resources

1. **Start with MacTutor** for any specific mathematician or concept — it's the most reliable single source.
2. **Use academic library databases** for peer-reviewed historical scholarship (Historia Mathematica, Archive for History of Exact Sciences).
3. **Check university course websites** — syllabi often link to excellent resources.
4. **Look for translated primary sources** — reading original works (in translation) beats secondary accounts.
5. **Beware popularizations** — they're engaging but often simplify or distort. Verify with scholarly sources.
6. **Explore non-English resources** — Mathematical history is global; don't limit to English-language sources.
