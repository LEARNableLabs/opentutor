# Marine Biology: Deep Ocean Ecosystems — Domain Teaching Config

## Exercise Types
- **Predict-then-reveal (primary move).** State a constraint (e.g. 'You are a fish at 3,000 m and your proteins are being compressed') and have the student predict the solution before it is shown.
- **Data and image interpretation.** Read depth profiles of light, temperature and O2, food-web diagrams, or an ROV still, and say what the organism or habitat tells you.
- **Back-of-envelope estimates.** Use simple numbers: about 1 atm per 10 m of depth, light falling about 10x per 75 m in clear water, 1-3% of surface productivity reaching the abyss. No calculus or formal modelling.
- **Energy-source tracing.** Every lesson ends with 'where does this organism's energy ultimately come from?' (sinking surface material, chemosynthesis, or a mix).
- **Compare and contrast.** Vent vs seep, whale fall vs marine snow, shallow vs deep. Ask the student to name the difference that matters.
- **Debate and position-taking** (Module 6). Mining, trawling and governance, where the student must argue from evidence and acknowledge uncertainty.
- **Teach-back.** Used for synthesis lessons and the capstone.

## Exercise Format Preference
**Mixed.** Use multiple choice for vocabulary, zonation and basic recall (which zone, which energy source). Use free-text reasoning for adaptation, energy and ecosystem-comparison questions, where the student has to show the chain from constraint to solution. Fall back to MC if the student is struggling with a concept, and move to free text once it is solid.

## Resource Types
- **Most valuable:** real ROV and submersible video (MBARI, NOAA, Nautilus, Schmidt Ocean Institute), annotated diagrams and depth profiles, specimen photos, and searchable databases (MBARI Deep-Sea Guide, OBIS, WoRMS).
- **For depth on a claim:** review articles and landmark papers (Haddock 2010, Yancey 2014, Corliss 1979, Cavanaugh 1981, Jamieson 2017, Orr 2005).
- **Books:** Herring and Van Dover for chapter-level backing.
- **Governance lessons:** primary documents from the ISA and the UN BBNJ site.
- Always check that a URL resolves before linking it. Never cite from memory a paper you cannot place.

## Difficulty Curve
- **Module 1 (physical environment):** 2-3. The ideas are simple, but they set up everything else. Make sure the student can state the five constraints: dark, cold, high pressure, scarce food, low oxygen in places.
- **Module 2 (adaptations):** 2-3. Engaging, but easy to turn into a list of weird animals. Insist that each adaptation is tied to a specific constraint.
- **Module 3 (food from above):** 3. Introduces the biological pump, diel vertical migration and isotopes. Isotope-based food-web tracing is the first abstract tool.
- **Module 4 (chemosynthesis):** 3-5, the peak. The hard parts are the redox chemistry (a chemical energy source replacing light) and the symbiosis in *Riftia*. Go slowly, use diagrams, and consolidate before moving on. Gate Module 5 on the student being able to explain chemosynthesis in their own words.
- **Module 5 (seafloor habitats and biodiversity):** 3-4. Integrates earlier modules and introduces the open biodiversity puzzle.
- **Module 6 (humans and the deep):** 3-4. Less new science, more weighing evidence and values.
- **Review lessons:** drop to 1-2 to consolidate.

## Domain Hooks
- Most of the deep sea is unexplored, and many expeditions find new species. Present this as an open frontier.
- Bioluminescence is the dominant form of visual communication on Earth by volume of habitat.
- The 1977 discovery of vents overturned the assumption that all life depends on sunlight.
- Whale falls feed specialised communities for years to decades.
- Hadal amphipods have been found carrying man-made pollutants, so even the deepest trenches are connected to human activity.
- Real live ROV footage and the chance that a student's question has no settled answer yet.
- Applied stakes: seabed mining decisions are being made now with incomplete knowledge.

## Common Failure Modes
- **'The deep sea is an empty desert.'** It is food-limited but diverse. Counter with biodiversity data and the open puzzle of why diversity is so high.
- **'All life depends on the sun.'** Vent and seep ecosystems are the counterexample. Even so, most of the deep sea does depend indirectly on surface production, and vent animals still use oxygen from photosynthesis, so avoid the overcorrection that chemosynthesis is entirely sun-independent.
- **Confusing chemosynthesis with photosynthesis.** The student may treat it as 'photosynthesis without light'. Stress the different energy source (reduced chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide and methane) and the same general idea of fixing carbon.
- **Crushing misconception about pressure.** Students assume deep animals are 'tough shells under pressure'. Many are soft-bodied and have no gas-filled spaces to compress; the problem is mostly at the level of proteins and membranes.
- **Adaptation lists with no reasoning.** Memorising 'anglerfish have lures' without tying it to scarce food and sparse mates.
- **Treating all 'deep' places as the same.** Midwater, abyssal plain, vent and trench are very different habitats.
- **Overstating certainty.** Distinguish what is well established (zonation, the biological pump) from what is uncertain (dispersal between vents, deep-sea species counts, the fish depth limit).
- **One-sided governance views.** Mining and conservation arguments both rest on incomplete data. Have the student state the best argument for the side they disagree with.

## Vocabulary
- **Use freely:** photic zone, aphotic zone, bioluminescence, hydrothermal vent, seamount, trench, plankton, food web, symbiosis, ecosystem.
- **Define carefully at first use:** chemosynthesis, piezolyte, symbiont, stable isotope, biological pump, marine snow, diel vertical migration, redox (oxidation vs reduction, at a simple level), bathyal, abyssal, hadal, seep, whale fall, oxygen minimum zone.
- **Use sparingly or avoid:** detailed taxonomic names beyond the key examples (*Riftia*, *Bathymodiolus* and a few others), advanced biochemical pathway names, oceanographic equations, and fisheries stock-assessment jargon.
- Prefer the common name first, then the Latin name when it adds precision.
