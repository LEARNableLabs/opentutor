# Freerunning and Parkour — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Parkour biomechanics requires bridging abstract physics with visceral physical experience. At the intermediate level, students typically have movement experience but lack the analytical framework to optimize technique or understand injury mechanisms. The key pedagogical challenge is making invisible forces visible — use video analysis, slow-motion breakdowns, and force-time graphs to connect felt sensation with biomechanical principles. This topic is uniquely suited to real-world application: every concept can be immediately tested through movement. Encourage students to film themselves, analyze their technique, and iterate.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Soft landings are always safer"** — Students conflate "soft" with "safe," but overly compliant landings increase joint travel and time under load. Reality: optimal landing uses controlled stiffness appropriate to drop height and terrain. Teach force-time curve manipulation instead of binary soft/hard thinking.

2. **"Rolling dissipates all the force"** — Rolls are incredibly effective but don't eliminate impact — they redistribute it across time and body surface area. Students need to understand that roll entry still requires proper landing mechanics. Bad landing → roll = injury. Good landing → roll = optimal.

3. **"More muscle = better parkour performance"** — Absolute strength has diminishing returns; relative strength (strength-to-weight ratio) and reactive strength matter more. Excessive muscle mass increases impact forces and energy cost. Emphasize functional strength and power-to-weight optimization.

4. **"You just need strong legs for parkour"** — Upper body force transmission is critical for vaults, climbs, and catch-ups. Students underestimate the importance of shoulder girdle strength, grip strength, and core stability. Teach whole-body force management.

5. **"Impact is bad, avoid it"** — Impact is unavoidable and necessary for adaptation. The goal isn't elimination but intelligent management and progressive exposure. Teach the difference between acute overload (injury) and progressive loading (adaptation).

6. **"Good technique eliminates injury risk"** — Technique reduces risk but doesn't eliminate it. Fatigue, tissue adaptation lag, and environmental factors all contribute. Teach multi-layered injury prevention: technique + strength + recovery + environmental awareness.

7. **"Flow means going fast"** — Flow is about efficiency, not speed. Students often conflate smooth transitions with high velocity. Reality: optimal flow minimizes unnecessary acceleration/deceleration. Teach economy of motion and momentum management.

8. **"Plyometric training = jumping a lot"** — Random jumping creates fatigue without adaptation. Effective plyometrics require specific volume, intensity, and recovery parameters. Teach structured progression and the stretch-shortening cycle mechanism.

9. **"If it doesn't hurt, it's working"** — Parkour's tolerance-building culture can lead to ignoring warning signs. Teach the difference between productive discomfort (muscle fatigue, challenge) and warning signs (joint pain, sharp pain, asymmetry).

10. **"You can learn everything from YouTube tutorials"** — Video provides technique models but can't give personalized feedback on force production quality, joint loading patterns, or subtle compensations. Emphasize the value of coaching, video self-analysis, and movement assessment.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner Level (if adapted down)
- Focus on qualitative understanding over quantitative analysis
- Use more analogies and demonstrations, fewer force diagrams
- Emphasize basic landing technique and movement patterns before biomechanical optimization
- Introduce one force concept at a time with extensive practice examples
- Skip advanced topics like stretch-shortening cycle mechanics and periodization theory

### Intermediate Level (current)
- Balance theoretical understanding with practical application
- Introduce quantitative concepts (force-time curves, power calculations) but focus on interpretation rather than derivation
- Students should be able to analyze technique videos and identify biomechanical inefficiencies
- Emphasize injury prevention through biomechanical understanding
- Integrate strength training principles with movement skill development

### Advanced Level (if adapted up)
- Include research papers and biomechanical studies
- Quantitative analysis: calculate forces, power output, energy cost
- Advanced topics: 3D motion analysis, muscle-tendon unit mechanics, computational modeling
- Focus on performance optimization and coaching applications
- Explore cutting-edge training methodologies and research gaps

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

- **Parkour and evolutionary biomechanics** — How modern parkour mirrors ancestral movement patterns (persistence hunting, arboreal locomotion). Drop this in during movement efficiency discussion. Connection to barefoot running mechanics and natural movement.

- **Parkour as controlled falling** — Frame parkour as continuous gravitational potential energy management. Every movement is a negotiation with gravity. Connects to orbital mechanics, roller coaster design, and cliff diving physics.

- **The cat-righting reflex and aerial awareness** — Biomechanics of how cats (and trained humans) reorient mid-air without external forces. Angular momentum conservation in freerunning flips. Connects to space station rotations and gymnastics.

- **Parkour in microgravity** — Thought experiment: how would parkour work on the Moon or Mars? Forces scale with gravity, but mass (inertia) doesn't. Reveals deep understanding of force vs mass. Connection to astronaut training.

- **Fascia and movement efficiency** — Recent research on fascial tissue as elastic energy storage system. Kangaroo locomotion as extreme example. Implications for parkour warm-up, flexibility training, and injury prevention.

- **Flow state and biomechanical automaticity** — When movements become automatic, conscious attention can focus on strategy/creativity. Connects neuroscience (motor learning, basal ganglia) with biomechanics. Relates to deliberate practice theory.

- **Parkour pedagogy and constraints-led learning** — How environmental constraints shape movement solutions. Ecological dynamics approach to coaching. Connects to affordance theory (James Gibson) and motor learning research.

- **Injury biomechanics and forensic analysis** — How biomechanists reconstruct injury mechanisms from damage patterns. Connects to crash testing, sports medicine, and protective equipment design.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

**Weeks 1-2 (Lessons 1-5)**: Establish foundational physics and biomechanics vocabulary. Keep difficulty moderate (2-3) to build confidence. Use lots of video examples and qualitative analysis.

**Weeks 2-3 (Lessons 6-11)**: Landing mechanics is the most critical module for safety — increase difficulty to 3-4 but provide extensive resources and review. This is where injury prevention really matters.

**Weeks 3-4 (Lessons 12-16)**: Vaulting biomechanics can go deep quickly. Balance technical detail with practical application. Use video analysis assignments to connect theory to practice.

**Week 4 (Lessons 17-19)**: Movement efficiency is conceptually accessible but application is subtle. Lower difficulty slightly to give students mental space to integrate earlier concepts.

**Weeks 5-6 (Lessons 20-25)**: Strength, conditioning, and training methodology require synthesis of all previous concepts. Difficulty peaks here (4) as students design complete training programs.

**Week 6 (Lesson 26)**: Final review and integration. Drop difficulty to consolidate learning and celebrate progress.

## Assessment Strategies

- **Video analysis assignments**: Student films a movement, analyzes forces, identifies inefficiencies
- **Teach-back challenges**: Explain a concept to a training partner and correct their technique
- **Training program design**: Create a periodized program for a specific goal (demonstrates synthesis)
- **Technique comparison**: Compare elite vs intermediate athlete performing same movement, explain differences biomechanically
- **Case studies**: Analyze injury mechanisms and propose prevention strategies
