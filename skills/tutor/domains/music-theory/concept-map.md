# Music Theory — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Functional Harmony** — tonic, dominant, and subdominant roles create tonal gravity
2. **Melodic Motion** — conjunct vs disjunct motion, contour, and singability
3. **Seventh Chords** — added dissonance creates tension and tendency tones. Depends on: Functional Harmony
4. **Harmonization** — selecting chords to support a melody. Depends on: Functional Harmony, Melodic Motion
5. **Primary Triads** — I, IV, V as the foundation of tonal harmony. Depends on: Functional Harmony
6. **Pre-dominant Function** — ii, iii, vi chords and their roles. Depends on: Primary Triads, Functional Harmony
7. **Cadences** — harmonic punctuation and phrase endings. Depends on: Primary Triads, Pre-dominant Function
8. **Chord Inversions** — bass note changes without altering function. Depends on: Primary Triads
9. **Non-Chord Tones** — passing tones, neighbor tones, suspensions embellish harmony. Depends on: Seventh Chords, Melodic Motion
10. **Parallel Motion** — why parallel fifths and octaves are avoided. Depends on: Chord Inversions
11. **Voice Ranges** — SATB spacing, doubling, and tessitura. Depends on: Harmonization
12. **Contrary Motion** — smooth voice leading through opposite direction movement. Depends on: Parallel Motion, Voice Ranges
13. **Chorale Style** — four-part writing in the Bach tradition. Depends on: Contrary Motion, Non-Chord Tones, Cadences
14. **Species Counterpoint** — systematic study of linear independence. Depends on: Melodic Motion, Contrary Motion
15. **First Species** — note-against-note consonant counterpoint. Depends on: Species Counterpoint
16. **Second Species** — two-against-one with passing dissonances. Depends on: First Species, Non-Chord Tones
17. **Imitation** — subject-answer relationship in contrapuntal texture. Depends on: Second Species
18. **Secondary Dominants** — tonicization through borrowed V/X chords. Depends on: Functional Harmony, Chorale Style
19. **Modal Mixture** — borrowing chords from parallel major/minor. Depends on: Secondary Dominants
20. **Altered Chords** — Neapolitan sixth and augmented sixth chords. Depends on: Modal Mixture
21. **Modulation** — pivot chords and key changes. Depends on: Secondary Dominants, Modal Mixture
22. **Phrase Structure** — antecedent-consequent pairs and periodicity. Depends on: Cadences
23. **Binary Form** — AB structure with tonal plan. Depends on: Phrase Structure, Modulation
24. **Ternary Form** — ABA with contrasting middle section. Depends on: Binary Form
25. **Sonata Form** — exposition-development-recapitulation architecture. Depends on: Ternary Form, Modulation, Thematic Development

## Dependencies

### Critical Foundation Dependencies
- **Functional Harmony** is the bedrock — everything builds from understanding tonic-dominant relationships
- **Melodic Motion** informs both harmony (voice leading) and counterpoint (linear independence)
- **Cadences** are essential before tackling voice leading because they establish harmonic goals
- **Non-Chord Tones** bridge the gap between strict harmony and real musical practice

### Voice Leading Dependencies
- **Parallel Motion** rules require understanding chord inversions (to recognize intervals between voices)
- **Contrary Motion** effectiveness depends on grasping why parallel motion is problematic
- **Chorale Style** synthesizes all voice leading principles plus cadential knowledge

### Counterpoint Dependencies
- **Species Counterpoint** requires solid melodic motion understanding (intervals, contour)
- **Second Species** builds on first species AND non-chord tone concepts (dissonance treatment)
- **Imitation** requires mastery of earlier species and melodic design

### Chromatic Harmony Dependencies
- **Secondary Dominants** require solid diatonic harmony foundation — can't tonicize without knowing what makes something tonic
- **Modal Mixture** builds on secondary dominants (both involve harmonic borrowing)
- **Modulation** synthesizes secondary dominants, modal mixture, and phrase structure

### Form Dependencies
- **Phrase Structure** requires cadential knowledge (phrases end with cadences)
- **Binary Form** needs phrase structure plus modulation (key areas define sections)
- **Sonata Form** is the culmination — requires all previous formal knowledge plus thematic development

## Bottleneck Concepts

### 1. Voice Leading Principles (Lessons 11-14)
**Why it's a bottleneck:** Students must internalize multiple competing rules (avoid parallels, maintain ranges, move smoothly, double correctly) simultaneously. This is the first truly multi-dimensional challenge.

**Unlock strategy:** Lots of simple exercises. Start with just soprano-bass framework, add one inner voice at a time. Use software (MuseScore) to hear results immediately.

### 2. Species Counterpoint (Lessons 16-19)
**Why it's a bottleneck:** Requires thinking linearly (melodic independence) while maintaining vertical control (consonance/dissonance). Students used to chord-first thinking struggle.

**Unlock strategy:** Emphasize that counterpoint is melody + melody, not chord decomposition. Sing both lines. Focus on voice independence before worrying about implied harmony.

### 3. Secondary Dominants (Lesson 20)
**Why it's a bottleneck:** First chromatic harmony concept. Requires mental shift from "these are the notes in the key" to "we're temporarily borrowing from other keys."

**Unlock strategy:** Start with listening — secondary dominants sound inevitable once you hear them. Analyze before composing. Use circle of fifths visualization.

### 4. Sonata Form (Lesson 28)
**Why it's a bottleneck:** Most complex formal structure, requires tracking multiple parameters (themes, keys, sections) across extended time spans.

**Unlock strategy:** Listen to actual sonata movements multiple times. Map out sections visually (timeline diagrams). Start with very clear examples (early Mozart).

## Mind-Blowing Moments

### The Deceptive Cadence Revelation (Lesson 7)
When students realize that V-vi creates surprise through harmonic misdirection, they suddenly understand that harmony isn't just vertical stacking — it's about managing expectation and delivering (or denying) resolution. This is the first glimpse of harmony as narrative.

### Parallel Fifths Actually Sound Bad (Lesson 11)
Students often think the rule against parallel fifths is arbitrary until they **hear** them in context. The moment they recognize the hollow, organum-like sound and understand why Renaissance/Baroque composers avoided it is transformative — theory becomes audible.

### Species Counterpoint = Musical Yoga (Lesson 16)
The realization that species counterpoint isn't how people compose, but rather a systematic training method (like scales for technique) liberates students. It's not "the rules of music" but rather "exercises to build linear thinking." This reframes difficulty as purposeful challenge.

### Secondary Dominants Are Everywhere (Lesson 20)
Once students learn secondary dominants, they hear them in every pop song, film score, and jazz standard. The "I can't unhear this now" moment where chromatic harmony shifts from theoretical concept to omnipresent musical device.

### Sonata Form = Musical Drama (Lesson 28)
The recognition that sonata form is storytelling — exposition (introduce characters), development (conflict), recapitulation (resolution) — makes classical music emotionally comprehensible. Structure becomes dramaturgy.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Harmony = chord progressions"
Reality: Harmony is the vertical dimension of music, but it's inseparable from voice leading (how individual lines move). Good harmony requires thinking about both simultaneity and succession.

### "Counterpoint is just old music rules"
Reality: Counterpoint is linear thinking — melodic independence. It's fundamental to jazz improvisation, film scoring, and modern composition. Species counterpoint is a training method, not a style requirement.

### "Theory dictates composition"
Reality: Theory describes practice. Composers don't consult textbooks while writing; theory codifies patterns found in successful music. Understanding theory expands options, not constrains creativity.

### "Parallel fifths are always wrong"
Reality: Parallel fifths are avoided in common-practice voice leading because they compromise voice independence. But they're perfectly fine in medieval organum, Impressionist music, rock power chords, etc. Context matters.

### "Every piece fits neatly into a form category"
Reality: Binary, ternary, rondo, sonata are models, not straitjackets. Real pieces often blend or modify formal archetypes. Form analysis is descriptive, not prescriptive.

### "Chromatic harmony means using all 12 notes"
Reality: Chromatic harmony means using notes outside the diatonic scale for harmonic purpose (tonicization, color, modulation). It's about function, not total pitch content.

### "You need to learn harmony before counterpoint"
Reality: Both are intertwined. Vertical (harmonic) and horizontal (contrapuntal) thinking develop together. Historical pedagogy separated them artificially — real music synthesizes both.

### "Roman numeral analysis tells you what the composer was thinking"
Reality: Roman numerals are analytical tools, not compositional blueprints. They describe functional relationships retrospectively. Composers think in sounds, not labels.

### "Functional harmony only applies to classical music"
Reality: Functional harmony (I-IV-V-I) underpins Western popular music, folk, jazz standards, musical theater, and film music. It's not a classical-only phenomenon.

### "If you follow all the voice leading rules, it will sound good"
Reality: Rules prevent common problems but don't guarantee musicality. Effective voice leading requires musical judgment about when to prioritize smoothness vs drama, when to break "rules" for expressive effect.

## Prerequisite Topics

### Essential Prerequisites
- **Music Reading** — required for understanding notation in examples and exercises
- **Intervals** — foundation for voice leading rules and species counterpoint
- **Major and Minor Scales** — needed for functional harmony and Roman numeral analysis
- **Basic Triads** — must recognize chord quality before studying progressions

### Helpful but Not Essential
- **Keyboard Facility** — makes it easier to test harmonizations and hear voice leading
- **Basic Ear Training** — helps internalize harmonic and melodic concepts
- **Performance Experience** — provides musical context for theoretical concepts
- **Music History** — gives cultural context for style periods (Baroque, Classical, Romantic)

## Learning Sequence Rationale

The curriculum follows a **spiral pedagogy**: harmony → voice leading → counterpoint → chromatic harmony → form. Each phase revisits earlier concepts at higher complexity.

**Phase 1 (Lessons 1-10): Diatonic Foundation**
Build functional understanding of how chords work in a key. Culminates in review before tackling voice leading.

**Phase 2 (Lessons 11-15): Voice Leading Mastery**
Apply harmonic knowledge to four-part writing. This is harder than diatonic harmony because it adds linear (melodic) constraints to vertical (harmonic) thinking.

**Phase 3 (Lessons 16-19): Counterpoint Introduction**
Shift from chord-first to melody-first thinking. Species counterpoint provides systematic training in linear independence.

**Phase 4 (Lessons 20-24): Chromatic Expansion**
Add chromatic harmony on top of diatonic foundation. Secondary dominants, modal mixture, and modulation expand tonal palette. Review at lesson 23 before tackling form.

**Phase 5 (Lessons 25-29): Form Analysis**
Synthesize all previous knowledge to understand large-scale structure. Form makes sense only after grasping harmony, voice leading, and modulation.

This sequence ensures each new concept builds on solid foundation rather than introducing too many variables simultaneously.
