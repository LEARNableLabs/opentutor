# Music Theory — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Music theory at the intermediate level requires **bimodal thinking**: vertical (harmonic) and horizontal (melodic) simultaneously. Unlike pure math where concepts cascade logically, music theory is inherently multimodal — students must coordinate sound, notation, analysis, and composition. The most effective pedagogy interleaves **analysis** (understanding existing music) and **composition** (creating within constraints). Always connect theoretical concepts to audible results — use software (MuseScore, MusicTheory.net) to let students **hear** what they write. Theory becomes real when it's audible.

This curriculum emphasizes **questions over definitions**: "Why do parallel fifths sound hollow?" rather than "Here's the rule about parallel fifths." Music theory explains patterns found in successful music; it's descriptive, not prescriptive. Students should understand **why** these patterns work, not just memorize rules.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Theory constrains creativity"
**Why students think this:** They encounter voice leading "rules" and counterpoint "restrictions" and assume theory limits compositional freedom.

**Reality:** Theory describes what skilled composers intuitively do. Understanding why certain voice leadings sound smooth or why secondary dominants create tension **expands** options by making unconscious patterns conscious. Jazz musicians with strong theory knowledge improvise more freely, not less.

**How to correct:** Emphasize that every "rule" has a reason (usually voice independence or tendency tone resolution) and show examples where breaking the rule serves expressive purposes. Theory is a toolkit, not a straitjacket.

### 2. "Harmony = vertical stacks of notes"
**Why students think this:** Initial exposure to triads and seventh chords emphasizes vertical construction.

**Reality:** Harmony is inseparable from voice leading — the **motion between** chords matters as much as the chords themselves. Good progressions arise from smooth voice leading, not just functional logic.

**How to correct:** From lesson 11 onward, never analyze a progression without considering voice leading. Show how the same progression (I-IV-V-I) sounds different with different voice leading.

### 3. "Counterpoint is irrelevant to modern music"
**Why students think this:** Species counterpoint feels archaic with its strict rules about cantus firmus and note-against-note writing.

**Reality:** Linear thinking (melodic independence) is fundamental to all multi-voice music. Film composers, jazz arrangers, and pop producers all use contrapuntal techniques even if they don't call it "counterpoint."

**How to correct:** Show contemporary examples — film scores with competing melodic lines, jazz bass walks against horn melodies, even pop vocal harmonies. Species counterpoint is training wheels for linear thinking.

### 4. "Roman numerals are universal"
**Why students think this:** Analytical systems appear objective and absolute.

**Reality:** Roman numeral analysis reflects functional tonal harmony (circa 1650-1900). It's less useful for modal music, Impressionism, post-tonal music, or non-Western traditions. It's one analytical lens, not the only one.

**How to correct:** Acknowledge the scope of applicability. Roman numerals are powerful for common-practice tonal music but not everything. Mention alternative systems (Schenkerian, neo-Riemannian) to show that analysis is tool selection.

### 5. "All music follows these forms"
**Why students think this:** Binary, ternary, rondo, sonata are taught as categories.

**Reality:** Forms are models derived from common practices, not blueprints composers must follow. Real pieces often blur, combine, or invent formal structures.

**How to correct:** When analyzing form, emphasize "this piece **resembles** sonata form with modifications" rather than "this **is** sonata form." Teach form as flexible framework, not rigid template.

### 6. "Secondary dominants mean the music changed key"
**Why students think this:** Chromatic notes suggest key change.

**Reality:** Tonicization (temporary emphasis on a chord) differs from modulation (actual key change). Secondary dominants create brief tonal detours without establishing a new tonic.

**How to correct:** Use listening examples. Play a progression with a secondary dominant vs actual modulation. The difference is audible — tonicization feels like a color note, modulation feels like a new home base.

### 7. "Voice leading rules are arbitrary"
**Why students think this:** Rules about parallel fifths, doubling, spacing seem like arbitrary restrictions.

**Reality:** Voice leading principles evolved to maintain voice independence and smooth melodic lines in SATB choral writing. They're not universal laws but conventions for a specific texture.

**How to correct:** Play examples that violate the rules and explain what's lost (voice independence, clarity). Then show contexts where violations work (parallel fifths in Impressionism). Context determines appropriateness.

### 8. "Non-chord tones are mistakes"
**Why students think this:** If a note doesn't belong to the chord, it must be wrong.

**Reality:** Non-chord tones (passing tones, neighbor tones, suspensions) create melodic interest and rhythmic variety. They're deliberate embellishments, not errors.

**How to correct:** Show how real melodies would be boring if restricted to chord tones. Non-chord tones make music sing. Analyze folk melodies and pop hooks to show ubiquity.

### 9. "You can't analyze music you like"
**Why students think this:** Analysis feels clinical and might "ruin" emotional enjoyment.

**Reality:** Understanding **how** music creates emotional effects deepens appreciation. Knowing **why** a deceptive cadence surprises or how a Neapolitan sixth adds darkness enhances listening, not diminishes it.

**How to correct:** Choose analysis examples from music students already love (film scores, pop ballads, jazz standards). Show how analysis reveals compositional craft without reducing emotional impact.

### 10. "Intermediate theory is just harder rules"
**Why students think this:** Chromatic harmony and counterpoint add complexity.

**Reality:** Intermediate theory shifts from **learning rules** to **understanding principles**. It's less about memorizing and more about developing musical judgment — when to apply which technique for what effect.

**How to correct:** Emphasize critical thinking: "Why would a composer use a Neapolitan sixth here?" rather than "Here's how to spell a Neapolitan sixth." Cultivate musical reasoning, not rote application.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate Level (This Curriculum)
Students at this level have basic music literacy and understand triads, scales, intervals. The curriculum builds from diatonic harmony through chromatic harmony and form, emphasizing **integration** — how harmony, voice leading, counterpoint, and form interrelate.

**Depth of formalism:** Use Roman numerals consistently. Introduce figured bass notation. Expect written exercises (part writing, species counterpoint). Analysis should identify harmonic function, cadences, formal sections.

**What to emphasize:**
- Voice leading (smooth connections, avoid parallels, resolve tendency tones)
- Functional harmony (T-PD-D-T paradigm)
- Contrapuntal independence (melody + melody, not chord decomposition)
- Form as harmonic trajectory (how key areas define sections)

**What to de-emphasize:**
- Advanced chromatic harmony (augmented sixth chords get brief mention, not extensive drill)
- Figured bass realization (introduce but don't require fluency)
- Advanced form (sonata-rondo, theme and variations — touch on but don't exhaustively catalog)

### How This Differs from Beginner Level
Beginners focus on identification and terminology — naming intervals, spelling triads, recognizing scales. Intermediate students **apply** concepts compositionally and analytically. They write chorale phrases, compose counterpoint exercises, analyze harmonic progressions in real music.

Beginner: "What is a V7 chord?"
Intermediate: "How does V7 create tension that demands resolution, and how do you resolve it with good voice leading?"

### How This Differs from Advanced Level
Advanced students tackle post-tonal theory (set theory, serialism), advanced formal analysis (Schenkerian reduction), jazz harmony (upper extensions, chord-scale theory), and 20th-century techniques (modal composition, minimalism). They analyze full movements, not just phrases.

Advanced: "How does Stravinsky's neoclassicism reinterpret tonal functions?" or "Analyze the pitch-class sets in this Webern piece."
Intermediate: "Analyze the harmonic progression and voice leading in this Bach chorale."

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Tangents)

### 1. The Harmonic Series and Consonance
**When to deploy:** Lessons 1-3 (functional harmony, seventh chords)

**The hook:** Why do fifths sound stable and seconds sound dissonant? The harmonic series (overtones naturally present in any pitched sound) explains consonance and dissonance **acoustically**. The first few overtones are octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), fourth (4:3) — the most consonant intervals. Dissonances involve higher, more complex ratios.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Theory isn't arbitrary — it's rooted in physics. The dominant seventh chord arises naturally in the overtone series.

### 2. Equal Temperament vs Just Intonation
**When to deploy:** Lessons 8-9 (inversions, non-chord tones)

**The hook:** Modern keyboards use equal temperament (12 equal semitones), which means every interval except the octave is slightly out of tune compared to pure ratios. Just intonation uses pure ratios but can't modulate freely. This is why older music sounds different on period instruments.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Tuning is a compromise. We sacrificed pure consonance for modulatory freedom. Every key sounds identical on modern pianos because of this "equal tempering."

### 3. The "Amen" Plagal Cadence in Pop Music
**When to deploy:** Lesson 7 (cadences)

**The hook:** The IV-I "plagal" cadence (called "Amen" because of church usage) is everywhere in pop/rock: "Let It Be," "Hey Jude," countless outros. It feels conclusive but softer than V-I. Why? It lacks the leading tone's strong pull.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Pop music inherited church cadences. The I-IV-I loop is omnipresent because it's stable yet non-demanding — perfect for vamps and fadeouts.

### 4. Schenkerian Analysis: Music as Elaborate Embellishment
**When to deploy:** Lessons 28-29 (sonata form, formal analysis)

**The hook:** Heinrich Schenker proposed that all tonal music reduces to a fundamental structure (Ursatz): I-V-I in the bass, 3-2-1 or 5-4-3-2-1 in the upper voice. Everything else is embellishment. Entire symphonies elaborate this basic gesture.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Form isn't just sections — it's nested layers of elaboration. A Mozart sonata is a glorified I-V-I with decades of compositional mastery filling the space between.

### 5. Jazz Harmony: Functional Harmony on Steroids
**When to deploy:** Lessons 20-24 (chromatic harmony, secondary dominants)

**The hook:** Jazz takes functional harmony and supercharges it: substitute dominants (tritone substitution), extended chords (9ths, 11ths, 13ths), altered dominants. A jazz ii-V-I progression can have 7-10 distinct notes across two chords.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Classical chromatic harmony is minimalist compared to jazz. Same functional principles, radically denser harmonic language.

### 6. The Tristan Chord: Tonal Ambiguity as Expressive Device
**When to deploy:** Lesson 22 (Neapolitan sixth, altered chords)

**The hook:** The opening chord of Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde* is famously ambiguous — analyzable as half-diminished seventh, augmented sixth, or suspension. It **delays resolution** for the entire opera (hours of music), creating unprecedented harmonic tension.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Wagner used harmonic ambiguity to represent unfulfilled longing. The Tristan chord shows how theory (ambiguous function) maps to emotion (unresolved desire).

### 7. Counterpoint in Hip-Hop Production
**When to deploy:** Lessons 16-19 (species counterpoint, imitation)

**The hook:** Hip-hop producers layer melodic samples, bass lines, synth hooks, and vocal melodies. The best beats have contrapuntal independence — each element occupies distinct register, rhythm, and timbre. J Dilla's polyrhythmic layering is counterpoint in practice.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Counterpoint isn't dead — it's thriving in DAWs. Linear independence applies across all genres.

### 8. The 12-Bar Blues: Simple Form, Infinite Variation
**When to deploy:** Lessons 25-27 (phrase structure, binary form)

**The hook:** The 12-bar blues is a hyper-simple form (I-I-I-I-IV-IV-I-I-V-IV-I-I) yet encompasses infinite variation. Every blues musician improvises over this framework, from Delta blues to jazz to rock.

**Why it's mind-blowing:** Form as **constraint** enables creativity. The 12-bar structure is tight enough to be immediately recognizable yet flexible enough for endless reinvention.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

The curriculum follows a **stepped ascent** with periodic plateaus (review lessons).

**Lessons 1-4 (Difficulty 1-2):** Accessible entry. Reviewing fundamentals and establishing analytical vocabulary. Even "easy" lessons ask questions to deepen understanding.

**Lessons 5-10 (Difficulty 2-3):** Diatonic harmony. Difficulty rises as concepts accumulate (inversions + non-chord tones + cadences). Review at lesson 10 consolidates before major jump.

**Lessons 11-15 (Difficulty 3-4):** Voice leading is the first **peak**. Difficulty 4 because students must juggle multiple constraints simultaneously. Teach-back lesson (14) ensures mastery before moving on.

**Lessons 16-19 (Difficulty 2-4):** Counterpoint. Builds through species counterpoint with systematic training in linear independence. Teach-back at lesson 19 (difficulty 4) caps the module.

**Lessons 20-24 (Difficulty 4-2-4):** Chromatic harmony. Second **peak**. Secondary dominants, modal mixture, and modulation require mental flexibility (thinking outside diatonic framework). Review at lesson 23 before final push.

**Lessons 25-29 (Difficulty 2-5):** Form. Difficulty builds to lesson 28 (**peak difficulty 5**) for sonata form — most complex concept in the curriculum. Final teach-back (29) at difficulty 4 demonstrates synthesis.

### Pacing Rationale
Review lessons every 5-7 lessons prevent cognitive overload. They're not passive recaps but active consolidation — apply skills learned, integrate concepts, self-assess readiness.

Difficulty 4-5 lessons should feel challenging but not impossible. If a student struggles, split the lesson across two days or add a mini-review before advancing.

## Assessment Strategies

### Formative Assessment (During Learning)
**1. Teach-back lessons (4, 14, 20, 30):** Student explains concept or demonstrates skill. If they can teach it, they understand it.

**2. Error diagnosis:** Present incorrect part writing or counterpoint and ask: "What's wrong here?" Identifying problems demonstrates understanding.

**3. Listening analysis:** Play a progression or cadence. Ask student to identify it by ear before analyzing the score. Theory should be audible.

**4. Comparative analysis:** "Why does this voice leading sound smoother than that one?" Forces critical listening and application of principles.

**5. Composition challenges:** "Write a 4-bar phrase ending with a deceptive cadence." Open-ended creation demonstrates understanding.

### Summative Assessment (Gauging Mastery)
**1. Chorale harmonization:** Given a melody, write a four-part SATB realization with proper voice leading and harmonic choices. Tests harmony + voice leading + musical judgment.

**2. Species counterpoint exercise:** Write first or second species counterpoint against a given cantus firmus. Tests linear thinking and consonance/dissonance treatment.

**3. Harmonic analysis:** Analyze a short excerpt (8-16 bars) from Bach, Mozart, or Chopin. Provide Roman numerals, identify cadences, note non-chord tones, describe form. Tests analytical skills across multiple domains.

**4. Composition project:** Compose a short piece (16-32 bars) in binary or ternary form demonstrating harmonic progression, voice leading, and formal coherence. Tests synthesis of all skills.

### Self-Assessment Prompts
- "Can you explain why this progression works?"
- "Can you hear the difference between a half cadence and a deceptive cadence?"
- "Can you write a simple two-part counterpoint without looking at rules?"
- "Can you identify the formal sections of a piece by listening?"

If students answer "yes" confidently and demonstrate the skill, they're ready to advance. If they hesitate or struggle, revisit the concept with different examples.

## Engagement Strategies

### Connect to Student's Musical Interests
If student loves film music, analyze Williams, Zimmer, or Giacchino. If they're into pop, dissect Beatles or Taylor Swift progressions. Theory becomes relevant when applied to music they care about.

### Use Interactive Tools
MusicTheory.net for exercises, MuseScore for playback, Hooktheory for harmonic analysis of popular songs. Technology makes abstract concepts tangible.

### Encourage Experimentation
"Try writing a progression that **breaks** the voice leading rules. How does it sound? When might that be desirable?" Exploration deepens understanding more than rote compliance.

### Cultivate Historical Context
A brief mention of why Bach wrote 371 chorales (training), why Fux invented species counterpoint (pedagogy), why sonata form dominated the Classical era (balance and drama) makes theory feel like inherited wisdom, not arbitrary rules.

### Celebrate "A-ha!" Moments
When a student hears a deceptive cadence in a pop song and recognizes it, or spots a secondary dominant in a film cue, **celebrate it**. Recognition in the wild proves internalization.
