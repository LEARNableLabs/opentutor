# Ethics — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Normative ethics** — The study of how we should act; prescriptive rather than descriptive
2. **Moral reasoning** — The process of deliberating about right and wrong using principles and evidence
3. **Justification** — Providing reasons that support a moral claim or judgment
4. **Moral frameworks** — Systematic approaches to ethical decision-making (theories)
5. **Ethical theories** — Comprehensive accounts of what makes actions right or wrong
6. **Consequentialism** — Actions are right if they produce good outcomes. Depends on: moral evaluation
7. **Deontology** — Actions are right if they follow moral duties/rules, regardless of outcomes. Depends on: moral evaluation
8. **Utility** — Well-being, happiness, or preference satisfaction; the good to be maximized in utilitarianism. Depends on: consequentialism
9. **Greatest happiness principle** — Actions are right if they maximize total happiness. Depends on: utility
10. **Hedonic calculus** — Bentham's method for calculating pleasure and pain. Depends on: utility, greatest happiness principle
11. **Higher and lower pleasures** — Mill's distinction between intellectual/spiritual and bodily pleasures. Depends on: greatest happiness principle
12. **Act utilitarianism** — Each action should maximize utility. Depends on: greatest happiness principle
13. **Trolley problem** — Thought experiment testing moral intuitions about sacrifice and harm. Depends on: consequentialism, moral reasoning
14. **Moral absolutes** — Some acts are always wrong, regardless of consequences. Depends on: deontology
15. **Categorical imperative** — Kant's test for universal moral laws. Depends on: moral absolutes, universalizability
16. **Universalizability** — A moral rule must be able to apply to everyone. Depends on: deontology
17. **Humanity formula** — Never treat people merely as means to an end. Depends on: categorical imperative
18. **Dignity** — The inherent worth of rational beings. Depends on: humanity formula
19. **Prima facie duties** — Duties that can be overridden by more pressing duties (Ross). Depends on: deontology, conflicting duties
20. **Virtue ethics** — Morality is about character traits rather than acts or consequences. Depends on: moral frameworks
21. **Character** — Settled dispositions to act in certain ways; the focus of virtue ethics. Depends on: virtue ethics
22. **Eudaimonia** — Human flourishing; the ultimate good in Aristotelian ethics. Depends on: virtue ethics
23. **Flourishing** — Living well; achieving excellence as a human being. Depends on: eudaimonia
24. **Doctrine of the mean** — Virtues lie between extremes of excess and deficiency. Depends on: virtue ethics
25. **Practical wisdom (phronesis)** — The ability to discern the right action in particular situations. Depends on: virtue ethics, doctrine of the mean
26. **Moral exemplars** — Virtuous people who serve as models for action. Depends on: virtue ethics, character
27. **Moral education** — How character and virtue are developed through practice. Depends on: character, habituation
28. **Habituation** — Developing virtues through repeated practice. Depends on: moral education
29. **Moral pluralism** — Multiple moral frameworks may be needed; no single theory captures all of ethics. Depends on: ethical theories, comparative analysis
30. **Reflective equilibrium** — Balancing moral principles with intuitions about cases. Depends on: moral reasoning, justification

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **Moral frameworks** require understanding **normative ethics** — we need to know what normative ethics is before we can classify different approaches to it
- **Ethical theories** build on **moral frameworks** — theories are fully developed frameworks with systematic principles
- **Consequentialism and deontology** depend on **moral evaluation** — both need a baseline understanding of how we assess moral worth, even though they differ in what they evaluate

### Utilitarianism Chain
- **Utility** requires **consequentialism** — utility is the specific good that utilitarian consequentialism aims to maximize
- **Greatest happiness principle** depends on **utility** — it's the specific formulation of how utility should be maximized
- **Hedonic calculus** builds on both **utility** and **greatest happiness principle** — it's Bentham's practical method for applying the principle
- **Act utilitarianism** depends on **greatest happiness principle** — it's the version that applies the principle to individual actions
- **Higher and lower pleasures** (Mill) depends on **greatest happiness principle** — it refines what counts as happiness

### Deontology Chain
- **Moral absolutes** requires **deontology** — absolutism is a characteristic feature of duty-based ethics
- **Categorical imperative** depends on both **moral absolutes** and **universalizability** — it's Kant's test for identifying absolute moral duties
- **Universalizability** requires **deontology** — the idea that moral rules must apply universally flows from duty-based thinking
- **Humanity formula** builds on **categorical imperative** — it's the second formulation of Kant's core principle
- **Dignity** depends on **humanity formula** — treating people as ends grounds their dignity
- **Prima facie duties** requires understanding **deontology** and **conflicting duties** — Ross's modification addresses when duties conflict

### Virtue Ethics Chain
- **Character** depends on **virtue ethics** — character is the central focus of virtue approaches
- **Eudaimonia** requires **virtue ethics** — it's the Aristotelian conception of the good life
- **Flourishing** is closely related to **eudaimonia** — they're nearly synonymous concepts
- **Doctrine of the mean** depends on **virtue ethics** — it's Aristotle's method for identifying virtues
- **Practical wisdom** requires both **virtue ethics** and **doctrine of the mean** — phronesis is needed to apply the mean in context
- **Moral exemplars** depend on **virtue ethics** and **character** — exemplars are people with excellent character
- **Moral education** depends on **character** — it's about how character develops
- **Habituation** depends on **moral education** — it's the Aristotelian method of character formation

### Synthesis Dependencies
- **Moral pluralism** requires understanding multiple **ethical theories** — you need to know the theories before assessing whether we need multiple frameworks
- **Reflective equilibrium** depends on **moral reasoning** and **justification** — it's a method for refining our moral judgments

## Critical Bottleneck Concepts

### 1. Consequentialism vs. Deontology (Lesson 3)
This is the first major fork in the road. Students must grasp that these evaluate different things:
- Consequentialism: outcomes/results
- Deontology: intentions/rules/duties

Everything in utilitarianism and Kantian ethics builds on understanding this distinction. If students conflate them, they'll struggle with both theories.

### 2. Categorical Imperative (Lesson 12)
Kant is notoriously difficult. The categorical imperative is the foundation of deontology, but it's abstract and requires understanding:
- Universalizability
- The difference between hypothetical and categorical imperatives
- How to formulate maxims

Many students struggle here. Extra time and examples are essential.

### 3. Eudaimonia (Lesson 18)
Students often misunderstand eudaimonia as "happiness" in the modern sense (feeling good). It's actually about flourishing and living well according to human nature. This misconception undermines virtue ethics if not corrected.

### 4. Practical Wisdom / Phronesis (Lessons 19-20)
This is virtue ethics' answer to action guidance. Students need to understand it's not rule-following or utility calculation, but skilled judgment developed through experience. Without grasping phronesis, virtue ethics seems empty.

## Common Conceptual Confusion Points

### Utilitarianism ≠ Egoism
Students often think utilitarianism means "do whatever makes you happy." It's actually demanding: maximize *total* happiness, which often requires personal sacrifice.

### Deontology ≠ Divine Command
Kant's ethics is based on reason, not God's commands. Students from religious backgrounds may conflate duty with religious obligation.

### Virtue Ethics ≠ Relativism
Just because virtue ethics is character-focused doesn't mean "anything goes." There are objective facts about human flourishing and virtues.

### Consequentialism ≠ Utilitarianism
All utilitarianism is consequentialism, but not all consequentialism is utilitarianism. There are other consequentialist theories (e.g., egoism, prioritarianism).

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic logical reasoning** — needed for understanding argument structure in all three theories
- **Critical thinking** — needed for evaluating objections and comparing frameworks
- **Argument analysis (premises, conclusions)** — needed for working with philosophical arguments, especially Kant's complex arguments

## Optional Extensions (for students who want to go deeper)

- **Meta-ethics** — What does "good" even mean? Moral realism vs. anti-realism
- **Rule utilitarianism** — Follow rules that maximize utility (vs. act utilitarianism)
- **Care ethics** — Feminist alternative emphasizing relationships and care
- **Contractualism** — Morality as what we could all agree to (Scanlon, Rawls)
- **Moral psychology** — How do people actually make moral decisions? (experimental philosophy)
- **Effective altruism** — Modern utilitarian-inspired movement
- **Bioethics** — Applied ethics in medicine and biology
- **Business ethics** — Applied ethics in commerce and organizations
