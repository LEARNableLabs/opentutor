# Ethics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Ethics is best taught **dialectically**: present a theory, test it against moral intuitions and cases, examine objections, then move to the next theory. Each framework should be presented charitably (strongest form) before critique. Use **real-world dilemmas** early and often to motivate theory — abstract principles stick when students see why we need them. At the intermediate level, students can handle **primary texts** (Mill, Kant, Aristotle) with scaffolding, and should learn to construct arguments rather than just summarize positions. Philosophy is about **doing**, not just knowing.

Balance three modes: **(1) Exposition** — explain the theory clearly; **(2) Application** — use cases to test it; **(3) Dialectic** — consider objections and replies. Avoid the common pitfall of teaching ethics as a list of "isms" to memorize. The goal is **moral reasoning skill**, not theory trivia.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Utilitarianism means doing whatever makes you happy"
**Why students believe this**: They conflate "happiness" with personal pleasure and miss the "greatest *total* happiness" component.

**How to correct**: Emphasize that utilitarianism is demanding and often requires personal sacrifice. Use Peter Singer's drowning child argument: if a child is drowning and you can save them by ruining your expensive shoes, you should. Utilitarianism often demands more than common sense morality.

**Key moment**: Lesson 5-6 when introducing utility. Stress "total" or "overall" happiness from the start.

### 2. "Kant says lying is always wrong, no exceptions, even to save a life"
**Why students believe this**: Kant *did* write this in one essay ("On a Supposed Right to Lie"), but it's a controversial application, not the core theory.

**How to correct**: Distinguish between (a) Kant's method (categorical imperative) and (b) his specific applications. The method is the key insight. Many modern Kantians think Kant was wrong about lying. Focus on the *procedure* for testing maxims, not memorizing Kant's own list.

**Key moment**: Lesson 14 (lying dilemma). Frame it as "What would Kant say?" vs. "What *should* a Kantian say?"

### 3. "Virtue ethics doesn't tell you what to do, so it's useless"
**Why students believe this**: Virtue ethics doesn't provide decision procedures like "maximize utility" or "universalize your maxim."

**How to correct**: That's a *feature*, not a bug. Virtue ethics says moral wisdom comes from experience and judgment (phronesis), not algorithms. Ask: "Would you trust a computer program to make moral decisions for you? Or someone with good judgment?" Emphasize moral exemplars: "What would a wise, compassionate person do here?"

**Key moment**: Lesson 20 (action guidance). Acknowledge the objection, then show how phronesis responds.

### 4. "These are just opinions — there's no right answer in ethics"
**Why students believe this**: Moral disagreement seems persistent; people often appeal to "different values."

**How to correct**: Distinguish between *disagreement* and *relativism*. Scientists disagree about dark matter, but that doesn't make physics "just opinions." In ethics, we can give *reasons* for our views and evaluate those reasons. Disagreement shows the questions are hard, not that anything goes.

**Key moment**: Lesson 2 (moral disagreement). Address this head-on early to establish that we're doing *philosophy*, not sharing feelings.

### 5. "Deontology is just religious ethics without God"
**Why students believe this**: Duty-talk sounds like commandments; students may associate moral absolutes with divine commands.

**How to correct**: Kant explicitly grounds morality in *reason*, not revelation. The categorical imperative is supposed to be discoverable by any rational being. Contrast with divine command theory: "Is murder wrong because God forbids it, or does God forbid it because it's wrong?" Kant takes the second option.

**Key moment**: Lesson 11-12 (introducing deontology). Clarify that this is a *secular* moral framework.

### 6. "Eudaimonia just means 'happiness'"
**Why students believe this**: It's often translated as "happiness," and students read it through a modern lens (subjective feeling).

**How to correct**: Eudaimonia is closer to "flourishing" or "living well" — it's *objective* (based on human nature) and *activity-based* (not just feeling good). Aristotle thinks you can *feel* happy while not flourishing (e.g., a drug addict) and vice versa (someone struggling nobly). Use the function argument: a good knife cuts well; a good human being...?

**Key moment**: Lesson 18 (eudaimonia). Spend extra time on this; it's the foundation of virtue ethics.

### 7. "The trolley problem proves utilitarianism is wrong"
**Why students believe this**: Many people have the intuition that you *shouldn't* push the fat man, even though it saves more lives.

**How to correct**: The trolley problem is a *test* of theories, not a refutation. Some utilitarians bite the bullet (yes, push him); others distinguish act vs. rule utilitarianism or appeal to decision-making under uncertainty. More importantly: our intuitions aren't infallible. Maybe the utilitarian answer is right and our intuition is wrong (shaped by evolution, not morality).

**Key moment**: Lesson 9 (trolley problem). Present it as a dilemma that challenges *all* theories, not just utilitarianism.

### 8. "Virtue ethics says different cultures have different virtues, so it's relativist"
**Why students believe this**: Aristotle talks about virtues being learned in a community; different cultures seem to value different traits.

**How to correct**: Aristotle thinks virtues are grounded in human nature, which is universal. While *expression* of virtues may vary by culture (what counts as brave in one context vs. another), the underlying virtues (courage, honesty, compassion) are human universals. Distinguish between *relativism* (no objective moral facts) and *contextualism* (moral principles apply differently in different situations).

**Key moment**: Lesson 17 and 21 (character and education). Emphasize the objectivity of human flourishing.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners (if student is less prepared than expected)
- **Simplify Kant**: Skip the complex formulations of the categorical imperative; focus on the universalizability test with concrete examples.
- **Use more cases, less theory**: Spend more time on trolley problems, real-world dilemmas, and intuition-pumping before introducing abstract principles.
- **Delay primary texts**: Use secondary sources and video summaries instead of reading Mill or Kant directly.
- **Focus on applied ethics**: Climate change, poverty, animal rights — start with concrete issues and work backward to theory.

### For Advanced (if student wants to go deeper)
- **Add meta-ethics**: What does "good" mean? Moral realism vs. expressivism. Read some G.E. Moore or Mackie.
- **Introduce more theories**: Care ethics (Gilligan, Noddings), contractualism (Scanlon, Rawls), moral particularism (Dancy).
- **Primary texts in depth**: Full readings of *Utilitarianism*, *Groundwork*, *Nicomachean Ethics* Book I-II.
- **Engage with contemporary debates**: Effective altruism, population ethics, AI ethics, the ethics of care.
- **Formal argument reconstruction**: Practice putting philosophical arguments into premise-conclusion form and evaluating validity.

### For Intermediate (current level)
- **Balance theory and application**: Each theory gets thorough exposition plus real-world testing.
- **Guided primary text reading**: Assign short passages from Mill, Kant, Aristotle with scaffolding questions.
- **Teach argument analysis**: Students should be able to identify premises, evaluate objections, and construct counterarguments.
- **Comparative analysis**: Lessons 23-26 explicitly compare frameworks and ask which handles cases best.
- **Build toward independence**: Early lessons are more structured; later lessons expect students to apply frameworks themselves.

## Difficulty Progression

### Arc 1: Foundations (Lessons 1-4, Difficulty 1-2)
Ease students into philosophical thinking. Use accessible cases and questions. Build vocabulary (normative ethics, consequentialism, etc.) but keep it concrete.

### Arc 2: Utilitarianism (Lessons 5-10, Difficulty 2-4)
Start accessible (greatest happiness principle is intuitive) but build to challenging questions: measuring happiness across people, total vs. average utility, trolley problem. Peak difficulty at lesson 8 (population ethics). Review at lesson 10.

### Arc 3: Deontology (Lessons 11-16, Difficulty 2-4)
Kant is hard. Lesson 12 (categorical imperative) is the toughest lesson in the curriculum (difficulty 4). Give extra time and examples. Lesson 14 (lying dilemma) is also difficult (conflicting intuitions). Review at lesson 16.

### Arc 4: Virtue Ethics (Lessons 17-22, Difficulty 2-4)
Start accessible (virtue ethics is intuitive after abstract Kant). Build to lesson 20 (action guidance objection, difficulty 4). Overall gentler than deontology. Review at lesson 22.

### Arc 5: Synthesis (Lessons 23-26, Difficulty 3-4)
Apply all three frameworks to hard cases (poverty, climate change). Students must juggle multiple theories and compare them. Cognitively demanding but rewarding. End with integration (lesson 26).

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Detours)

### 1. The Experience Machine (Nozick)
**Connection**: Challenges hedonic utilitarianism. Would you plug into a machine that gives you perfect experiences but isn't real? Most people say no, suggesting we value more than just pleasure.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 7 (Mill's higher pleasures) or lesson 8 (what counts as utility).

### 2. The Trolley Problem Variants
**Connection**: There are dozens of versions (loop track, fat man, transplant surgeon, etc.). They tease apart different moral intuitions.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 9, but only if students are engaged. Can easily eat up a whole session.

### 3. Moral Luck (Bernard Williams, Thomas Nagel)
**Connection**: Should we blame someone for a bad outcome if it was due to luck (e.g., drunk driver who hits someone vs. drunk driver who gets home safely)? Challenges both consequentialism and deontology.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 14 or 24, when students are thinking about intention vs. outcome.

### 4. The Repugnant Conclusion (Derek Parfit)
**Connection**: If we maximize *total* utility, a world with billions of people living barely-worth-living lives has more total utility than a world with fewer people living great lives. Absurd?

**When to deploy**: Lesson 8 (total vs. average utility). Very thought-provoking for advanced students.

### 5. Effective Altruism
**Connection**: Modern utilitarian-inspired movement (Peter Singer, Will MacAskill) focused on using evidence and reason to do the most good. Covers global poverty, animal welfare, existential risk.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 23 (global poverty) or lesson 26 (building your framework). Great for students interested in practical ethics.

### 6. The Doctrine of Double Effect
**Connection**: Catholic moral theology principle: it's permissible to cause harm as a *side effect* of a good action, but not as a *means*. Explains intuitions about trolley problem variants.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 9 or 14, if students are puzzled by the difference between switching the trolley and pushing the fat man.

### 7. Moral Particularism (Jonathan Dancy)
**Connection**: Challenges the idea that ethics needs general principles. Maybe each situation is unique and requires judgment, not rules.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 24 (pluralism vs. monism), if students are skeptical about grand theories.

### 8. Stoic Ethics
**Connection**: Ancient Greek school (Epictetus, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius) combining virtue ethics with focus on what's in your control. Having a resurgence in popular culture.

**When to deploy**: Lesson 21 or 22, as a variant of virtue ethics. Great for students interested in practical philosophy.

## Teaching Strategy by Lesson Type

### Mini-lessons (primary mode)
**Format**: Short exposition (2-3 paragraphs) → question for reflection → resource → follow-up.

**Example** (Lesson 12, Kant): "Kant says we need a test for moral rules. Here's the categorical imperative... Now think: if everyone lied when convenient, could lying work? No — no one would trust anyone. So lying fails the test. Read more: [link]. Now you try: is promise-breaking permissible?"

### Questions (Socratic mode)
**Format**: Pose dilemma → student responds → probe their reasoning → introduce theory → revisit dilemma.

**Example** (Lesson 1): "Why is stealing wrong?" Student: "Because it hurts people." Tutor: "What if stealing from a rich person helps poor people more?" Push them to articulate a principle, then introduce normative ethics.

### Real-world (application mode)
**Format**: Present actual case → ask student to analyze → apply theories → compare frameworks.

**Example** (Lesson 25, climate ethics): "Should wealthy countries pay for climate damage? What would a utilitarian say? A Kantian? An Aristotelian?" Force engagement with theory in context.

### Teach-back (assessment mode)
**Format**: Ask student to explain a concept or theory in their own words, then apply it to a new case.

**Example** (Lesson 15, prima facie duties): "Explain Ross's view in your own words. Now: you promised to meet a friend, but you see someone having a medical emergency. What does Ross say?"

### Resource-drop (exploration mode)
**Format**: Provide curated resources (video, article, interactive) and ask student to explore, then discuss.

**Example** (Lesson 26): "Here are three resources on building an ethical framework. Explore one and share what resonates with you."

### Review (consolidation mode)
**Format**: Revisit key concepts, quiz understanding, correct misconceptions, ask student to compare/contrast.

**Example** (Lesson 10, utilitarianism review): "What's the greatest happiness principle? How does Mill's version differ from Bentham's? What's the strongest objection you've seen?"

## Red Flags (Signs Student Needs Extra Support)

1. **Conflating normative and descriptive claims** (e.g., "people do X" → "people should do X"). Revisit the is/ought distinction.
2. **Treating all views as equally valid** ("it's just your opinion"). Reinforce that we're evaluating *arguments*, not feelings.
3. **Reducing theories to slogans** (e.g., "utilitarianism = greatest good"). Push for deeper understanding.
4. **Struggling with Kant** (very common). Slow down, use more examples, maybe skip technical formulations.
5. **Confusing virtue ethics with relativism**. Emphasize objective human flourishing.
6. **Difficulty with abstract reasoning**. Use more concrete cases and examples.
7. **Disengagement** ("this doesn't matter"). Connect to real-world issues they care about.

## Success Indicators

1. **Student asks "What would a utilitarian/Kantian/Aristotelian say about X?"** — they're internalizing the frameworks.
2. **Student identifies tensions between theories** — they're thinking comparatively.
3. **Student challenges the theories** ("But what if...") — they're engaging critically, not just absorbing.
4. **Student applies concepts to new cases without prompting** — transfer is happening.
5. **Student reads primary texts and asks clarifying questions** — they're engaging with the material at depth.
6. **Student references earlier lessons** ("This is like that trolley problem from lesson 9") — they're building a coherent knowledge structure.
