# Macroeconomics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Macroeconomics at the intermediate level bridges intuition and formalism. The core challenge is helping students develop **dual vision**: seeing both the forest (aggregate economy-wide patterns) and the trees (microeconomic foundations of individual behavior). Teaching should emphasize **real-world relevance** — students should constantly connect models to current economic events, policy debates, and data. Use FRED graphs liberally to ground abstract concepts in observable patterns. The field's structure — long run vs. short run, real vs. nominal, closed vs. open economy — provides natural scaffolding. Build intuition first with graphs and verbal logic, then introduce algebra only when it clarifies rather than obscures.

At this level, students should graduate from "policy X causes Y" to "policy X causes Y *under these conditions*, but may cause Z when conditions differ." The goal is conditional reasoning and model-selection judgment, not memorizing cause-and-effect chains.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. GDP equals welfare or happiness
**Why:** Students conflate "measuring economic activity" with "measuring what matters"
**Correction:** Explicitly list what GDP misses (inequality, leisure, environment, home production). Show cases where GDP rises but welfare plausibly falls (natural disaster cleanup, crime prevention spending).

### 2. Government budget is like household budget
**Why:** Personal finance intuition dominates; students don't grasp government's unique ability to tax, print money, and roll over debt indefinitely
**Correction:** Highlight key differences — households can't tax or print money, have finite lifespans, face borrowing constraints. Emphasize sustainability (debt/GDP ratio) not absolute levels.

### 3. Inflation is always caused by money printing
**Why:** Quantity theory is taught early and sticks; students ignore cost-push, imported inflation, wage-price spirals
**Correction:** Distinguish money-driven inflation (Zimbabwe, Weimar) from supply shocks (1970s oil) and demand-pull (overheating). Emphasize that quantity theory holds *in the long run* but short-run inflation has multiple sources.

### 4. Unemployment results from laziness or unwillingness to work
**Why:** Just-world bias and moral framing of labor markets
**Correction:** Explain frictional unemployment (job search takes time), structural unemployment (skills/location mismatch), and cyclical unemployment (deficient demand). Use recessions to show unemployment spikes when nothing about worker preferences changed.

### 5. Trade deficits mean "losing" to other countries
**Why:** Mercantilism is intuitive; accounting identity (trade deficit = capital inflow) is not
**Correction:** Frame trade deficit as "other countries are investing in us" (capital inflow). Show that US has run trade deficits during periods of high growth. Use saving-investment identity: CA = S - I.

### 6. Central banks directly control the money supply
**Why:** Textbooks often present this simplification for pedagogical ease
**Correction:** Explain that modern central banks target interest rates and let money supply adjust endogenously. Reserves → loans → deposits process is demand-driven.

### 7. Higher savings always increases growth
**Why:** Solow model shows higher saving rate → higher steady state
**Correction:** Introduce golden rule capital stock (over-saving reduces consumption). For advanced students, discuss paradox of thrift in Keynesian short run.

### 8. Sticky prices mean prices never change
**Why:** "Sticky" sounds absolute rather than relative
**Correction:** Clarify that sticky means "slow to adjust" not "fixed." Show menu costs, wage contracts, and other sources of adjustment friction. Prices are sticky relative to the speed at which demand/supply conditions change.

### 9. Real and nominal GDP differ only by "a number" (the deflator)
**Why:** Deflator algebra obscures the conceptual point
**Correction:** Emphasize real GDP measures *quantities* produced, nominal measures *dollar value*. The deflator adjusts for changing prices. Use examples: same number of cars but higher prices → nominal rises, real flat.

### 10. Recessions happen randomly or are unpredictable
**Why:** News coverage focuses on "surprise" downturns
**Correction:** Discuss leading indicators (yield curve, consumer confidence, housing starts). Explain that recessions often follow identifiable shocks (oil price spikes, financial crises, tight monetary policy). Forecasting is hard but not random.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students (target level)

- **Formalism:** Use graphs extensively (AS-AD, Solow diagrams, Phillips curve, IS-LM if desired). Light algebra — derive key results (multiplier, steady state) but don't dwell on calculus-based optimization.
- **Depth:** Cover microfoundations conceptually (why consumption smoothing, why sticky prices) without full dynamic optimization.
- **Breadth:** Survey all major topics (growth, cycles, unemployment, inflation, policy, open economy) but don't go deep into every model variant.
- **Empirics:** Show stylized facts and use FRED data to motivate and validate models. Students should learn to interpret data and graphs critically.
- **Policy:** Emphasize real-world policy debates (inflation targeting, fiscal stimulus, exchange rate regimes). Encourage evaluating trade-offs rather than seeking "right answers."

### Compared to Introductory Level

- **More:** Formal models (Solow, AS-AD), distinction between short-run and long-run, expectations, policy mechanisms
- **Less:** Storytelling without models, simplistic cause-effect (e.g., "deficits always bad")
- **New:** Growth accounting, natural rate of unemployment, Phillips curve, Ricardian equivalence, impossible trinity

### Compared to Graduate Level

- **Less:** Dynamic optimization, microfounded DSGE models, econometric identification, full GE treatment, advanced New Keynesian models
- **More:** Intuition-building, broad survey of topics, policy applications, real-world data interpretation
- **Same:** Core models (Solow, AS-AD, Phillips curve, Mundell-Fleming) appear at both levels but with different rigor

## Rabbit Holes

### When to Drop Them In

- **Secular stagnation** (lesson 8, after endogenous growth) — Why have advanced economies seen slowing productivity growth? Connect to current debates about innovation, aging, and demand deficiency.

- **Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)** (lesson 24, government debt) — Provocative heterodox view that deficits don't matter for currency issuers. Use to sharpen students' understanding of budget constraints and inflation risks.

- **Cryptocurrency and digital currencies** (lesson 19, what is money) — Bitcoin, central bank digital currencies. Challenges traditional banking and monetary policy transmission.

- **Climate change and green growth** (lesson 8, endogenous growth) — Can we decouple growth from emissions? Technological optimism vs. limits to growth.

- **Inequality and redistribution** (lesson 1, GDP limitations) — Piketty's r > g, capital share debates. Macro has traditionally ignored distribution; new research integrates it.

- **Zero lower bound and unconventional policy** (lesson 22, monetary policy) — QE, negative rates, forward guidance. Post-2008 policy innovations.

- **Automation and the future of work** (lesson 15, unemployment) — Will AI/robots create mass unemployment? Historical precedents (Luddites, mechanization) and new challenges.

- **Sovereign debt crises** (lesson 28, open economy) — Eurozone crisis, emerging market defaults. When does debt become unsustainable?

## Difficulty Progression

The curriculum difficulty follows this arc:

**Lessons 1-3 (Measurement):** Difficulty 2 — Accessible introduction using familiar concepts (GDP, unemployment). Focus on definitions and interpretation rather than theory.

**Lessons 4-9 (Growth):** Difficulty 2-4 — Gradual ramp-up. Solow model (lessons 5-6) hits difficulty 3; technological progress and endogenous growth (lessons 7-8) peak at difficulty 4. Review at lesson 9 drops back to 2.

**Lessons 10-14 (Business Cycles):** Difficulty 2-4 — Start accessible (what are cycles?) then build to multiplier algebra and sticky price theory (difficulty 4 at lesson 14).

**Lessons 15-17 (Labor Markets):** Difficulty 3-4 — Conceptually challenging (efficiency wages, matching) but builds on earlier unemployment discussion.

**Lesson 18 (Review):** Difficulty 1 — Light consolidation before new topics.

**Lessons 19-22 (Money and Inflation):** Difficulty 2-4 — Money basics (lesson 19) are accessible; Phillips curve and policy (lessons 21-22) require integrating multiple concepts (difficulty 4 at lesson 21).

**Lessons 23-25 (Fiscal Policy):** Difficulty 3-4 — Policy trade-offs and Ricardian equivalence demand sophisticated reasoning.

**Lesson 26 (Review):** Difficulty 2 — Consolidate policy tools before open economy.

**Lessons 27-30 (Open Economy):** Difficulty 3-4 — Exchange rate regimes and impossible trinity (lesson 28) hit difficulty 4. Final lesson (financial crises) is difficulty 4 but as a resource-drop with scaffolding.

**Overall shape:** Sawtooth pattern with difficulty peaks in the middle of each module, review lessons providing respite, and a general upward trend in baseline difficulty as students build fluency.

## Teaching Tips

### Use Current Events Relentlessly
- Inflation debates, Fed rate decisions, jobs reports, trade tensions, debt ceiling fights — all provide immediate hooks. Start lessons with "in the news" then reverse-engineer the model.

### FRED is Your Friend
- Every lesson should show real data. Don't just describe the Phillips curve, plot it with actual CPI and unemployment.

### Emphasize Units and Magnitudes
- Students often don't internalize that GDP is trillions, unemployment is percent, inflation is annual percent change. Use examples: "A 1 percentage point rise in unemployment means ~1.5 million jobs lost."

### Comparative Statics, Not Forecasting
- Models tell us "if X changes, Y moves in direction Z" — not precise predictions. Be humble about model limitations.

### Teach the Debate, Not the Answer
- On deficits, inflation targeting, exchange rate regimes, there are smart people on multiple sides. Present the trade-offs.

### Solve Problems Together
- Work through 1-2 concrete examples each module: calculate real GDP growth, solve for Solow steady state, compute multiplier, evaluate a policy proposal.
