# Psycholinguistics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Psycholinguistics is uniquely **empirical and phenomenon-driven**. Unlike formal linguistics, which builds theories from intuitions, psycholinguistics tests theories with reaction times, eye movements, and brain imaging. At the intermediate level, **lead with phenomena, not models**: start with garden-path sentences, speech errors, or the McGurk effect, then introduce models as explanations. Balance three elements: (1) **classic phenomena** that ground the field, (2) **experimental methods** that students can understand and critique, and (3) **theoretical debates** (modularity, syntax-first vs. constraint-based). Keep neuroscience integrated but not front-loaded — students should understand behavioral evidence before neural substrates.

Pedagogy should emphasize **prediction and falsification**: before revealing results, ask "What would we expect if language were modular?" or "How could we test if word recognition is automatic?" This mirrors how psycholinguists think and helps students move beyond memorizing effects to evaluating theories.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Language processing is conscious and deliberate"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Introspection makes language feel effortful (e.g., choosing words while writing). But speech comprehension and production are mostly automatic.  
   **How to correct**: Use the Stroop effect — students can't *not* read words. Show reaction time data: lexical access happens in ~200ms, too fast for conscious control. Contrast effortful tasks (translating, puns) with automatic ones (understanding speech).

2. **"We process words sequentially, then build sentences"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Reading feels word-by-word. They don't notice incremental parsing.  
   **How to correct**: Garden-path sentences show we commit to structure *before* the sentence ends (otherwise, how would we get garden-pathed?). Eye-tracking shows fixations on each word but regressions when parsing fails.

3. **"Broca's area = production, Wernicke's area = comprehension"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Textbooks oversimplify 19th-century lesion studies.  
   **How to correct**: Show that Broca's aphasia has comprehension deficits (especially syntax) and Wernicke's aphasia has production deficits (fluent but meaningless). Modern neuroimaging shows distributed networks, not strict localization. Emphasize that "areas" are starting points, not the full story.

4. **"Bilinguals translate between languages"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: That's how they learned a second language in school.  
   **How to correct**: Proficient bilinguals access both languages directly. Translation is slow and effortful because it requires suppressing the direct route. Show code-switching data — bilinguals switch mid-sentence without cost, which wouldn't happen if translating.

5. **"Speech perception is just acoustic pattern matching"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: It seems like ears → sound → words.  
   **How to correct**: Phoneme restoration shows context fills in missing sounds. McGurk shows vision overrides audition. Categorical perception shows we hear categories, not raw acoustics. All require top-down knowledge.

6. **"Reading is unnatural, so it must work differently from speech"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Reading is learned; speech isn't. Writing systems are recent (~5,000 years).  
   **How to correct**: Visual word recognition recruits the same lexical access mechanisms as speech. Evidence: semantic priming works identically for spoken and written words. The difference is input modality (auditory vs. visual), not lexical architecture. Dyslexia often has phonological roots.

7. **"Garden-path sentences are rare and artificial"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Linguists cherry-pick dramatic examples ("The horse raced past the barn fell").  
   **How to correct**: Show naturally occurring garden-paths in newspapers or speech (e.g., "The old man the boats"). Explain that attachment ambiguities are common; the parser just recovers quickly. Eye-tracking shows regressions are frequent in normal reading.

8. **"N400 = semantic processing, P600 = syntactic processing"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Early ERP papers made this claim; it's in textbooks.  
   **How to correct**: N400 appears for any unexpected word, not just semantic anomalies (even orthographic). P600 appears for reanalysis, not just syntactic violations. Modern view: N400 tracks prediction/integration; P600 tracks repair/reanalysis. Don't map components 1:1 to linguistic levels.

9. **"Frequency effects mean we store word frequency explicitly"**  
   **Why students get this wrong**: Confusing behavioral effect with mechanism.  
   **How to correct**: Frequency effects emerge from activation dynamics — frequent words have lower thresholds due to repeated use, not a "frequency tag." Analogy: well-worn paths in grass aren't labeled "walk here more."

10. **"Speech errors are random mistakes"**  
    **Why students get this wrong**: Errors feel like noise.  
    **How to correct**: Errors are systematic. Phonological errors respect syllable structure (onset swaps with onset, not coda). Semantic errors are within-category ("dog" → "cat," not "dog" → "car"). This systematicity reveals production stages.

11. **"Eye movements are random or just about visual clarity"**  
    **Why students get this wrong**: They think eyes scan text uniformly for sharpness.  
    **How to correct**: Readers fixate content words longer than function words. Difficult words get longer fixations and more regressions. Eye movements are tightly linked to cognitive processing, not just visual acuity.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner
- Focus on phenomena over models. Skip debates (syntax-first vs. interactive).
- Use demonstrations (Stroop, McGurk) and introspection probes.
- Minimal formalism; avoid neural networks, Bayesian models, or detailed anatomy.
- Emphasize "what we know" over "competing theories."

### Intermediate (this curriculum)
- **Balance phenomena and models**: cohort model, spreading activation, Levelt's production stages.
- Introduce **experimental design**: students should evaluate whether a study tests its claim.
- Include **neural basis** but tie to behavior (Broca's aphasia → agrammatic speech).
- Present **debates**: modularity, garden-path resolution strategies, bilingual control.
- Expect students to critique: "Does this reaction time difference support the cohort model?"

### Advanced
- Deep dive into **competing models**: TRACE, Shortlist, interactive activation, constraint-based parsing.
- Statistical modeling: mixed-effects models for reaction time, Bayesian models of prediction.
- Neuroimaging methods: fMRI design, ERP component analysis, MEG source localization.
- Read primary literature: assign classic papers (Marslen-Wilson, Tanenhaus, Bock).
- Cross-linguistic evidence: how does psycholinguistics change for languages unlike English?

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

- **Phoneme restoration and predictive processing** (Lesson 5) — Connect to predictive coding in neuroscience, Bayesian brain hypothesis. Reading: Clark's "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science."

- **McGurk effect and multisensory integration** (Lesson 7) — Link to ventriloquism effect, rubber hand illusion, out-of-body experiences. Shows perception is unified, not modality-specific.

- **Garden-path sentences and "good enough" processing** (Lesson 14) — Sometimes readers don't fully reanalyze; they settle for shallow parse. Connects to memory illusions, gist vs. verbatim memory.

- **Tip-of-the-tongue and two-stage retrieval** (Lesson 10) — Semantic access succeeds, phonological fails. Connects to anomia in aphasia, proper name retrieval in aging.

- **Bilingual control and executive function** (Lesson 22) — Bilingualism as mental juggling. Controversial claim: cognitive advantages in task-switching. Great for discussing replication and individual differences.

- **N400 and prediction** (Lesson 25) — N400 isn't "semantic violation," it's prediction error. Connects to machine learning (prediction error minimization), anticipatory eye movements.

- **Eye-tracking and embodied cognition** (Lesson 15) — Visual world paradigm shows listeners look at mentioned objects. Connects to grounded cognition, action-perception coupling.

- **Code-switching and identity** (Lesson 22) — Not just cognitive; sociolinguistic. Spanglish, code-meshing, translanguaging. Connects to sociolinguistics, identity performance.

- **Speech errors and inner speech** (Lesson 18) — Do we make "errors" in inner speech? What does that reveal about monitoring?

- **Reading and neural recycling** (Lesson 21) — The "visual word form area" repurposes object recognition cortex. Reading parasitizes existing neural machinery. Connects to neuroplasticity, critical periods.

## Difficulty Progression

### Early Lessons (1-3): Foundations (Difficulty 2-3)
Start accessible but not trivial. Establish automaticity, introduce methods (reaction time, priming). Students often underestimate how much happens unconsciously — make this visceral with Stroop.

### Middle Build (4-11): Speech and Words (Difficulty 2-3, occasional 4)
Core phenomena: categorical perception, phoneme restoration, McGurk, lexical access, frequency. Most at difficulty 2-3. Tip-of-the-tongue (Lesson 10) is easier (2) because it's relatable. Semantic priming (Lesson 11) is harder (3) because spreading activation is abstract.

### Peak Difficulty (12-16): Sentence Processing (Difficulty 3-4)
Garden-path sentences (Lesson 14) and self-paced reading (Lesson 16) are difficulty 4 — parsing is complex, experimental logic is subtle. Eye-tracking (Lesson 15) as resource-drop bridges methods and phenomena.

### Plateau (17-22): Production, Reading, Bilingualism (Difficulty 2-4)
Production starts easier (Levelt's model is intuitive). Code-switching (Lesson 22) is difficulty 4 because inhibition mechanisms are abstract.

### Neural Synthesis (23-25): Brain Basis (Difficulty 2-4)
Start with classic areas (difficulty 2), then aphasia double dissociations (difficulty 3), finally ERPs (difficulty 4 — N400/P600 require integrating temporal and functional dimensions).

### Review Placement
- Lesson 6: After speech perception (consolidate categorical perception, phoneme restoration, McGurk)
- Lesson 13: Midpoint — integrate speech perception, word recognition, and early sentence processing
- Lesson 20: Before neural basis — contrast production vs. comprehension
- Lesson 25 (final): Not a review but a synthesis — ERPs as *tools* for seeing semantic/syntactic processes

## Key Warnings

1. **Don't front-load brain anatomy** — Students should understand behavioral phenomena first. Neuroscience is evidence *for* cognitive models, not a replacement.

2. **Methods are content** — Don't skip experimental design. Students should be able to say, "That interpretation doesn't follow from reaction time alone."

3. **Avoid the "box model" trap** — Levelt's production stages and similar diagrams are heuristics, not literal modules. Emphasize that real processing is parallel and interactive.

4. **Connect to student experience** — Tip-of-the-tongue, speech errors, garden-path "aha" moments. Psycholinguistics studies what they do every day without noticing.

5. **Probabilistic, not deterministic** — Frequency effects, attachment preferences are *biases*, not rules. Individual differences matter (working memory, vocabulary size, bilingualism).

6. **Cross-linguistic humility** — Most data is from English. Caution against over-generalizing. Mention non-English examples when possible (code-switching in Spanish-English, reading in Chinese).
