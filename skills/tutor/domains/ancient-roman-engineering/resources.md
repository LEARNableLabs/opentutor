# Ancient Roman Engineering — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Ancient Texts
- **Vitruvius, _De Architectura_ (c. 15 BCE)** — foundational text on Roman building practices, materials, surveying, and hydraulics. Available in translation at https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/home.html — essential for understanding Roman engineering philosophy and techniques from a practitioner's perspective.

- **Frontinus, _De aquaeductu_ (c. 98 CE)** — water commissioner's detailed account of Rome's aqueduct system. Available at https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Frontinus/De_Aquis/home.html — remarkable primary source on administration, maintenance, and technical specifications.

### Academic Courses & Lectures
- **MIT OpenCourseWare: Engineering and Ancient Civilizations** — https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/civil-and-environmental-engineering/ — search for courses on engineering history and structural systems. Free lecture notes, assignments, and readings.

- **Yale Open Courses: Roman Architecture** — https://oyc.yale.edu/classics/roman-architecture — Professor Diana E. E. Kleiner's comprehensive course on Roman building from technical and cultural perspectives. Video lectures, transcripts, and syllabus available.

- **Coursera: Roman Architecture (University of Michigan)** — https://www.coursera.org/learn/roman-architecture — includes modules on engineering, materials, and construction techniques.

### Textbooks & Monographs
- **_Roman Engineering_ by L. A. Hamey and J. A. Hamey** — accessible overview of roads, aqueducts, bridges, harbors, and building techniques.

- **_The Roman Aqueducts_ by Trevor Hodge** — comprehensive technical analysis of aqueduct engineering, gradients, and construction methods.

- **_Building in the Roman World_ by Janet DeLaine** — scholarly examination of Roman construction techniques, labor organization, and materials.

- **_Ancient Concrete Technology_ by Heinz-Otto Lamprecht** — detailed analysis of opus caementicium chemistry and applications.

## People (researchers, practitioners, communicators)

### Ancient Engineers & Authors
- **Vitruvius** (1st century BCE) — architect and engineer, wrote _De Architectura_, the only surviving ancient text on architecture and engineering.

- **Frontinus** (c. 40-103 CE) — water commissioner (curator aquarum) who documented Rome's aqueduct system.

- **Apollodorus of Damascus** (c. 60-130 CE) — chief engineer under Trajan, designed Trajan's Bridge and Forum.

### Modern Researchers
- **Marie Jackson** (UC Berkeley / University of Utah) — geologist studying Roman concrete chemistry, especially seawater reactions and tobermorite formation. Key papers on why Roman concrete strengthens over time.

- **Trevor Hodge** (Carleton University) — classical archaeologist specializing in aqueducts, author of _Roman Aqueducts & Water Supply_.

- **Lynne Lancaster** (Ohio University) — architectural historian focusing on Roman concrete construction techniques and innovation.

- **Norman J. W. Thrower** — historian of cartography who studied Roman surveying and measurement systems.

### Science Communicators
- **Tom Scott** — YouTube creator who has covered Roman engineering in accessible formats. Search for "Tom Scott Roman engineering" on YouTube.

- **Practical Engineering (Grady Hillhouse)** — civil engineer YouTuber who occasionally covers ancient engineering principles. https://www.youtube.com/c/PracticalEngineeringChannel

- **History for Granite (podcast)** — episodes on Roman infrastructure engineering and political economy.

## Unexpected Connections (for wild cards)

### Cross-Disciplinary Links

**Roman Engineering → Network Science**
- Roman road network as early example of hub-and-spoke topology
- Cursus publicus as information network optimization problem
- _ORBIS_ project (Stanford) models network effects in empire cohesion
- Modern parallel: how Internet routing protocols resemble Roman way-station logic

**Roman Concrete → Climate Adaptation**
- Modern interest in low-carbon Roman cement formulations (lime-based vs Portland cement)
- Marine infrastructure and rising sea levels: Roman seawater-resistant concrete as template
- DOE and Navy funding research into reviving Roman formulations for coastal resilience
- Connection to sustainability and circular economy (using volcanic waste products)

**Aqueduct Gradients → Modern Water Security**
- Gravity-fed systems require no energy (vs modern pumping infrastructure)
- Relevant to water-stressed regions with unreliable power
- Engineers in developing countries studying Roman passive design
- Connections to appropriate technology movement and off-grid infrastructure

**Roman Surveying → GPS/GNSS**
- Ancient precision without satellites: different technological pathway to same goal (positioning accuracy)
- Surveying tools as embodied geometry: groma = physical plumb line coordinate system
- Philosophy of measurement: what counts as "precise enough" in different technological contexts

**Knowledge Loss → Digital Preservation**
- Why did concrete chemistry knowledge vanish for 1000+ years despite written records?
- Parallels to modern digital file format obsolescence, dead programming languages
- Roman knowledge transmission depended on state funding and apprenticeship systems
- Lessons for preserving contemporary technical knowledge (documentation ≠ transmission)

**Public Infrastructure → Political Philosophy**
- Roads/aqueducts as Roman "grand strategy" (Edward Luttwak)
- Infrastructure as legitimacy: why emperors built monumental public works
- Modern parallels: New Deal projects, interstate highway system, China's Belt and Road
- Public goods provision and state capacity

**Arch Mechanics → Gothic Architecture**
- Roman arch principles repurposed in medieval cathedrals
- Flying buttresses as distributed arch system
- Continuity of knowledge despite political fragmentation
- Structural engineering as trans-historical dialogue

**Roman Standards → Modern Interoperability**
- Standardized road widths, wagon axle gauges
- Milestones as early interface specification
- Modern parallel: railway gauge standardization, USB-C, ISO containers
- Political power of setting standards (who defines "standard Roman road"?)

### Provocative Questions for Deep Dives

- **Could Roman Empire have survived if infrastructure maintenance had been privatized?** (Public choice economics meets Roman history)

- **What would Roman concrete research look like if we didn't have Portland cement incumbency?** (Path dependence in materials science)

- **Are there Roman engineering principles we can't replicate because we lack patient capital?** (Time horizons and financial systems)

- **How much Roman engineering success was replicable luck (volcanic ash availability) vs transferable knowledge?** (Geography and innovation)

## Self-Study Extensions

For students who want to go deeper:

1. **Build a scale model** — construct miniature Roman arch or layered road cross-section
2. **Field survey project** — use simple tools (string, stakes) to lay out a "Roman road" in a park
3. **Chemistry experiment** — mix lime mortar and observe carbonation over weeks
4. **GIS mapping project** — map Roman roads using ORBIS or Pleiades data
5. **Historical source analysis** — close reading of Vitruvius Book VIII (water) or Frontinus
6. **Engineering economics paper** — estimate construction cost and economic return for a specific aqueduct
7. **Comparative infrastructure study** — Roman vs Inca vs Chinese ancient roads: what's universal, what's culturally specific?
