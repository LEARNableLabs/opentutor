# Podcast and Audio Storytelling — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Podcast and audio storytelling is a **craft discipline** that balances art and technique. At the intermediate level, students need both conceptual frameworks (what makes good audio storytelling) and hands-on skills (how to execute it). The most effective teaching approach is **analysis + practice**: deconstructing excellent work to understand choices, then applying those lessons to original pieces.

Unlike purely technical subjects, audio storytelling requires developing **taste** — the ability to recognize what works and why. This means extensive listening homework, critique sessions analyzing professional work, and iterative revision of student projects. The feedback loop is critical: students often can't hear their own errors (pacing too slow, narration overwritten, music too loud) until they've trained their ears.

The technical side (recording, editing, mixing) should be taught as **tools in service of story**, never as ends in themselves. DAW choice doesn't matter; storytelling judgment does. Use whatever software the student already knows, and teach production techniques as solutions to specific narrative problems ("Your interview has room noise — let's fix it" vs. "Here's how noise reduction works in the abstract").

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Good audio storytelling requires expensive equipment"
**Why students believe this**: Professional podcasts sound polished, and beginners assume it's the gear.

**The truth**: A $100 USB microphone in a quiet room produces broadcast-quality audio. The expensive studios at NPR and Gimlet are for convenience and speed, not necessity. Students waste money on gear when they should invest time in craft.

**How to correct**: Show examples of excellent podcasts recorded on budget equipment. Emphasize that the story and the editing make the difference. Teach students to optimize their recording environment (blankets, closets, choosing quiet times) rather than shopping for gear.

### 2. "Music makes everything more emotional"
**Why students believe this**: Every podcast they love uses music, so it must be essential.

**The truth**: Music can enhance emotion, but it can also manipulate, overwhelm, or distract. The best audio storytelling uses music sparingly and intentionally. Silence and natural sound are often more powerful.

**How to correct**: Play examples of over-scored audio vs. minimal scoring. Teach students to ask "Does this moment need music, or am I using it as a crutch?" Point to shows like *S-Town* and *The Daily* that use music carefully vs. *Radiolab* which is maximalist (and that's an intentional style choice).

### 3. "Longer episodes are more valuable"
**Why students believe this**: Prestige podcasts (Serial, S-Town) tend to be 45-60 minutes.

**The truth**: Episode length should match the material. Some stories need 15 minutes; some need an hour. Padding a 20-minute story to 45 minutes kills pacing and loses listeners. Editing to the tightest version shows respect for the audience's time.

**How to correct**: Teach the "tightness test" — can you cut 10% without losing essential meaning? If yes, do it. Show examples of excellent short-form work (NPR's *All Things Considered* features, *99% Invisible*). Emphasize that every minute must earn its place.

### 4. "You need to fill every silence in interviews"
**Why students believe this**: Dead air feels awkward, especially for new interviewers.

**The truth**: Strategic silence lets subjects think and often produces the most honest, revealing responses. Jumping in too quickly cuts off deeper answers. Interviewers should get comfortable with 3-5 second pauses.

**How to correct**: Role-play interviews where the student practices staying silent after asking a question. Play examples of great interviewers (Studs Terkel, Terry Gross) who use silence. Explain that silence in the interview is not silence in the final edit — you control pacing in post.

### 5. "Narration should explain what the listener is about to hear"
**Why students believe this**: They're mimicking traditional radio introductions and textbook writing.

**The truth**: Over-narrating treats the listener as passive and kills discovery. Good audio storytelling trusts the listener to follow along and draws conclusions themselves. The best narration is minimal, poetic, or absent.

**How to correct**: Play over-narrated vs. tight examples. Teach the "show, don't tell" principle from fiction writing. Encourage students to cut their narration in half, then half again, seeing how much the tape can carry on its own.

### 6. "Editing means removing mistakes"
**Why students believe this**: They see editing as error correction, not storytelling.

**The truth**: Editing is **rewriting** — restructuring for clarity, pacing, and emotional impact. The best audio editors are storytellers who shape raw material into narrative. Removing "ums" and breathing is basic hygiene; real editing is about flow, rhythm, and emphasis.

**How to correct**: Show before/after examples where the content is identical but the order and pacing change the impact. Teach students to outline their episode structure before cutting. Emphasize that first assemblies are drafts, not finished products.

### 7. "You need perfect recording conditions to make a podcast"
**Why students believe this**: They hear pristine studio sound on professional shows.

**The truth**: Some of the best podcast moments are imperfectly recorded — field recordings, phone calls, found tape. Ambient noise can add authenticity and place. The goal is "good enough to understand clearly," not "laboratory clean."

**How to correct**: Show examples of lo-fi but emotionally powerful tape (historical recordings, phone interviews, verité moments). Teach students to evaluate if noise serves the story (adds authenticity) or distracts from it. Normalize "good enough" over "perfect."

### 8. "Listeners need constant narration to stay oriented"
**Why students believe this**: Fear of confusing the audience.

**The truth**: Listeners are smart and can follow non-linear narratives, time jumps, and perspective shifts if you use sonic signposting (music, sound effects, voice changes). Over-explaining insults their intelligence.

**How to correct**: Analyze shows like *Radiolab* and *This American Life* that jump through time and space without heavy narration. Teach sonic signposting techniques. Encourage students to test their edits on fresh listeners to identify genuine confusion vs. imagined confusion.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners (if student is actually beginner, not intermediate)
- Start with extremely simple stories (single interview, no sound design)
- Focus on one skill at a time (first: record cleanly; second: cut for clarity; third: add music)
- Use templates and formulas (3-act structure, standard intro/outro)
- Emphasize technical competence before creative experimentation
- Shorter episodes (5-10 minutes) to complete the full production cycle faster

### For Intermediate (this curriculum's target)
- Assume basic technical competence; focus on refining craft
- Introduce sound design and multi-layered production
- Emphasize developing a unique voice and style
- Encourage experimentation with structure and form
- Teach critical listening and self-editing
- Longer, more complex stories (15-30 minutes)
- Address ethical considerations in depth

### For Advanced (if student accelerates beyond this curriculum)
- Focus on innovation and pushing boundaries
- Deep dives into specialized techniques (binaural recording, immersive sound design)
- Serialized narratives and multi-episode story arcs
- Building an audience and distribution strategy
- Collaborative production and team workflows
- Monetization and sustainability

## Rabbit Holes

These are fascinating tangents that can enrich understanding but shouldn't derail the core curriculum. Drop them in as "bonus" exploration for curious students.

### When discussing narrative structure (Lessons 2-4)
- **The history of radio drama** — how audio storytelling developed before television. Listen to old Orson Welles broadcasts.
- **Ira Glass's "This American Life" formula** — the specific structure they use (anecdote → moment of reflection → escalation). Analyze several episodes.

### When discussing interviews (Lessons 7-10)
- **Oral history methodology** — techniques from historians and archivists for preserving voices. Read *Listening to History* by Mark M. Smith.
- **Studs Terkel's interview style** — the master of working-class voices. Listen to his tape and read his advice.

### When discussing sound design (Lessons 12-17)
- **Binaural recording and 3D audio** — techniques for spatial, immersive listening. Experiment with in-ear microphones.
- **Foley artistry** — creating sound effects for film. Watch a foley session on YouTube; try creating your own.
- **Psychoacoustics** — how our brains process sound. Why does reverb make voices sound distant? Why do certain frequencies grab attention?

### When discussing mixing (Lessons 18-22)
- **The loudness wars** — the trend in music production toward hyper-compression. Why podcasts rejected this approach.
- **Mastering for different platforms** — how Spotify, Apple Podcasts, YouTube apply different normalization. Test your episode across platforms.

### When discussing distribution (Lessons 24-26)
- **The podcast discovery problem** — why it's hard to find new shows and what's being done about it.
- **Podcast fiction vs. nonfiction** — different audiences, different conventions, different monetization.
- **Podcast networks and collectives** — how shows band together for cross-promotion and resources.

## Difficulty Progression

The curriculum follows a **wave pattern** of difficulty:

### Lessons 1-3 (Difficulty 2-3): Gentle start
Introduce core concepts (audio-first thinking, hooks, structure) at accessible level. Students should feel confident.

### Lessons 4-6 (Difficulty 2-3): Building foundations
Scene construction and transitions add complexity but remain manageable. Mix of question and mini-lesson types keeps engagement high.

### Lessons 7-10 (Difficulty 2-4): First peak
Interview techniques get challenging, especially ethics (lesson 10, difficulty 4). Students work harder here.

### Lesson 11 (Difficulty 1): Review
Breathing room to consolidate interview concepts through analysis exercise.

### Lessons 12-13 (Difficulty 2-4): Sound design ramp
Ambient sound is accessible (2), but music ethics is challenging (4). Intentional contrast.

### Lessons 14-16 (Difficulty 3-4): Peak complexity
Sound design techniques layer complexity. Students are in deep focus mode.

### Lesson 17 (Difficulty 2): Review
Creative project as a break — design a soundscape. Difficulty is lower because it's applied practice, not new concepts.

### Lessons 18-19 (Difficulty 3-4): Technical depth
Editing and mixing get technical. Difficulty stays elevated.

### Lessons 20-21 (Difficulty 3-4): Sustained challenge
LUFS and mixing coherence are the technical peak. Students work hard but have momentum.

### Lesson 22 (Difficulty 2): Review
Applied mixing exercise consolidates before final module.

### Lessons 23-26 (Difficulty 2-3): Gentle landing
Voice and distribution are less technically demanding. Students finish feeling accomplished, not exhausted.

The **review lessons** (11, 17, 22) are strategically placed after challenging sections to prevent burnout. The **types vary** to keep students engaged — not all mini-lessons, plenty of questions, resource-drops, and applied work.

## Teaching Style Notes

- **Use professional examples constantly** — students learn by osmosis from great work. Assign listening homework for every lesson.
- **Encourage iteration** — first drafts are always rough. Build re-editing into the workflow.
- **Normalize imperfection** — even pros make mistakes, over-score, or misjudge pacing. Revision is the norm.
- **Teach critical listening** — train students to articulate *why* something works, not just whether they like it.
- **Emphasize ethics early and often** — documentary work requires trust and care. Revisit consent and representation throughout.
- **Make it playful** — audio storytelling is creative work. Encourage experimentation, weird ideas, and sonic play.
