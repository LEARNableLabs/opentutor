# Bookbinding — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Textbooks and Manuals

- **Jane Greenfield, *The Care of Fine Books*** — Comprehensive guide to conservation principles and techniques. Perfect for intermediate learners. Covers condition assessment, repair techniques, and ethical decision-making.
  - https://www.nedcc.org/ (NEDCC publications page often references this)

- **Aldren Watson, *Hand Bookbinding: A Manual of Instruction*** — Step-by-step guide to traditional binding structures. Clear diagrams and accessible instructions.
  - Classic text, widely used in library schools and conservation training

- **Keith Smith, *Non-Adhesive Binding* series (4 volumes)** — Explores non-adhesive structures (stab binding, coptic, accordion, etc.) with exceptional clarity. Volume 1 is essential for understanding historical alternatives to case binding.
  - https://www.keithsmithbooks.com/

- **Manly Banister, *The Craft of Bookbinding*** — Practical guide to case binding, covering tools, materials, and techniques in detail.

- **Library of Congress, *Care and Handling of Recorded Materials*** — Government publication on preservation standards and best practices.
  - https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/

### Professional Standards and Ethics

- **American Institute for Conservation (AIC), Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice** — The foundational ethical framework for conservation practice.
  - https://www.culturalheritage.org/about-conservation/code-of-ethics

- **NEDCC (Northeast Document Conservation Center) Preservation Leaflets** — Free, authoritative guides on preservation topics (environmental controls, storage, disaster planning, repair techniques).
  - https://www.nedcc.org/free-resources/preservation-leaflets/overview

- **Library of Congress Preservation Directorate Resources** — Standards, research, and practical guides for preservation and conservation.
  - https://www.loc.gov/preservation/

### University Courses and Syllabi

- **University of Texas at Austin, School of Information — Preservation Management** — Course materials and readings lists for library preservation programs.
  - https://www.ischool.utexas.edu/

- **Ligatus Research Centre (University of the Arts London)** — Research on historical bookbinding techniques; publishes typologies and structural analyses.

## Unexpected Cross-Discipline Connections

### 1. **Forensic Science and Provenance Research**
Bookbinding structures can identify forgeries, track stolen manuscripts, and date books. Conservators collaborate with art crime investigators to trace looted cultural heritage. The FBI Art Crime Team consults binding experts.

- **Rabbit hole:** The Vinland Map controversy — binding evidence was used to argue (inconclusively) whether this "pre-Columbian" map of North America was a forgery.

### 2. **Medieval History through Material Evidence**
Bookbindings preserve fragments of earlier manuscripts (binders recycled parchment as waste material). These "binding waste" fragments have recovered lost texts, music, and legal documents. Conservation labs are archaeology sites.

- **Rabbit hole:** The discovery of previously unknown medieval music in the binding waste of a 16th-century account book. Entire chants recovered from recycled parchment.

### 3. **Textile Conservation and Fashion History**
Many book covering materials are textiles (velvet, brocade, embroidered silk). Textile conservators and book conservators share techniques for stabilizing, cleaning, and storing fibrous materials. Historical bookbindings inform fashion history.

- **Example:** The "Mildmay Fane" embroidered bindings (17th century) show the same needlework techniques used in contemporary clothing.

### 4. **Materials Science and Nanotechnology**
Modern conservation research involves polymer chemistry, photochemical degradation studies, and nanoparticle consolidants. Conservators collaborate with chemists to develop better adhesives, consolidants, and deacidification treatments.

- **Rabbit hole:** Klucel G (hydroxypropyl cellulose) as a leather consolidant — reversible, non-toxic, and compatible with historical materials. Developed for conservation, now used in pharmaceuticals and food science.

### 5. **Environmental Science and Climate Data**
Book deterioration is an environmental proxy. Acid paper degradation maps to industrial pollution; insect damage correlates with humidity shifts. Conservators track climate change through material evidence.

- **Example:** The "Great Book Disaster" of 1966 (Florence flood) led to massive advances in disaster recovery and created the modern conservation field. Climate events drive conservation innovation.

### 6. **Disability Studies and Access**
Conservation must balance preservation with access. Some repairs (rebinding) make books more usable for people with limited hand mobility. Conservators work with adaptive technology specialists to create accessible handling solutions for fragile materials.

- **Rabbit hole:** The ethics of digitization — is scanning a fragile book an act of preservation (reduces handling) or violation (exposes it to light and mechanical stress)? No easy answers.

### 7. **Economics and Cultural Heritage Policy**
Who decides what gets conserved? Funding priorities reveal what societies value. Book conservation intersects with intellectual property (preserving orphan works), indigenous knowledge (repatriating manuscripts), and public access (open libraries vs. private collections).

- **Question:** Should a unique 15th-century manuscript be conserved in its original binding (which is falling apart) or rebound to preserve the text? The binding has historical value; rebinding destroys evidence but saves the content. Economics forces the choice.
