# Aerospace Engineering — Flight and Propulsion — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Textbooks and Course Materials

- **Aircraft and Rocket Propulsion by Brian J. Cantwell (Stanford)** — Comprehensive treatment of both air-breathing and rocket engines from a unified thermodynamic perspective. Available online as course reference text. Excellent for intermediate students because it balances theory with practical design considerations. Used in Stanford AA283.
  - https://web.stanford.edu/~cantwell/AA103_Course_Material/AA103%20Course%20Reference%20Texts%20from%20AA210%20and%20AA283/AA283_Aircraft_and_Rocket_Propulsion_BOOK_Brian_J_Cantwell.pdf

### University Courses (Open Access)

- **MIT 16.100 Aerodynamics (Fall 2005)** — Complete OCW course covering subsonic potential flow, viscous flows, boundary layers, airfoil/wing theory, supersonic/hypersonic flow. Lecture notes, assignments, and exams available. Perfect intermediate-level resource.
  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-100-aerodynamics-fall-2005/

- **MIT 16.50 Introduction to Propulsion Systems (Spring 2012)** — Presents aerospace propulsive devices as systems with functional requirements and engineering limitations. Covers air-breathing engines and rockets. Includes lecture notes and problem sets.
  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-50-introduction-to-propulsion-systems-spring-2012/

- **MIT 16.885J Aircraft Systems Engineering (Fall 2005)** — Systems-level view of aircraft design including propulsion integration, aerodynamics, structures. Includes video lectures.
  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-885j-aircraft-systems-engineering-fall-2005/

- **Stanford AA283 Aircraft and Rocket Propulsion (Online)** — Introduction to design and performance of airbreathing and rocket engines. Physical parameters for propulsion system performance. Available through Stanford Online.
  - https://online.stanford.edu/courses/aa283-aircraft-and-rocket-propulsion

- **MIT Course Catalogs** — Full listings of Aeronautics and Astronautics courses with prerequisites and descriptions.
  - https://catalog.mit.edu/schools/engineering/aeronautics-astronautics/

## Unexpected Cross-Discipline Connections

### Aerospace → Other Fields

1. **Formula 1 Racing** — F1 cars are essentially upside-down aircraft. The aerodynamics principles are identical but inverted (downforce instead of lift). Ground effect, diffusers, and DRS systems use venturi effects and circulation control. F1 is a perfect case study for aerodynamic trade-offs: drag vs. downforce, cooling vs. drag.

2. **America's Cup Sailing** — Modern racing yachts use rigid wing sails that are literally aircraft wings turned vertical. The foils that lift the hull out of the water are underwater wings. Boundary layer transition, induced drag, and aspect ratio all apply. This shows how the same fluid mechanics works in water (Re ~ 10⁶) and air.

3. **Wind Turbines** — Wind turbine blades are rotating wings. Blade element momentum theory, airfoil selection, tip speed ratio, and wake effects all draw from aerospace. The difference: extracting energy rather than generating thrust. Shows time-reversibility of some aerodynamic principles.

4. **Atmospheric Science** — Lee waves (mountain waves), rotors, and föhn winds are atmospheric compressible flow phenomena. Air flowing over mountains can reach Mach 1 relative to local sound speed, creating standing shock waves. This connects aerodynamics to meteorology and climate.

5. **Biomimicry and Animal Flight** — Birds, bats, and insects use aerodynamic principles but often in unsteady regimes that challenge conventional wing theory. Leading-edge vortices (dragonflies), variable-camber wings (birds), clap-and-fling (tiny insects) reveal the limits of steady aerodynamics. Connects to biology and inspires MAV design.

6. **Astrophysics and Shock Waves** — Supersonic jets from young stars, supernova remnants, and bow shocks around planets all involve compressible flow and shock waves. The same Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions apply. Scale: parsecs instead of meters; Mach numbers: 10-1000 instead of 1-5.

7. **Cardiovascular Medicine** — Blood flow in arteries involves Reynolds numbers (~ 1000-4000), transition to turbulence, boundary layers, and flow separation (aneurysms). Atherosclerotic plaques create flow blockages analogous to airfoil stall. Medical device design (stents, heart valves) uses CFD.

8. **Architecture and Wind Engineering** — Building aerodynamics (pressure distributions, vortex shedding, flutter) uses the same principles as aircraft. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse was an aeroelastic phenomenon. Skyscraper design considers crosswinds and turbulence.

## Topic-Specific Learning Pathways

### For Students Interested in...

**Space Exploration:**
- Focus on lessons 15-18 (compressible flow), 29-31 (rocket propulsion)
- Deep-dive resources: Cantwell's rocket chapters, NASA propulsion simulations
- Rabbit holes: SpaceX Raptor, staged combustion, hypersonic re-entry

**Commercial Aviation:**
- Focus on lessons 5-14 (aerodynamics), 22-27 (air-breathing engines)
- Deep-dive resources: MIT aircraft systems engineering, pilot ground school videos
- Rabbit holes: winglets, area rule, high-bypass turbofans

**Drones and eVTOL:**
- Focus on lessons 8-9 (finite wings), 23-26 (turbofans/propellers), 32 (integration)
- Deep-dive resources: FlightStream (UAM applications), AirShaper
- Rabbit holes: distributed electric propulsion, boundary layer ingestion

**Hypersonic Flight:**
- Focus on lessons 15-18 (compressible flow), 26 (ramjets/scramjets)
- Deep-dive resources: MIT supersonic/hypersonic modules, Whitcomb area rule
- Rabbit holes: X-43, scramjets, thermal protection systems

**CFD and Simulation:**
- Use interactive tools throughout: NASA sims (early), FlightStream (mid), Stallion 3D (advanced)
- Code-based: AeroToy for real-time experimentation
- Theory: boundary layer equations, shock relations, discretization methods
