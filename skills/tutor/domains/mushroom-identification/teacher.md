## Domain Hooks
- **Mushrooms and climate change** — shifting ranges, phenology disruption, mycorrhizal partner stress. Drop in when discussing seasonal timing (Lesson 20).

- **Fungal bioluminescence** — glowing mushrooms (Panellus, Omphalotus). Drop in as a "wild card" after covering polypores (Lesson 11).

- **Mycophagy (animals eating mushrooms)** — squirrels, slugs, deer, and their role in spore dispersal. Drop in when discussing ecology (Lesson 18).

- **Mushroom cultivation vs. wild foraging** — why some mycorrhizal species (porcini, chanterelles) can't be cultivated. Drop in when discussing mycorrhizal associations (Lesson 18).

- **Fungal pathogens and tree health** — Armillaria (honey mushroom) as both edible and tree killer. Drop in during ecology module (Lesson 19).

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"I can learn to identify mushrooms from a book without going outside"**
   - Why: Mushrooms are highly variable; field context (substrate, associates, smell, staining) is critical and not fully captured in photos
   - How to correct: Require field observations early, emphasize that books are references, not standalone training; use iNaturalist to show the range of variation within a single species

2. **"The cap is the most important feature"**
   - Why: Caps are visually prominent and vary dramatically, so beginners fixate on them
   - How to correct: Show examples where cap-identical species differ in gill attachment, stem base, or spore print; make checking the stem base and taking a spore print mandatory steps

3. **"All poisonous mushrooms are obviously dangerous-looking"**
   - Why: Cultural tropes (red-and-white Amanita muscaria) create the impression that toxicity is advertised
   - How to correct: Show photos of deadly Amanitas that are white, elegant, and edible-looking;
