# Law and Economics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Law and economics at the intermediate level requires balancing formal analysis with intuitive examples. The math is simple (basic calculus and algebra) but the *applications* are subtle — students must learn to see legal rules as incentive structures. Use concrete cases to ground abstract concepts, then work backward to the general principle. This is an applied field: every theoretical concept should connect to a real legal rule, court case, or policy debate. At the intermediate level, formalism is a tool, not the goal — teach students to think like economic analysts of law, not just to solve optimization problems.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Economics is just about money"** — Students often think economic analysis only considers financial costs/benefits. Emphasize that economics studies *all* incentives: reputational, psychological, social. Utility functions can include fairness, altruism, revenge.

2. **"The Coase theorem means law doesn't matter"** — This is the #1 misconception. Students latch onto "outcome is efficient regardless of initial allocation" and miss "when transaction costs are zero." Real-world transaction costs (bargaining, enforcement, information asymmetry) mean law *does* matter. Use examples where Coase applies (two neighbors and a barking dog) vs. where it doesn't (thousands of pollution victims).

3. **"Efficient breach means we want people to break contracts"** — Students confuse *descriptive* efficiency with *normative* endorsement. Clarify: efficient breach with expectation damages makes the breacher internalize the harm, so they only breach when it's socially valuable. It's not a license to break promises.

4. **"Strict liability is always stricter"** — The name misleads. Under strict liability, injurers may take *less* care if care doesn't affect whether they're liable. The key is which party is the "least-cost avoider" of harm.

5. **"Hand formula means judges do math"** — Students think courts literally calculate B < PL in every case. Explain it's a *conceptual framework*: negligence law implicitly compares costs and benefits of precaution, even if judges don't write equations.

6. **"Behavioral economics disproves rational choice"** — Students sometimes think bounded rationality undermines all law and economics. Instead: behavioral insights *extend* the toolkit by showing how legal defaults, framing, and nudges can improve outcomes when people aren't fully rational.

7. **"Economic analysis ignores justice"** — Law students especially resist efficiency as "amoral." Clarify early: efficiency is *one* criterion for evaluating law; equity/fairness/rights matter too. Economics helps us see tradeoffs, not choose values.

8. **"Regulation is always bad (or always good)"** — Students from different political backgrounds bring priors. Teach the tools (market failure, capture, public choice) neutrally and let students apply them to real cases. The goal is analysis, not ideology.

## Level Adjustments

**Beginner** — Focus on intuition over formalism. Use simple numerical examples. Avoid heavy math. Emphasize stories and cases.

**Intermediate (this curriculum)** — Introduce formal models (Hand formula, expected damages calculations) but always connect to real applications. Students should be able to work through basic optimization problems (optimal care level, optimal penalty) and translate legal rules into economic language. Some game theory (settlement bargaining, Coase negotiations) but keep it simple (2x2 games, backward induction).

**Advanced** — Graduate-level rigor. Formal proofs, Nash equilibrium, mechanism design. Read academic papers, not just textbooks. Engage with empirical evidence: do tort reforms actually reduce accidents? Does capital punishment deter crime? Critiques of law and economics (distributional concerns, behavioral limits, expressive function of law).

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

- **Evolutionary game theory and legal norms** — how do social norms (like promise-keeping or revenge) emerge without formal law? Connects to repeated games, focal points, and conventions. Drop this in during contract or criminal law modules.

- **Mechanism design and constitutional law** — the U.S. Constitution is a mechanism for aggregating preferences under constraints (federalism, separation of powers). Connects to voting theory, incentive compatibility. Save for after students understand basics of agency problems.

- **Intellectual property and innovation** — patents and copyrights as property rights in ideas. Tradeoff: more protection → more innovation but higher monopoly costs. Connects to monopoly, regulation, and dynamic efficiency. Great capstone topic.

- **Prediction markets for law** — can we use betting markets to aggregate information about legal outcomes? Connects to information economics, wisdom of crowds. Fun real-world example: betting on Supreme Court decisions.

- **Law and economics of privacy** — externalities from data sharing, property rights in personal information, behavioral biases in consent. Timely application that synthesizes multiple concepts.

- **Economics of legal origins** — do common law vs. civil law systems produce different economic outcomes? Controversial empirical literature. Good for students interested in comparative law or development.

- **Expressive function of law** — law shapes behavior not just through incentives but by signaling values (e.g., anti-discrimination law). Challenges pure economic models. Save for advanced students or as a critical perspective.

## Difficulty Progression

**Lessons 1-5 (Foundations)** — Difficulty 1-3. Start gentle with "why economics?" Build to Coase theorem, which is conceptually hard but mathematically easy. Students need time to internalize the idea that legal rules create incentives.

**Lessons 6-11 (Property & Contract)** — Difficulty 1-4. Review lesson 6 consolidates. Efficient breach (lesson 9) and incomplete contracts (lessons 10-11) are conceptually challenging. Lesson 11 (penalty defaults) is a peak difficulty moment — it requires understanding information economics.

**Lessons 12-16 (Tort & Crime)** — Difficulty 1-4. Review lesson 13. Hand formula (lesson 12) is accessible; the teach-back on optimal sanctions (lesson 16) is challenging because students must synthesize deterrence theory and probability.

**Lessons 17-19 (Litigation)** — Difficulty 2-3. Settlement (lesson 17) requires game theory intuition but no complex math. Fee shifting and class actions introduce policy tradeoffs.

**Lessons 20-24 (Markets & Regulation)** — Difficulty 1-4. Review lesson 20. The Microsoft case (lesson 22) is a peak difficulty application requiring students to integrate antitrust economics with legal/strategic considerations.

**Lessons 25-26 (Advanced Topics)** — Difficulty 2-4. Behavioral economics (lesson 25) is conceptually challenging but engaging. Final review (lesson 26) is difficulty 2 — synthesis and reflection, not new hard material.

## Teaching Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Don't frontload math** — Students who see too many equations early will tune out. Build intuition first, formalism second.
- **Don't skip real cases** — Abstract principles feel empty without concrete applications. Every major concept should tie to a real legal rule or court decision.
- **Don't ignore distributional concerns** — Efficiency analysis often has regressive distributional effects (e.g., pollution victims bear costs). Acknowledge this; don't pretend economics answers every normative question.
- **Don't assume legal knowledge** — Even intermediate students may not know basic legal concepts (what's a tort? what's consideration?). Provide brief glosses when introducing legal terms.
- **Don't treat Coase theorem as a curiosity** — It's the theoretical foundation. Students must understand both the theorem and why transaction costs break it in practice.
