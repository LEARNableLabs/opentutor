# Law and Economics — Domain Teaching Config

## Exercise Types
This domain uses a mix of delivery formats:
- **concept-focused mini-lessons** — 8 lessons (31%)
- **Socratic questions** — 5 lessons (19%)
- **real-world application challenges** — 4 lessons (15%)
- **review and consolidation sessions** — 4 lessons (15%)
- **curated resource exploration** — 3 lessons (12%)
- **teach-back exercises (student explains)** — 2 lessons (8%)

Primary format: concept-focused mini-lessons.

## Resource Types
This domain has strong coverage in: textbooks, video lectures, interactive tools, academic papers, code repositories, online courses. Prioritize these when recommending resources.

## Domain Hooks
- **Evolutionary game theory and legal norms** — how do social norms (like promise-keeping or revenge) emerge without formal law? Connects to repeated games, focal points, and conventions. Drop this in during contract or criminal law modules.

- **Mechanism design and constitutional law** — the U.S. Constitution is a mechanism for aggregating preferences under constraints (federalism, separation of powers). Connects to voting theory, incentive compatibility. Save for after students understand basics of agency problems.

- **Intellectual property and innovation** — patents and copyrights as property rights in ideas. Tradeoff: more protection → more innovation but higher monopoly costs. Connects to monopoly, regulation, and dynamic efficiency. Great capstone topic.

- **Prediction markets for law** — can we use betting markets to aggregate information about legal outcomes? Connects to information economics, wisdom of crowds. Fun real-world example: betting on Supreme Court decisions.

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"Economics is just about money"** — Students often think economic analysis only considers financial costs/benefits. Emphasize that economics studies *all* incentives: reputational, psychological, social. Utility functions can include fairness, altruism, revenge.

2. **"The Coase theorem means law doesn't matter"** — This is the #1 misconception. Students latch onto "outcome is efficient regardless of initial allocation" and miss "when transaction costs are zero." Real-world transaction costs (bargaining, enforcement, information asymmetry) mean law *does* matter. Use examples where Coase applies (two neighbors and a barking dog) vs. where it doesn't (thousands of pollution victims).

3. **"Efficient breach means we want people to break contracts"** — Students confuse *descriptive* efficiency with *normative* endorsement.
