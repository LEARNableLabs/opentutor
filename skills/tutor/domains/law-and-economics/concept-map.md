# Law and Economics — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Positive vs normative analysis** — distinction between describing how law works vs. prescribing how it should work
2. **Efficiency as a goal** — using economic efficiency (Pareto, Kaldor-Hicks) as a criterion for evaluating legal rules
3. **Property rights** — legally enforceable claims to resources; foundation for markets and exchange
4. **Externalities** — costs/benefits imposed on third parties; source of market failure
5. **Tragedy of the commons** — overuse of open-access resources due to lack of property rights. Depends on: externalities, property rights
6. **Coase theorem** — when transaction costs are zero, initial legal allocation doesn't affect efficiency. Depends on: property rights, externalities
7. **Transaction costs** — costs of negotiating and enforcing agreements. Depends on: Coase theorem
8. **Contract enforcement** — legal mechanisms to ensure promises are kept; enables trade. Depends on: property rights
9. **Efficient breach** — breaking a contract when gains from breach exceed damages paid. Depends on: contract enforcement, efficiency
10. **Expectation damages** — compensation that puts the non-breaching party in the position they would have been in if the contract had been performed. Depends on: efficient breach
11. **Incomplete contracts** — contracts that don't specify actions for all possible states of the world. Depends on: contract enforcement, transaction costs
12. **Default rules** — legal rules that apply when parties haven't specified otherwise. Depends on: incomplete contracts
13. **Negligence** — liability based on failure to take reasonable care. Depends on: efficiency
14. **Hand formula** — cost-benefit test for negligence (B < PL). Depends on: negligence
15. **Strict liability** — liability regardless of care level. Depends on: negligence
16. **Optimal deterrence** — setting penalties to minimize social costs of harmful behavior. Depends on: efficiency
17. **Criminal sanctions** — punishment for socially harmful acts; differs from tort damages. Depends on: optimal deterrence
18. **Settlement** — resolving disputes without trial. Depends on: transaction costs
19. **Fee shifting** — rules about who pays legal costs; American rule vs. English rule. Depends on: settlement
20. **Class actions** — aggregating many small claims into one lawsuit. Depends on: collective action
21. **Monopoly power** — ability to raise price above competitive level. Depends on: efficiency, market failure
22. **Antitrust** — legal rules to prevent or remedy excessive market power. Depends on: monopoly power
23. **Regulatory capture** — when regulators serve industry interests over public interest. Depends on: public choice theory
24. **Agency problems** — conflicts between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers). Depends on: transaction costs
25. **Behavioral economics** — systematic deviations from rational choice. Depends on: bounded rationality
26. **Nudges** — choice architecture that influences behavior without restricting options. Depends on: behavioral economics, default rules

## Dependencies

### Foundational Layer
- **Efficiency** is the lens through which all other concepts are evaluated — it must be understood first
- **Property rights** and **externalities** are fundamental building blocks that appear throughout the field
- **Transaction costs** explain when the Coase theorem applies (when costs are low) and when it doesn't (when costs are high)

### Contract Law
- **Efficient breach** requires understanding both **contract enforcement** (what's being breached) and **efficiency** (why breach might be desirable)
- **Incomplete contracts** arise because of **transaction costs** — it's too expensive to specify every contingency
- **Default rules** only matter because contracts are **incomplete**; they fill the gaps parties left

### Tort & Criminal Law
- **Negligence** vs. **strict liability** is a choice between two liability regimes, both aimed at **optimal deterrence**
- The **Hand formula** operationalizes **negligence** by making the cost-benefit test explicit
- **Criminal sanctions** differ from tort damages because some defendants are **judgment-proof** (can't pay damages)

### Litigation
- **Settlement** happens when **transaction costs** of trial exceed the expected gains
- **Fee shifting** rules affect **settlement** incentives by changing the cost of going to trial
- **Class actions** solve a **collective action problem** but create **agency costs** between plaintiffs and lawyers

### Markets & Regulation
- **Antitrust** law addresses **monopoly power**, which is a form of market failure
- **Regulatory capture** arises from **agency problems** between regulators (agents) and the public (principals)
- **Behavioral economics** challenges the rational actor assumption underlying many economic analyses

### Advanced Synthesis
- **Nudges** combine insights from **behavioral economics** (how defaults affect choices) and **default rules** (law's role in setting defaults)

## Bottlenecks

**The Coase Theorem** is the single most important concept — it appears in property, contract, tort, and regulation. Students must deeply understand both its claim (efficiency is independent of initial allocation when transaction costs are zero) and its limits (transaction costs are rarely zero in practice).

**Transaction costs** explain why law matters at all. If transaction costs were always zero, the Coase theorem would hold and initial legal rules wouldn't affect efficiency. Understanding this concept unlocks why legal design matters.

**Efficiency vs. equity** — students must understand that economic analysis focuses on efficiency, but equity/fairness matters too. This tension appears in every area of law.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"The Coase theorem says law doesn't matter"** — No, it says law doesn't matter *for efficiency when transaction costs are zero*. In practice, transaction costs are rarely zero, so law matters a great deal.

2. **"Efficient breach means breaking contracts is good"** — No, it means breach is sometimes efficient when the breacher pays expectation damages. The damages internalize the harm.

3. **"Strict liability means stricter than negligence"** — No, strict liability can lead to *less* care in some situations (if care doesn't affect liability). The choice depends on which party can avoid harm most cheaply.

4. **"Economic analysis ignores fairness"** — No, it focuses on efficiency but doesn't claim efficiency is the only goal. Equity matters too; economics helps clarify the tradeoffs.

5. **"Behavioral economics disproves law and economics"** — No, it enriches it by showing how legal rules can account for bounded rationality and systematic biases.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Microeconomics** — needed for: supply and demand, consumer/producer surplus, externalities, market failure, game theory basics
- **Calculus** — needed for: optimization (Hand formula, optimal deterrence calculations), marginal analysis
- **Game theory** — needed for: Coase bargaining, settlement dynamics, strategic behavior in litigation
- **Basic legal concepts** — helpful but not required: understanding what contracts, torts, property rights, and crimes are makes applications more concrete
