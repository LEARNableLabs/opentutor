# Lichenology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Lichenology is fundamentally visual and hands-on, so the teaching approach should emphasize observation, pattern recognition, and field application over rote memorization. At the intermediate level, we move beyond "lichens are cool symbioses" to explore mechanistic questions: *why* does symbiosis work, *how* do ecological gradients structure communities, and *what* makes certain species reliable bioindicators. The material balances biological mechanisms (symbiotic physiology, stress tolerance) with applied skills (identification, monitoring design), making it ideal for interleaving conceptual depth with real-world practice.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Lichens are a type of plant/fungus/algae"** — Students often try to force lichens into familiar taxonomic categories. Reality: lichens are symbiotic systems, not single organisms. The lichen *thallus* is the visible integrated structure, but taxonomically we name the fungal partner. This matters for understanding reproduction, ecology, and evolution. Correction: Emphasize the holobiont concept early and revisit it when discussing ecology and dispersal.

2. **"All lichens fix nitrogen"** — Many students assume nitrogen fixation is universal because cyanobacteria are photosynthetic. Reality: only lichens with cyanobacterial photobionts (or cephalodia) fix N₂; many lichens use green algae (Trebouxia) and obtain nitrogen from atmospheric deposition or substrate. This matters for understanding lichen ecology and succession. Correction: Explicitly contrast green algal vs. cyanobacterial partners in Lesson 3.

3. **"Crustose lichens are primitive; fruticose are advanced"** — Students often interpret growth forms as evolutionary grades. Reality: all three major growth forms appear across the lichen phylogeny; morphology reflects ecological adaptation to substrate, moisture, and disturbance, not evolutionary stage. Correction: Lesson 6 should emphasize functional trade-offs (crustose = secure but slow-growing; fruticose = high surface area but vulnerable to desiccation).

4. **"Lichens grow where nothing else can because they're tough"** — This oversimplifies stress tolerance. Reality: lichens are *desiccation* tolerant and can tolerate extreme cold/heat when dry, but they photosynthesize slowly and are poor competitors for nutrients and light. They dominate harsh environments because other organisms can't tolerate the stress, not because lichens are universally superior. Correction: Lesson 12 should contrast poikilohydry with competitive exclusion.

5. **"Any lichen indicates clean air"** — Students often generalize from "some lichens are pollution-sensitive" to "seeing lichens = clean air." Reality: pollution tolerance varies widely among species; urban environments support pollution-tolerant species (e.g., *Xanthoria*, *Physcia*) while excluding sensitive ones (e.g., *Lobaria*, *Usnea*). Bioindication requires species-level identification and knowledge of tolerance gradients. Correction: Lesson 17-18 should show contrasting species and emphasize diversity indices over presence/absence.

6. **"Chemical spot tests identify species definitively"** — Students treat spot tests like a color-coded answer key. Reality: chemical reactions (K+ yellow, C+ red, etc.) narrow possibilities but require morphological context and often don't distinguish closely related species. Molecular and TLC methods resolve many groups. Correction: Lesson 24 should demonstrate spot tests in context of a full identification workflow.

7. **"Lichens reproduce sexually like other fungi"** — Students often overlook asexual propagules because sexual spores are familiar from mycology. Reality: most lichens rely heavily on soredia, isidia, and fragmentation because sexual spores must re-acquire photobionts after germination—a major constraint. Correction: Lesson 8 should emphasize the unique challenge of maintaining symbiosis across generations.

8. **"Lichen diversity is highest in the tropics"** — Following the latitudinal diversity gradient assumption. Reality: lichen diversity peaks in temperate and boreal forests with high humidity and substrate diversity; tropical lowlands are often lichen-poor due to heat stress during wet periods. Correction: Lesson 25 can note this biogeographic inversion and its climatic drivers.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners
- Focus on symbiosis as "teamwork," growth forms as shapes, and bioindication as "canaries in coal mines."
- Skip chemical taxonomy entirely; use photo-based identification only.
- Emphasize visual pattern recognition and basic ecology (succession, habitat).
- Provide more scaffolding for field observation (checklists, annotated photos).

### For Intermediate (This Curriculum)
- Balance mechanistic understanding (nutrient exchange, poikilohydry) with applied skills (sampling design, spot tests).
- Introduce controlled parasitism debate to nuance mutualism narrative.
- Require understanding of diversity indices and basic study design.
- Use primary literature sparingly (1-2 papers) for bioindication and climate change topics.

### For Advanced
- Deep dive into lichen genomics, horizontal gene transfer, and co-evolution theory.
- Rigorous treatment of statistical methods for biomonitoring (ordination, mixed models).
- TLC and molecular identification (ITS sequencing, phylogenetics).
- Comparative physiology of desiccation tolerance mechanisms.
- Independent research project: design and execute a local biomonitoring or diversity survey.

## Difficulty Progression

- **Lessons 1-5 (Symbiosis foundations)** — Start accessible (D=2) with "what is a lichen?" then ramp to D=3-4 as we question mutualism and explore specificity. This sets up critical thinking early.
- **Lessons 6-10 (Form and function)** — Maintain D=2-3 for morphology and reproduction (observational), then review at D=1 (Lesson 10) to consolidate.
- **Lessons 11-16 (Ecology)** — Start D=2 with succession basics, peak at D=4 for dispersal and community assembly (Lesson 14), then review at D=2 (Lesson 16).
- **Lessons 17-22 (Bioindication)** — Start D=2 with "lichens indicate air quality" concept, peak at D=4 for study design (Lesson 19) and climate modeling (Lesson 21), review at D=2 (Lesson 22).
- **Lessons 23-26 (Identification/conservation)** — Maintain D=3-4 for chemical taxonomy (Lesson 24) and conservation synthesis (Lesson 26). End on application, not review, to launch into practice.

## Rabbit Holes (Unexpected Connections)

### When to deploy:

- **Lichen secondary metabolites as antibiotics** — Drop during Lesson 9 (secondary chemistry). Usnic acid has antibacterial properties and was historically used medicinally; connects to natural products chemistry and drug discovery. Great for pre-med or biochem-interested students.

- **Lichens in space research** — Drop during Lesson 12 (desiccation tolerance). *Xanthoria*, *Rhizocarpon*, and others survived 16 days exposed outside the ISS in the EXPOSE experiments; tests for panspermia hypotheses and astrobiology relevance. Mind-blowing for physics/astro students.

- **Harris's tweed and lichen dyes** — Drop during Lesson 9 or 25 (chemistry or biodiversity). Traditional Scottish tweed used *Parmelia*, *Ochrolechia*, *Roccella* for wool dyes (purples, reds, browns). Connects chemistry to cultural history and ethnobotany.

- **Lichens as miniature climate records** — Drop during Lesson 21 (climate change). Rhizocarpon growth rates used for lichenometry—dating glacial retreat, rockfall events, archaeological features. Connects to geomorphology, dendrochronology, and paleoclimatology.

- **Old man's beard and old-growth forests** — Drop during Lesson 15 (forest age). *Usnea longissima* and *Lobaria* as flagship conservation species in PNW old-growth; habitat loss drives declines. Emotional hook for conservation motivation.

- **Lichen-eating mammals** — Drop during Lesson 13 (succession). Caribou/reindeer rely on *Cladonia* (reindeer lichen) in winter; overgrazing, climate change, and habitat fragmentation affect herds. Connects to wildlife ecology and Indigenous subsistence.

- **Lichens and rock art preservation** — Drop during Lesson 11 (substrate weathering). Lichens both damage rock art (bioweathering acids) and protect it (surface stabilization). Tension between ecological processes and cultural preservation; links to archaeology and heritage management.

- **The "green liver" hypothesis** — Drop during Lesson 4 (controlled parasitism). Some researchers argue the fungus "farms" algae like we farm livestock, harvesting photosynthate; others argue algae gain more than they lose. Philosophy of biology question: can we call it mutualism if partners have unequal power?

- **Lichen names and mythology** — Drop anytime during identification lessons. *Usnea* (old man's beard), *Peltigera* (dog lichen, historically treated rabies), *Lobaria pulmonaria* (lungwort, Doctrine of Signatures). Connects to medical history and ethnolichenology.

## Engagement Strategies

- **Photo challenges** — "Find 3 different growth forms on one tree" or "Spot the cyanobacterial lichen in this image set." Builds observational skills and reinforces morphology.
- **Spot test simulations** — If field work isn't feasible, show before/after images of K, C, KC, P tests on different species. Students predict chemistry from morphology, then "test" (reveal).
- **iNaturalist contributions** — Encourage students to photograph and upload local lichens; gamifies learning and builds citizen science database.
- **Design a biomonitoring study** — Lesson 19 can be hands-on: students pick a local site (urban park, industrial area, wildland), propose sampling design, predict results based on pollution sources. Integrates ecology, chemistry, and spatial thinking.
- **Lichen "speed dating"** — Rapid-fire presentation: 10 lichen species, 30 seconds each, covering genus, growth form, cool fact. Builds taxonomic literacy and surface area for curiosity.
