# Food Chemistry — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Textbooks

- **Harold McGee, "On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen"** (2004 revised edition) — The definitive text for food chemistry. Encyclopedic coverage from molecular mechanisms to cultural history. Used in university courses at Coast Community College and others. Combines rigorous chemistry with accessible prose. Essential reading for intermediate students.
  - Wikipedia overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_McGee
  - Journal review: https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/ed080p880.1

- **Tom Coultate, "Food: The Chemistry of its Components"** (7th edition) — Comprehensive academic textbook used in food science programs. More technical than McGee; good for deeper dives into specific compounds and reactions.

- **Provost, Colabroy, Kelly, and Wallert, "The Science of Cooking: Understanding the Biology and Chemistry Behind Food and Cooking"** — Used at UT Tyler. Blends biology with chemistry; good for students interested in fermentation and enzymatic processes.

- **Fennema's Food Chemistry** (edited by Damodaran, Parkin, and Fennema, CRC Press) — Reference used at Rutgers. Graduate-level depth; use for advanced topics.

## Unexpected Cross-Discipline Connections

### Materials Science
- **Soft matter physics** — Gels, foams, emulsions are all soft matter systems. Understanding colloidal physics helps explain food texture. EdX course "Science & Cooking: From Haute Cuisine to Soft Matter Science" makes this connection explicit.

### Neuroscience
- **Sensory perception and psychophysics** — How we perceive flavor is neuroscience: receptor binding, signal transduction, brain integration. Cross-modal effects (color changes taste perception, sound affects texture perception).

### Chemical Engineering
- **Heat and mass transfer** — Convection, conduction, radiation in ovens; diffusion of salt into meat; water evaporation from bread crust. Chemical engineering principles govern cooking.

### Microbiology and Evolution
- **Fermentation ecology** — Wild fermentation is microbial succession and competition. Sourdough starters are evolving ecosystems. Cheese caves have unique microbiomes.

### Anthropology
- **Cultural food chemistry** — Different cuisines evolved to optimize local ingredients' chemistry. Nixtamalization (treating corn with lime) was discovered independently because it improves nutrition and flavor. Fermentation techniques (kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, tempeh) converge on similar chemistry across cultures.

### Organic Chemistry
- **Functional group reactivity** — Carbonyl groups in sugars undergo Maillard reaction; hydroxyl groups affect polarity; carboxylic acids determine pH. Understanding functional groups unlocks predicting reactivity.

### Polymer Science
- **Gluten as a polymer network** — Bread dough is a viscoelastic polymer. Starch gelatinization is polymer swelling. Meat connective tissue is collagen polymer breaking down to gelatin.

### Thermodynamics
- **Phase transitions and entropy** — Water boiling, ice cream freezing, sugar crystallizing are all phase transitions. Entropy drives protein denaturation (exposing hydrophobic residues increases system entropy).

## Tools for Experimentation

### Kitchen Equipment as Lab Tools
- **Instant-read thermometer** — Essential for understanding temperature-dependent reactions (protein coagulation, Maillard onset, sugar stages)
- **pH test strips or meter** — Measure acidity in pickles, sourdough, sauces
- **Kitchen scale** — Precision for experiments (boiling point elevation, sugar solutions)
- **Sous vide circulator** — Temperature control for protein denaturation experiments
- **Microscope** (optional) — Observe starch granules, fat emulsions, protein networks (can use smartphone microscope adapter)

### No specialized lab equipment needed — this is the beauty of food chemistry. Your kitchen is the lab, and you can eat the experimental results.

## Quick Reference: Key Concepts and Temperatures

- **Protein denaturation:** begins around 40°C (egg white), complete by 80°C
- **Maillard reaction:** significant above 140°C, optimal 140-180°C
- **Caramelization:** begins 160°C, full range 160-190°C
- **Water boiling:** 100°C at sea level (decreases with altitude)
- **Sugar stages:** thread (110°C), soft ball (115°C), hard ball (125°C), soft crack (135°C), hard crack (150°C)
- **Starch gelatinization:** varies by starch type, typically 60-80°C
- **Enzymatic browning:** occurs at room temp when cells are damaged (polyphenol oxidase)
- **Optimal bread baking:** 175-220°C (crust formation, oven spring, Maillard)
- **Sous vide meat:** 55°C (rare), 60°C (medium rare), 65°C (medium)
