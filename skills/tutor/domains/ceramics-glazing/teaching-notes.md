# Ceramics Glazing Chemistry — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Glaze chemistry sits at the intersection of art and science — students are typically drawn to ceramics for creative reasons, but mastering glazes requires genuine chemical understanding. The key pedagogical challenge is to **ground abstract chemistry concepts in tangible, visible outcomes** that students can see and touch. Every chemical principle should connect to a practical question: "Will this glaze run? Why is it blue? How do I make it matte?"

At the intermediate level, students should already have hands-on glazing experience, so we leverage their practical intuition as a foundation for theory. This is a **practice-driven theory** approach: start with phenomena they've observed (crazing, color shifts, surface differences), then explain the chemistry that causes them. Use real glaze recipes as case studies rather than abstract examples. Encourage systematic testing — this is an experimental science, not a cookbook.

The curriculum is deliberately calculation-heavy in the middle (lessons 11-16) because **fluency with Unity Molecular Formula is the unlock**. Once students can read oxide ratios, they gain x-ray vision into glazes. Before UMF, glazes are mysterious recipes; after UMF, they're analyzable systems.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Glaze chemistry is about memorizing recipes"**
   - **Why students think this**: Most beginners use published recipes without understanding them; chemistry seems like advanced trivia
   - **How to correct**: Show two glazes with identical visual results but completely different oxide compositions. Emphasize that recipes are starting points, not endpoints — the chemistry lets you modify and troubleshoot.

2. **"Weight percentages tell you everything you need to know"**
   - **Why students think this**: Recipes are written in grams and percentages; seems complete
   - **How to correct**: Compare a recipe with 10% feldspar vs. 10% silica — same weight percentage, radically different chemical contribution. Molecular formula reveals what's actually happening.

3. **"Silica is the 'main ingredient' because it's usually the largest percentage"**
   - **Why students think this**: Silica often dominates by weight (40-60%)
   - **How to correct**: Show that in UMF, silica is typically 2-4 moles (in the RO₂ position) while RO fluxes sum to 1.0 by definition. Emphasize that **molar ratios matter, not weight**. Silica provides structure, but fluxes make it work.

4. **"You need different chemistry knowledge for low-fire, mid-range, and high-fire"**
   - **Why students think this**: Different temperature ranges use different recipes
   - **How to correct**: Same principles apply everywhere; only the specific flux choices change. Low-fire uses stronger fluxes (sodium, boron), high-fire uses weaker fluxes (calcium, potassium). The oxide roles are universal.

5. **"Matte glazes are just underfired glossy glazes"**
   - **Why students think this**: Incomplete melting looks matte
   - **How to correct**: Explain the three types of matte: alumina matte (high Al₂O₃ creates viscosity), crystalline matte (micro-crystals scatter light), and saturation matte (insufficient silica). Show examples of properly-fired mattes with smooth, functional surfaces.

6. **"Colorants are just decorative — they don't affect the glaze chemistry"**
   - **Why students think this**: Colorants are added in small amounts (1-10%)
   - **How to correct**: Demonstrate that some colorants act as fluxes (copper, iron) or opacifiers (tin, zirconium), significantly changing melt behavior and surface. Heavy copper can make a glaze run; heavy rutile can turn it matte.

7. **"Reduction firing is required for 'real' pottery colors"**
   - **Why students think this**: Reduction produces dramatic effects (copper reds, celadons) that get attention
   - **How to correct**: Celebrate oxidation firing's clarity and range. Most historical ceramics are oxidation. Reduction is a specialized technique, not a hierarchy of quality.

8. **"Crazing means the glaze is defective and will leak"**
   - **Why students think this**: Crazing looks like failure; cracks seem wrong
   - **How to correct**: Distinguish aesthetic crazing (intentional, stable) from progressive crazing (functional problem). Explain that crazing in compression (glaze too small for body) is stable; crazing that continues to develop post-firing is a fit problem. Context matters: crazing on sculpture vs. food-safe dinnerware have different implications.

9. **"Software calculates glazes for you, so you don't need to understand the math"**
   - **Why students think this**: GlazeMaster, Glazy, and other tools automate calculations
   - **How to correct**: Agree that software is essential for efficiency, but explain that **understanding what the software is doing** lets you catch errors, interpret results, and make intelligent adjustments. Software can't tell you *what to change* — only what happens *if* you change something.

10. **"Each oxide has one specific effect"**
    - **Why students think this**: Learning materials often categorize oxides simply (e.g., "CaO is a flux")
    - **How to correct**: Teach that oxides have **primary roles** but also **secondary effects** that depend on concentration and context. CaO is a flux but also promotes matte surfaces at high levels. MgO is a flux at low levels but refractory at high levels. Context is everything.

## Level Adjustments

### For Beginners (if adapting down)
- Skip or simplify lessons 11-13 (calculation details); focus on understanding UMF conceptually, use software as a black box
- Remove lesson 15 (formula-to-recipe conversion) — too abstract for beginners
- Simplify eutectic discussion (lesson 9) to "fluxes work better together than alone"
- Focus on one temperature range (cone 5-6 or cone 9-10) rather than comparing across ranges
- Use more visual aids: color charts, fired test tiles, phase diagrams
- Reduce emphasis on limit formulas and Stull charts — these are reference tools, not learning content for beginners

### For Intermediate (current level)
- Balance hands-on practice with theoretical understanding
- Expect students to do manual UMF calculations at least once, then use software
- Emphasize troubleshooting and adjustment over perfect formulation
- Include real-world case studies from the student's own work or community glaze issues
- Encourage systematic testing (line blends, triaxial blends) as part of learning, not just exploration

### For Advanced (if adapting up)
- Add lessons on **phase diagrams** and **phase equilibria** (chemistry depth)
- Include **thermal expansion calculations** (COE prediction from oxide composition)
- Discuss **glaze durability testing** (acid resistance, leaching, food safety)
- Add **advanced colorant chemistry** (interference colors, metallic lusters, nanoparticle effects)
- Explore **alternative flux systems** (lithium, strontium, barium in depth)
- Include **glaze layering and interaction chemistry** (glaze-over-glaze effects)
- Discuss **slip and engobe chemistry** (midway between clay and glaze)
- Dive into **crystalline glaze formulation** (nucleation theory, cooling rate control)

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Tangents)

### When to drop each in:

- **The glaze iceberg analogy** (Lesson 1-2) — Most of a glaze's mass is silica and alumina, but the "tip of the iceberg" (fluxes, colorants) determines its personality. Like icebergs, the bulk provides structure, but the visible part gets all the attention.

- **Why ancient Chinese celadons used wood ash** (Lesson 6-7) — Wood ash contains calcium, potassium, and phosphorus — a natural flux package. Celadon glazes were discovered through ash landing on pottery in wood-fired kilns. This is eutectic chemistry happening by accident over centuries.

- **The feldspar family tree** (Lesson 6) — Feldspars are the workhorses of glazes. Show the series from albite (sodium) to orthoclase (potassium) to anorthite (calcium). Each is a natural eutectic of flux + alumina + silica, perfectly evolved for glaze chemistry.

- **Why lead was perfect (and terrible)** (Lesson 6-7) — Lead oxide (PbO) is the most powerful flux in ceramics: super low melting, brilliant gloss, intense color response. It was used for millennia because nothing else worked as well at low fire. Then we discovered it's toxic. This is a chemistry lesson and a history lesson: sometimes the best technical solution is unacceptable for other reasons.

- **The role of boron in solving the lead problem** (Lesson 7) — Boron oxide (B₂O₃) from borax and gerstley borate became the non-toxic alternative to lead for low-fire glazes. Boron is both a flux AND a glass former — a rare dual role. It's why modern commercial glazes work so well.

- **Phase diagrams as maps of possibility** (Lesson 9, 14) — Show a simple binary phase diagram (e.g., CaO-SiO₂). Explain that these diagrams map **every possible outcome** for a combination — solid, liquid, eutectic. Glaze formulation is navigation through these maps. Advanced students love this level of rigor.

- **The Stull chart as a cultural artifact** (Lesson 14) — Stull's chart from the 1920s mapped cone 8-10 glazes in 2D (CaO vs. SiO₂ with Al₂O₃ fixed). It was the first attempt to scientifically organize glaze chemistry. It's still useful, but also shows the limits of 2D thinking in a multi-dimensional system.

- **Why copper turns red in reduction** (Lesson 18) — Copper red glazes (sang de boeuf, oxblood) are among the most coveted ceramic colors. In reduction firing, Cu²⁺ (blue/green) reduces to Cu⁺ or Cu⁰ (red). The red comes from **metallic copper nanoparticles** suspended in the glass — essentially a glass-metal composite. This connects to modern materials science.

- **Chun glazes and opalescence** (Lesson 19-20) — Chun (Jun) glazes have a milky blue opalescence from **phase separation** — tiny droplets of one glass type suspended in another, scattering blue light (Rayleigh scattering, same physics as blue sky). This is advanced colloid chemistry hiding in ancient Chinese pottery.

- **Raku and thermal shock** (Lesson 22) — Raku firing involves pulling red-hot pots from the kiln and dunking them in water or combustibles. This creates **extreme thermal shock**. Successful raku glazes need very low expansion to survive. Crazing in raku is usually deliberate and extreme. This connects glaze fit to firing technique.

- **Shino glazes and carbon trapping** (Lesson 18-19) — American Shino glazes have orange/gray colors from **carbon trapped in the glaze** during firing. The glaze melts and seals before carbon can burn out, creating a ghost of the flame. This is reduction chemistry in solid state.

- **The titanium crystal family** (Lesson 20) — Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) can form spectacular crystals in glazes: rutile crystals (golden/brown streaks), titanium-calcium crystals (matte white), and perovskite structures. Each has different formation chemistry. Great example of how one oxide creates multiple effects.

- **Glaze as a frozen liquid** (Lesson 5) — Glass is technically a **supercooled liquid**, not a true solid. At room temperature, it's so viscous it behaves like a solid, but at the atomic level, it lacks crystalline order. This is why glazes can "heal" small scratches when refired — they're amorphous, not crystalline.

- **Historical glaze archaeology** (Throughout) — Analyzing ancient glazes (Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese, Islamic) reveals their chemistry. We can reverse-engineer recipes from sherds using XRF or SEM. This connects ceramics to archaeology, art history, and cultural preservation.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

The difficulty curve is intentionally designed with these features:

- **Lessons 1-5**: Gentle entry (difficulty 1-2) — building conceptual foundations without heavy math
- **Lessons 6-9**: First peak (difficulty 3-4) — oxide chemistry requires chemical thinking
- **Lesson 10**: Review and consolidation (difficulty 1)
- **Lessons 11-15**: Calculation peak (difficulty 3-4) — hardest conceptual work, lots of new math
- **Lesson 16**: Review and tool exploration (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 17-20**: Applied chemistry (difficulty 3-4) — easier than calculations, but still conceptually demanding
- **Lesson 21**: Review and integration (difficulty 1)
- **Lessons 22-24**: Practical application (difficulty 3-4) — troubleshooting and testing
- **Lesson 25**: Capstone project (difficulty 5) — integrates everything

This creates a **wave pattern**: build → review → build → review → build → review → integrate. Students need consolidation time after each conceptual push.

## Teaching Style Notes

- **Use real recipes** — never teach with "Recipe A" or abstract examples. Use actual glazes with names and histories.
- **Test tiles are truth** — when theory conflicts with test results, trust the test. Glaze chemistry is empirical.
- **Encourage note-taking on fired results** — systematic documentation is part of the discipline
- **Normalize failure** — most glaze tests don't work. The winners emerge from testing dozens of variations.
- **Celebrate surprises** — unexpected results (happy accidents) are learning opportunities and creative fuel
- **Connect to materials** — always tie oxides back to the actual materials students can buy (feldspars, frits, carbonates, etc.)
- **Show diversity** — feature glazes from multiple cultures and traditions (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Islamic, European, contemporary)
- **Demystify commercial glazes** — explain that commercial glazes follow the same chemistry; they're not magic, just well-tested
