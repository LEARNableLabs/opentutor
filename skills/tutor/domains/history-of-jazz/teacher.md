# History of jazz — improvisation as a practice — Domain Teaching Config

## Exercise Types
This domain uses a mix of delivery formats:
- **concept-focused mini-lessons** — 8 lessons (33%)
- **Socratic questions** — 4 lessons (17%)
- **review and consolidation sessions** — 4 lessons (17%)
- **real-world application challenges** — 3 lessons (13%)
- **curated resource exploration** — 3 lessons (13%)

Primary format: concept-focused mini-lessons.

## Domain Hooks
- **Jazz and technology** — How did recording change improvisation? Players could study solos repeatedly, creating the modern transcription practice. The LP format (vs. 78s) allowed longer solos, enabling new narrative structures. Drop in when discussing the transition from swing to bebop.

- **Improvisation and race** — Bebop emerged partly as a reaction to white appropriation of swing. The harmonic complexity and fast tempos were barriers to commercial co-option. Free jazz connected to Black Arts Movement and civil rights. Drop in during bebop and free jazz units.

- **Cross-cultural influences** — Gillespie's Afro-Cuban innovations reconnected jazz to African and Caribbean rhythms. Coltrane's study of Indian music and modal thinking. Contemporary fusion with hip-hop, electronic music. Drop in when discussing rhythmic displacement (lesson 13) and contemporary synthesis (lesson 22).

- **Institutional vs. autodidactic learning** — Early jazz: purely oral tradition. Post-Tristano: form

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"Improvisation means making it up randomly"** — Students often miss that jazz improvisation is deeply structured by form, harmony, rhythm, and idiomatic language. Counter by showing how even "free jazz" has internal coherence and shared vocabulary. Emphasize that improvisation is **spontaneous composition**, not random playing.

2. **"You need to master all theory before you can improvise"** — This blocks the aural, experimental learning that historically characterized jazz. Early players didn't have theory books — they learned by ear. Counter with Lennie Tristano's pedagogy: transcription and ear training come first, theory follows to explain what you're hearing.

3. **"Each era replaced the previous one"** — Students think bebop "killed" swing, modal "killed" bebop, etc. In reality, all approaches coexist and inform each other. Counter by showing contemporary players who synthesize styles (e.g., Brad Mehldau combines bebop lines, modal thinking, and free harmonic exploration).
