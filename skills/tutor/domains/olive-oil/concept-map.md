# Olive Oil — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Quality spectrum** — olive oil grades from lampante to extra virgin, based on chemistry and sensory
2. **Free fatty acids** — primary chemical quality marker; EVOOs must be ≤0.8%
3. **Sensory defects** — off-flavors that disqualify oil from extra virgin status
4. **Traditional vs. modern production** — historical methods vs. centrifugation and controlled processing
5. **Cultivar diversity** — 500+ olive varieties with distinct chemical and sensory profiles
6. **Regional characteristics** — geographic patterns in flavor driven by cultivar selection and terroir
7. **Terroir** — how soil, climate, and microclimate shape olive and oil composition. Depends on: cultivar diversity
8. **Major cultivars** — Arbequina, Picual, Koroneiki, Frantoio, Coratina and their defining traits. Depends on: cultivar diversity, regional characteristics
9. **Commercial viability** — why certain cultivars dominate (yield, disease resistance, market demand). Depends on: major cultivars
10. **Fatty acid structure** — saturated vs. unsaturated bonds, double bond chemistry
11. **Oleic acid and mono-unsaturation** — C18:1 as dominant fatty acid in olive oil. Depends on: fatty acid structure
12. **Phenolic compounds** — hydroxytyrosol, oleuropein, tyrosol; antioxidant and sensory effects. Depends on: fatty acid structure
13. **Phenolic bitterness** — chemical basis for bitter taste as quality indicator. Depends on: phenolic compounds
14. **Fatty acid profile interpretation** — reading GC analysis, comparing to standards. Depends on: oleic acid and mono-unsaturation
15. **Oxidation mechanisms** — autoxidation, peroxide formation, rancidity development. Depends on: fatty acid structure
16. **Pungency and oleocanthal** — throat burn as indicator of phenolic content and freshness. Depends on: phenolic compounds
17. **Panel testing methodology** — blind evaluation, controlled conditions, standardized protocol
18. **Positive sensory attributes** — fruitiness, bitterness, pungency as quality markers. Depends on: phenolic bitterness, pungency and oleocanthal
19. **Sensory defects identification** — fusty, rancid, muddy, winey-vinegary detection. Depends on: sensory defects, oxidation mechanisms
20. **IOC tasting protocol** — blue glass, temperature control, scoring sheet. Depends on: panel testing methodology
21. **IOC scoring system** — median-based panel scores, defect vs. attribute intensity. Depends on: positive sensory attributes, panel testing methodology
22. **Harvest timing and maturity** — ripeness index, phenolic evolution during maturation. Depends on: phenolic compounds, major cultivars
23. **Cultivar-chemistry-sensory integration** — predicting sensory profile from variety and production. Depends on: major cultivars, phenolic compounds, positive sensory attributes, harvest timing
24. **Quality excellence** — balance, complexity, typicity as hallmarks of great oil. Depends on: all prior concepts

## Dependencies

### Critical Path
The curriculum follows a staged dependency structure:

**Foundations (Lessons 1-3)** → **Cultivars (4-8)** → **Chemistry (10-14)** → **Sensory (16-20)** → **Integration (22-24)**

- **Sensory attributes** require understanding **chemistry** because bitterness and pungency are explained by phenolic compounds and oleocanthal
- **Integration lessons** require understanding **all three domains** because harvest timing affects phenolic chemistry, which determines sensory intensity, and cultivar sets baseline potential
- **Fatty acid profile interpretation** requires understanding **fatty acid structure** and **oleic acid** because you're comparing measured values to expected ranges
- **Defect identification** requires understanding **oxidation mechanisms** because rancidity is the sensory manifestation of chemical degradation
- **IOC scoring** requires understanding **positive attributes** and **defects** because the scoring system balances attribute intensity against defect presence

### Bottleneck Concepts

1. **Phenolic compounds** — this is the conceptual bottleneck that connects chemistry to sensory. Students must understand that phenolics cause both positive attributes (bitterness, pungency) and health benefits. Without this, the sensory module won't make chemical sense.

2. **Cultivar-chemistry-sensory integration** — this meta-concept requires understanding all three domains. Students who struggle to integrate will see each area as isolated facts rather than interconnected system.

3. **Quality excellence vs. quality standards** — students must distinguish between "meets EVOO specs" (technical pass/fail) and "great oil" (subjective excellence). This requires both chemistry knowledge and sensory discrimination.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic organic chemistry** — needed for fatty acid structure, saturation, oxidation mechanisms
- **Food science fundamentals** — needed for understanding shelf life, processing effects, quality degradation
- **Sensory evaluation basics** — needed for panel methodology, bias elimination, discrimination testing
- **Basic statistics** — needed for understanding median calculation in IOC scoring (light requirement)

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Bitter = bad"** — students from culinary backgrounds may think bitterness is a defect. Reality: bitterness is a positive attribute indicating phenolic content and freshness.

2. **"Green = better"** — students may think greener color means higher quality. Reality: color is controlled by chlorophyll and not correlated with quality; IOC bans clear glasses for this reason.

3. **"Higher price = higher quality"** — students may conflate market price with technical quality. Reality: branding, packaging, and distribution costs often dominate price.

4. **"All EVOOs are the same"** — students may not realize the enormous diversity in cultivar-driven flavor profiles, from delicate to intense.

5. **"Chemistry determines everything"** — students may underweight sensory analysis once they learn chemical tests. Reality: sensory panel is required for EVOO certification; chemistry alone is insufficient.

6. **"Oxidation is binary"** — students may think oil is either fresh or rancid. Reality: oxidation is gradual; peroxide value peaks then declines as secondary oxidation products form.

## Learning Sequence Rationale

- **Foundations first** — establish quality framework before diving into technical details
- **Cultivars before chemistry** — students need concrete examples (cultivar names, regional oils) before abstract concepts (fatty acids, phenolics)
- **Chemistry before sensory** — chemical understanding makes sensory attributes meaningful rather than arbitrary
- **Integration last** — requires mastery of all three domains to predict how changes in one area affect others
- **Reviews every 6-7 lessons** — spaced repetition at lessons 7, 13, and 20 for optimal retention
