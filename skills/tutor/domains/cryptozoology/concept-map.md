# Cryptozoology as Pseudoscience — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Pseudoscience** — Claims that mimic science but lack its methodological rigor
2. **Demarcation Problem** — The philosophical challenge of drawing the boundary between science and non-science
3. **Falsifiability** — Popper's criterion: a claim must be testable and potentially disprovable. Depends on: Demarcation Problem
4. **Burden of Proof** — The responsibility to provide evidence lies with the claimant, not the skeptic. Depends on: Falsifiability
5. **Extraordinary Claims** — Carl Sagan's principle: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Depends on: Burden of Proof
6. **Evidence Types** — Empirical vs. anecdotal; controlled vs. uncontrolled; reproducible vs. one-off. Depends on: Burden of Proof
7. **Eyewitness Testimony** — Human memory and perception are fallible; witness accounts are weak scientific evidence. Depends on: Evidence Types
8. **Null Hypothesis** — Default position: the claim is false until proven true. Depends on: Burden of Proof
9. **Confirmation Bias** — Tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms pre-existing beliefs. Depends on: Pseudoscience
10. **Pareidolia** — Pattern recognition that leads us to see meaningful images in random stimuli. Depends on: Confirmation Bias
11. **Motivated Reasoning** — Cognitive process where emotions and desires shape our logical reasoning. Depends on: Confirmation Bias
12. **False Analogy** — The coelacanth fallacy: "one thing was rediscovered, therefore all cryptids exist." Depends on: Evidence Types
13. **Base Rates** — Prior probability matters: large land mammals are very unlikely to go undiscovered. Depends on: False Analogy
14. **Absence of Evidence** — When should lack of findings count as evidence of absence? Depends on: Base Rates, Evidence Types
15. **Authority Bias** — Why the "Surgeon's Photograph" was believed for decades. Depends on: Confirmation Bias
16. **Hoax Anatomy** — How hoaxes exploit cognitive biases and media dynamics. Depends on: Confirmation Bias, Authority Bias
17. **False Balance** — Media practice of giving "equal time" to unequal positions. Depends on: Burden of Proof
18. **Media Economics** — Financial incentives that drive sensational coverage. Depends on: False Balance
19. **Compartmentalization** — How intelligent people can hold irrational beliefs in specific domains. Depends on: Motivated Reasoning
20. **Metacognition** — Thinking about your own thinking; awareness of your biases. Depends on: Confirmation Bias, Motivated Reasoning
21. **Intellectual Humility** — Recognizing the limits of your knowledge and being open to revision. Depends on: Metacognition

## Dependencies

- **Falsifiability requires understanding the Demarcation Problem** — You can't judge whether a claim is testable without first understanding what separates science from pseudoscience.
- **Burden of Proof builds on Falsifiability** — Once we establish that claims must be falsifiable, it follows that the claimant must provide testable evidence.
- **Confirmation Bias explains why pseudoscience persists** — Understanding this bias illuminates how believers interpret ambiguous evidence as supporting their claims.
- **Pareidolia and Motivated Reasoning extend Confirmation Bias** — These are specific mechanisms through which bias operates in cryptozoology.
- **False Analogy and Base Rates work together** — The coelacanth argument fails both because it's a logical fallacy AND because it ignores prior probabilities.
- **Hoax Anatomy requires understanding multiple biases** — Successful hoaxes exploit authority bias, confirmation bias, and media dynamics simultaneously.
- **Metacognition depends on understanding biases** — You can't monitor your own thinking for bias until you know what biases exist.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic Scientific Method** — needed for Evidence Types, Null Hypothesis, Falsifiability
- **Logical Reasoning** — needed for False Analogy, Demarcation Problem
- **Probability & Statistics (light)** — needed for Base Rates, Absence of Evidence
- **Critical Thinking Fundamentals** — needed for all cognitive bias concepts

## Key Bottlenecks

1. **Falsifiability** (Lesson 2) — Students often struggle to distinguish "unfalsified" from "unfalsifiable." This is the gateway to understanding burden of proof.
2. **Confirmation Bias** (Lesson 17) — Recognizing this in others is easy; recognizing it in yourself is hard. This concept unlocks metacognition.
3. **False Analogy** (Lesson 13) — The coelacanth argument is persuasive until you understand base rates. This is where probabilistic thinking becomes crucial.
4. **Motivated Reasoning** (Lesson 19) — This is the deepest concept — it explains why smart people defend bad ideas. Requires integration of all prior bias concepts.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence"** — True in general, but when we've searched extensively in the right places, absence of evidence IS meaningful. (Addressed in Lesson 13)
- **"Science can't explain everything, so cryptids might exist"** — Conflates "unknown" with "unknowable" and "not proven false" with "true." (Addressed in Lessons 2, 5)
- **"Eyewitnesses all describe the same creature, so it must be real"** — Ignores cultural contagion, confirmation bias, and leading questions. (Addressed in Lessons 8, 16)
- **"Scientists are closed-minded about cryptids"** — Confuses skepticism with dogmatism; burden of proof ≠ refusal to investigate. (Addressed in Lessons 6, 14)
- **"The coelacanth proves cryptids exist"** — False analogy; ignores that we had fossil evidence of coelacanths and why large land mammals are different. (Addressed in Lessons 13, 14)
