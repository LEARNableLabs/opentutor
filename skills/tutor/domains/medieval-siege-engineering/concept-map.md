# Medieval Siege Engineering — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Siege warfare context** — why castles required specialized equipment to assault
2. **Defensive architecture** — features that made castles resistant to direct assault
3. **Tactical problem space** — offensive objectives (breach, suppress, demoralize) and constraints (time, resources, casualties)
4. **Projectile motion** — parabolic trajectory of thrown objects, relationship between angle, velocity, and range
5. **Energy transfer** — conversion between potential, kinetic, and elastic energy in mechanical systems
6. **Mechanical advantage** — force multiplication through levers, pulleys, and other simple machines
7. **Energy-range tradeoffs** — how engine design balances projectile mass, range, and rate of fire
8. **Scaling laws** — why engines don't scale linearly with size; square-cube law and its implications
9. **Structural limits** — material strength, stress distribution, and failure modes in large wooden structures
10. **Torsion springs** — twisted rope or sinew as energy storage medium
11. **Ballista mechanics** — crossbow-like engines using torsion springs; Roman and medieval variants
12. **Elastic potential energy** — energy stored in deformed materials
13. **Technological regression** — loss of Roman engineering knowledge in early medieval period
14. **Knowledge transmission** — how technical knowledge spreads (or fails to spread) across cultures and time
15. **Traction trebuchet** — engine powered by human pullers; predecessor to counterweight designs
16. **Human power limits** — maximum force and endurance constraints on human-powered engines
17. **Force multiplication** — how trebuchet geometry amplifies human pulling force
18. **Team coordination** — synchronized pulling and its effect on power delivery
19. **Counterweight trebuchet** — engine powered by falling weight; culmination of medieval siege technology
20. **Gravitational potential energy** — energy stored in elevated mass
21. **Hybrid designs** — transitional engines combining human traction and counterweights
22. **Sling mechanics** — how the sling extends the effective throwing arm and controls release
23. **Release angle optimization** — tuning sling length and pivot position for maximum range
24. **Timing precision** — empirical methods for achieving consistent release point
25. **Empirical optimization** — trial-and-error tuning without mathematical models
26. **Parameter tuning** — adjusting counterweight mass, sling length, axle height, etc.
27. **Material properties** — strength, flexibility, and durability of woods, ropes, and metals
28. **Wood selection** — choosing timber species for specific structural roles
29. **Structural engineering** — load paths, bracing, and reinforcement in large frames
30. **Failure analysis** — identifying weak points and predicting failure modes
31. **Stress concentration** — where forces focus in a structure (joints, transitions)
32. **Biological warfare** — throwing diseased animals or corpses to spread plague
33. **Psychological tactics** — terror weapons and morale attacks
34. **Counter-battery fire** — using defensive engines to destroy attacking engines
35. **Range estimation** — pre-modern methods for gauging distance and aiming
36. **Combined siege operations** — coordinating multiple engine types and infantry assault
37. **Technological obsolescence** — how gunpowder artillery supplanted mechanical engines
38. **Gunpowder revolution** — shift from mechanical to chemical energy in warfare
39. **Experimental archaeology** — modern reconstruction and testing of historical engines

## Dependencies

- **Projectile motion** requires basic physics (external prerequisite: kinematics)
- **Energy transfer** builds on projectile motion — understanding that range depends on energy delivery
- **Mechanical advantage** is needed to understand how levers and arms multiply force
- **Energy-range tradeoffs** depends on energy transfer and mechanical advantage — design choices emerge from physics constraints
- **Scaling laws** requires structural limits — understanding why bigger isn't always better
- **Torsion springs** need elastic potential energy — the energy storage mechanism
- **Ballista mechanics** combines torsion springs + mechanical advantage + projectile motion — first complete engine type
- **Technological regression** builds on ballista mechanics — can't understand what was lost without knowing what Romans achieved
- **Traction trebuchet** requires force multiplication and mechanical advantage — human power made effective through geometry
- **Team coordination** depends on traction trebuchet understanding — coordination problems unique to this engine type
- **Counterweight trebuchet** combines gravitational potential energy + sling mechanics + all prior trebuchet concepts
- **Hybrid designs** require understanding both traction and counterweight engines — transitional forms
- **Sling mechanics** is critical to counterweight trebuchet performance — the sling is what makes it work
- **Release angle optimization** depends on sling mechanics and projectile motion — bringing together geometry and ballistics
- **Empirical optimization** builds on parameter tuning concepts — how medieval engineers worked without modern math
- **Material properties** is foundational to structural engineering — can't build if you don't know what materials can do
- **Wood selection** depends on material properties and structural engineering — matching material to role
- **Failure analysis** requires structural limits + stress concentration + scaling laws — predicting what breaks
- **Stress concentration** builds on structural engineering — understanding where loads focus
- **Biological warfare** and **psychological tactics** depend on understanding projectile capabilities — what you can throw determines tactical options
- **Counter-battery fire** requires range estimation + all engine types — defensive use of offensive weapons
- **Combined siege operations** integrates all engine types + tactical problem space — synthesis of technical and tactical knowledge
- **Technological obsolescence** requires understanding counterweight trebuchet peak performance — can't judge obsolescence without knowing the baseline
- **Gunpowder revolution** compares mechanical vs chemical energy — why chemistry beat mechanics
- **Experimental archaeology** depends on all prior concepts — modern testing validates historical understanding

## Critical Path (bottleneck concepts)

1. **Energy transfer** — if students don't grasp energy conversion, all engine types will be opaque
2. **Mechanical advantage** — foundational to understanding why these machines work
3. **Scaling laws** — crucial for understanding why larger engines face different problems
4. **Sling mechanics** — the trebuchet doesn't make sense without understanding the sling's role
5. **Empirical optimization** — without this, students miss how medieval engineers actually worked

## Common Conceptual Bottlenecks

- **Square-cube law** — students often think scaling is linear ("twice as big = twice as powerful")
- **Energy efficiency** — confusion about where energy is lost (friction, air resistance, flex in structure)
- **Release timing** — difficulty grasping how sling length determines release point without calculus
- **Material selection** — not appreciating that different woods have very different properties
- **Tactical context** — viewing engines as standalone weapons rather than parts of combined operations

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic physics** (projectile motion, energy, forces) — needed for energy transfer, mechanical advantage, scaling laws
- **Medieval history overview** — needed for tactical problem space, technological regression, combined operations
- **Simple machines** (levers, pulleys) — needed for mechanical advantage, force multiplication
- **Basic material science** (optional but helpful) — makes material properties and wood selection more intuitive
