# Rhetoric and Persuasion — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Rhetoric is best taught through **analysis and application cycles**: students examine real persuasive texts (speeches, ads, op-eds, tweets), identify rhetorical strategies, then apply those strategies in their own composition. At the intermediate level, move beyond simple identification ("this is pathos") to evaluation ("why does this emotional appeal work for this audience but not that one?"). Rhetoric is inherently practical — every lesson should connect theory to performance. Use contemporary examples alongside classical texts to show that ancient frameworks still illuminate modern persuasion.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Rhetoric is just fancy words"** — Students think rhetoric = elaborate language or BS. Reality: rhetoric is strategic communication design. Plain language can be deeply rhetorical (think MLK's "I have a dream" — simple words, powerful structure). Correct by analyzing effective plain-style persuasion.

2. **"The three appeals are separate techniques"** — Students treat ethos, pathos, logos like discrete tools to sprinkle in. Reality: they're integrated dimensions of every act of persuasion. A single sentence can build credibility, evoke emotion, and advance logic simultaneously. Correct by showing how appeals interweave in strong examples.

3. **"Pathos is bad/manipulative"** — Students learn fallacies and become suspicious of emotional appeal. Reality: emotion is legitimate and necessary — humans are not purely logical. The question is whether emotion substitutes for logic or supports it. Correct by distinguishing ethical pathos (emotion + sound reasoning) from manipulation (emotion instead of reasoning).

4. **"Ethos is just credentials"** — Students reduce ethos to listing degrees or authority. Reality: ethos is about perceived character, goodwill, and trustworthiness, built through tone, fairness, and acknowledging counterarguments. A writer with no credentials can have strong ethos through demonstrated care and honesty. Correct by showing credibility-building moves beyond credentials.

5. **"Logical fallacies mean the conclusion is wrong"** — Students think spotting a fallacy disproves the claim. Reality: a fallacy means the reasoning is flawed, not that the conclusion is false. An ad hominem doesn't make someone's position wrong; it just means the attack isn't a reason to reject it. Correct by distinguishing flawed reasoning from false conclusions.

6. **"Rhetoric is for formal speeches and essays"** — Students miss rhetoric in everyday contexts. Reality: rhetoric operates in texts, emails, memes, product design, architecture. Every act of communication that seeks to influence is rhetorical. Correct by analyzing informal, visual, and digital rhetoric.

7. **"Good arguments are all logos"** — Students (especially STEM-oriented ones) dismiss ethos and pathos as fluff. Reality: even scientific papers build credibility and make readers care. Logic alone rarely persuades — you need trust and motivation. Correct by showing how scientific communication uses all three appeals.

8. **"Stasis theory is just about debate"** — Students see stasis (fact, definition, quality, policy) as a debate framework only. Reality: it's a discovery tool for any argument — helps identify where disagreement actually lies. Two people might argue past each other because one's on "definition" and one's on "policy." Correct by using stasis to diagnose everyday arguments.

9. **"The five canons are a checklist"** — Students treat invention, arrangement, style, memory, delivery as sequential steps to check off. Reality: they're interwoven — style choices affect arrangement, delivery shapes invention. They're lenses, not steps. Correct by showing how they interact in real composition.

10. **"Rhetorical analysis is subjective opinion"** — Students think identifying appeals or strategies is just interpretation without rigor. Reality: rhetorical analysis makes evidence-based claims about how texts work. You must point to specific textual features and explain their persuasive function. Correct by requiring textual evidence and functional explanations in all analyses.

## Level Adjustments

**Beginner level** would focus on identifying the three appeals in simple texts, learning basic fallacies, and practicing clear argument structure. Less theory, more pattern recognition.

**Intermediate level** (this curriculum) emphasizes understanding *why* strategies work — the relationship between rhetorical moves and context. Students should master classical frameworks (stasis, Toulmin, five canons) and apply them to analyze complex, multimodal texts. Expect some theoretical reading (selections from Aristotle, Burke) alongside practical analysis.

**Advanced level** would add rhetorical theory depth (sophistic rhetoric, Burke's dramatism, constitutive rhetoric), require original rhetorical criticism research, and explore specialized contexts (legal rhetoric, scientific rhetoric, activist rhetoric). More focus on producing sustained rhetorical criticism and less on basic skill-building.

At intermediate, balance theory and practice: don't drown them in classical texts, but don't skip the conceptual foundations. Use theory to illuminate practice, not as an end in itself.

## Rabbit Holes

- **Kenneth Burke's identification and dramatism** — when discussing pathos and audience, Burke's idea that persuasion works through identification (making audiences see themselves in the speaker) is a powerful extension. Introduce after lesson 9. His dramatistic pentad (act, scene, agent, agency, purpose) is a rich analysis tool for advanced students.

- **Cicero's *De Oratore* and the ideal orator** — when covering the five canons, Cicero's vision of the orator as a broadly educated cultural leader (not just a technician) adds historical depth and raises questions about rhetoric's civic role. Good for lesson 4 extension.

- **Visual rhetoric and the "rhetoric of the image"** — Roland Barthes' semiotic analysis of images as persuasive texts opens up advertising, photography, film as rhetorical objects. Perfect rabbit hole after lesson 24.

- **Algorithmic rhetoric** — Ian Bogost's "procedural rhetoric" argues that software and algorithms make arguments through their rule structures. Great extension of lesson 25 on digital rhetoric — how does TikTok's algorithm persuade?

- **Rhetoric of science** — examining how scientific claims are constructed rhetorically (Latour, Gross) challenges the myth that science is "just facts." Good for STEM-oriented students after lesson 17 on evidence.

- **Feminist and anti-colonial critiques of classical rhetoric** — interrogating whose voices and what modes of persuasion classical frameworks privilege. Relevant to lesson 26 on rhetorical ethics. Scholars like Jacqueline Jones Royster, Gloria Anzaldúa.

- **Generative AI as rhetor** — beyond detection and ethics (lesson 27), explore what happens when machines persuade, compose, and mimic human voice. Does an AI have ethos? What does authorship mean? Cutting-edge and generative for discussion.

## Difficulty Progression

Difficulty builds in three waves:

**Wave 1 (lessons 1-7)**: Establish foundations — relatively accessible conceptual material (rhetorical situation, five canons, stasis). Difficulty 1-2 with one spike to 3 for real-world application.

**Wave 2 (lessons 8-19)**: Core skill-building — the three appeals and argument structure. This is the heart of the curriculum. Difficulty 2-3 with peaks at 4 (Toulmin model, legal rhetoric analysis). Review at lesson 19 to consolidate before moving on.

**Wave 3 (lessons 20-28)**: Style and contemporary application — students apply integrated knowledge to delivery, visual rhetoric, digital contexts, and ethical questions. Difficulty 2-4, with the final synthesis project (lesson 28) at 4.

Review lessons (7, 19) drop to difficulty 1 to give consolidation time before the next wave.

The progression mirrors Bloom's taxonomy: early lessons focus on comprehension and application, middle lessons on analysis, late lessons on evaluation and creation.

## Engagement Hooks

- **Analyze persuasion in the wild**: political ads, Super Bowl commercials, viral tweets, TED talks. Students love dissecting real-world persuasion.
- **Debate classic rhetorical questions**: Is rhetoric amoral (just a tool) or does it have inherent ethics? Should emotional appeals be allowed in courtrooms?
- **Compose in constrained forms**: write a 280-character persuasive tweet, a 60-second elevator pitch, a one-paragraph op-ed. Constraints force rhetorical thinking.
- **Reverse-engineer famous speeches**: take MLK's "I Have a Dream," Lincoln's Getaway Address, or a modern commencement speech and map its appeals, structure, and devices.
- **Before/after revision**: show weak persuasive writing, have students diagnose rhetorical failures, then revise collaboratively.

## Supplementary Practice

Between lessons, students can:
- Maintain a "rhetoric in the wild" journal — collect and analyze one persuasive text daily
- Practice rhetorical precis writing — summarizing arguments by identifying rhetorical situation and strategy
- Deliver short persuasive speeches on low-stakes topics
- Analyze memes for visual and multimodal rhetoric
- Write opposing op-eds on the same issue to practice audience adaptation
