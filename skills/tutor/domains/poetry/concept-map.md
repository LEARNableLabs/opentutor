# Poetry — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Rhythm** — the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in language, exists in all speech
2. **Stress** — emphasis placed on certain syllables; the basic unit of English prosody
3. **Line break** — where a poet chooses to end a line; fundamental difference between poetry and prose
4. **Metrical foot** — a repeating unit of stressed/unstressed syllables. Depends on: rhythm, stress
5. **Scansion** — the notation and analysis of metrical patterns. Depends on: metrical foot, stress
6. **Iamb** — unstressed-stressed pattern (da-DUM); most common English meter. Depends on: metrical foot
7. **Trochee** — stressed-unstressed pattern (DUM-da); reversal of iamb. Depends on: metrical foot
8. **Iambic pentameter** — five iambs per line; Shakespeare's meter. Depends on: iamb, scansion
9. **Anapest** — two unstressed, one stressed (da-da-DUM); galloping rhythm. Depends on: metrical foot
10. **Dactyl** — one stressed, two unstressed (DUM-da-da); falling rhythm. Depends on: metrical foot
11. **Metrical substitution** — breaking the expected pattern for emphasis. Depends on: meter, scansion
12. **Closed/fixed form** — poetry with predetermined structure (rhyme, meter, length). Depends on: meter
13. **Sonnet** — 14-line form with specific rhyme scheme and turn. Depends on: closed form, iambic pentameter
14. **Villanelle** — 19-line form with two refrains rotating. Depends on: closed form
15. **Sestina** — 39-line form with end-word rotation instead of rhyme. Depends on: closed form
16. **Free verse** — poetry without regular meter or rhyme. Depends on: understanding what it rejects (closed form, meter)
17. **Enjambment** — continuation of a sentence beyond a line break. Depends on: line break
18. **Sound devices** — alliteration, assonance, consonance; repetition of sounds. Depends on: stress, rhythm
19. **Diction** — word choice; formal decisions about vocabulary. Depends on: close reading awareness
20. **Speaker** — the voice in the poem, not necessarily the poet. Depends on: dramatic situation
21. **Form-meaning relationship** — how structure shapes interpretation. Depends on: all formal elements + close reading

## Dependencies

### Foundational layer
- **Rhythm and stress** are prerequisites for everything else. Students must hear and feel these before notation.
- **Line breaks** distinguish poetry from prose; understanding why they matter opens up formal analysis.

### Scansion layer
- **Scansion** requires facility with identifying stress and recognizing repeated patterns.
- **Meter identification** builds on scansion practice; students must be comfortable marking feet before naming meters.
- **Metrical substitution** only makes sense after students internalize what the "normal" pattern is.

### Form layer
- **Fixed forms** depend on understanding meter and rhyme as constraints.
- **Sonnets** are accessible because iambic pentameter is familiar from earlier lessons.
- **Villanelle and sestina** introduce more complex constraint systems; villanelle is easier (rhyme-based), sestina harder (mathematical).
- **Free verse** requires understanding what it's free *from*; teach after fixed forms so students grasp the contrast.

### Close reading layer
- **Enjambment** builds on basic line-break awareness but requires understanding how syntax and line interact.
- **Sound devices** layer on top of stress/rhythm knowledge; students analyze *why* certain sounds are chosen.
- **Diction and speaker** introduce interpretive complexity; students move from formal description to meaning-making.

### Synthesis layer
- **Form-meaning relationship** is the culmination; students integrate all previous concepts to show how formal choices create or reinforce meaning.

## Key Bottlenecks

1. **Stress identification** — Some students can't hear stress patterns naturally. Solution: Use body (tap foot, clap, walk the rhythm) before notation.
2. **Scansion notation** — Symbol overload can intimidate. Solution: Start with just marking stressed syllables, add foot divisions later.
3. **Metrical substitution** — Students get confused when the pattern breaks. Solution: Teach the "rule" first, then show variations as meaningful departures.
4. **Form vs. meaning** — Students treat form as decorative rather than integral. Solution: Always ask "why did the poet choose this form for this subject?"
5. **Speaker ≠ poet** — Beginners conflate biographical poet with dramatic speaker. Solution: Use obvious persona poems (dramatic monologues) to establish the distinction.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic literary terminology** — needed for: close reading, sound devices, diction analysis (metaphor, simile, imagery, tone)
- **Reading comprehension** — needed for: understanding what the poem says before analyzing how it says it
- **Comfort with ambiguity** — needed for: interpretive work, multiple valid readings
- **Some exposure to poetry** — needed for: having a baseline; complete beginners may need pre-work

## Common Misconceptions (flagged for teaching-notes.md)

1. **All poems rhyme** — many modern poems don't; rhyme is one choice among many
2. **Meter is rigid** — actual poems vary the pattern; variation creates meaning
3. **Form is arbitrary** — form and content are inseparable; the structure *is* part of the meaning
4. **Close reading finds "the answer"** — poems resist single interpretations; rigor ≠ one right reading
5. **Free verse has no structure** — it lacks *regular* meter but has lineation, rhythm, sound patterning
6. **Scansion is mechanical** — it's interpretive; different readers may scan differently based on how they hear emphasis
7. **The poet's biography explains the poem** — the speaker is a construction; biographical context is useful but not determinative
