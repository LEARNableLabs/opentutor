# Competitive Rock Climbing — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Competitive rock climbing is a highly **embodied and experiential** discipline where theory must be grounded in physical practice. The curriculum balances conceptual understanding (energy systems, periodization models, mental frameworks) with practical application (designing training weeks, reading competition footage, self-assessment). For intermediate learners, assume basic movement literacy but limited exposure to systematic training or competition environments.

Use **visual resources heavily** — climbing is a visual-spatial skill, so route reading lessons should include competition footage, movement analysis videos, and diagram-based explanations. Training science concepts benefit from charts (periodization timelines, strength-endurance continuum diagrams) and real athlete examples. Mental training requires introspection and self-experiment prompts rather than pure theory.

The teaching progression moves from **perception → analysis → design → synthesis**: first learn to see patterns (route reading, movement efficiency), then understand mechanisms (energy systems, injury physiology), then design interventions (training blocks, warm-up protocols), finally integrate everything (full periodized plan with mental strategies and injury prevention). Reviews are crucial because concepts interconnect heavily — isolation zone management depends on energy system knowledge, injury prevention depends on load management from periodization.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Route reading is about memorizing every hold"
**Why students believe this:** Competition footage shows athletes staring at routes, and they assume that means rote memorization of sequences.

**Why it's wrong:** Route reading is pattern recognition and decision-making under uncertainty — identifying rest positions, crux sequences, and decision points. Trying to memorize everything creates cognitive overload.

**How to correct:** Use the "rest-to-rest" framework. Show competition footage and ask: "Where would you recover?" Then: "What's the hard part between those rests?" This shifts focus from holds to strategic sections.

### 2. "Training harder always produces better results"
**Why students believe this:** Cultural narrative in climbing emphasizes grit and high volume; beginner gains come from just climbing more.

**Why it's wrong:** Ignores supercompensation cycle and accumulation of fatigue. Intermediate climbers hit diminishing returns without structured recovery.

**How to correct:** Show the supercompensation curve explicitly. Assign a "design a recovery week" exercise. Use real examples of athletes who broke through by training less but smarter.

### 3. "Fingerboard training will injure your fingers"
**Why students believe this:** Stories of finger injuries circulate widely; controlled hanging feels risky compared to "natural" climbing movement.

**Why it's wrong:** Progressive, protocol-driven fingerboard training is often safer than random hard bouldering because load is measurable and controlled.

**How to correct:** Compare injury mechanisms: uncontrolled dynamic loading (campus, hard moves) vs. controlled static loading (hangboard). Introduce max hang protocols with conservative load progression. Emphasize autoregulation.

### 4. "Campus board is the fastest way to get strong"
**Why students believe this:** Elite climbers use campus boards; explosive movements feel powerful and productive.

**Why it's wrong:** Campus develops power (rate of force production), not maximum strength. Requires baseline strength to use safely. Many climbers need strength or endurance more than power.

**How to correct:** Introduce the strength-endurance continuum diagram. Assess where the student is deficient before prescribing tools. Make campus board conditional on demonstrated readiness (e.g., one-arm hangs on large holds).

### 5. "Flexibility isn't important for climbing"
**Why students believe this:** Many successful climbers aren't notably flexible; strength and technique seem sufficient for most routes.

**Why it's wrong:** Functional flexibility unlocks movement options, especially high steps, drop-knees, and heel hooks on steep terrain. Lack of hip mobility forces compensatory movement that wastes energy.

**How to correct:** Show specific movement problems that mobility solves (high step past a blank section, stemming rest). Focus on "functional flexibility" — range of motion needed for climbing, not gymnastic flexibility.

### 6. "Fear is a mental weakness"
**Why students believe this:** Cultural emphasis on bravery and "sending"; fear feels like personal failure.

**Why it's wrong:** Fear is a physiological response (fight-or-flight) that can be managed through exposure, breathing, and reframing, not eliminated through willpower.

**How to correct:** Explain the physiology of fear response. Introduce progressive exposure (fall practice, controlled scary routes). Reframe fear as information, not character flaw. Teach arousal management techniques.

### 7. "Flow state is mysterious and uncontrollable"
**Why students believe this:** Flow feels spontaneous and rare; discussion of flow often emphasizes serendipity.

**Why it's wrong:** Flow has identifiable trigger conditions (challenge-skill balance, clear goals, immediate feedback) that can be cultivated.

**How to correct:** Break down flow components. Have students identify past flow experiences and extract common conditions. Design experiments to recreate those conditions.

### 8. "Periodization is too complex for non-elites"
**Why students believe this:** Periodization literature is dense; seems like overkill for recreational climbers.

**Why it's wrong:** Basic periodization (strength phase → power phase → performance phase) prevents stagnation and overtraining. Simple > complex for most climbers.

**How to correct:** Start with minimal viable periodization: 4-week strength block, 2-week power block, 1-week taper, 1-week performance/test. Show how this beats random training.

### 9. "Pain always means stop training"
**Why students believe this:** Fear of injury; "listen to your body" advice taken to extreme.

**Why it's wrong:** Need to distinguish productive discomfort (muscle fatigue, skin soreness) from injury signals (sharp pain, joint instability, lasting pain after rest).

**How to correct:** Introduce pain signal taxonomy. Teach autoregulation based on pain type, not pain presence. Emphasize "training to pain, not through pain."

### 10. "Competition tactics are just about being strong enough"
**Why students believe this:** Assumption that physical preparation determines results; tactics seem secondary.

**Why it's wrong:** Effort distribution, time management, psychological state, and warm-up quality significantly affect performance independent of fitness.

**How to correct:** Show competition examples where tactics made the difference (strategic rest, problem order, observation time use). Have students design a competition day plan including warm-up, nutrition, mental prep, not just physical capacity.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Level (Current)
- **Depth:** Introduce training science with conceptual frameworks (energy systems, periodization models) but avoid dense physiological detail. Focus on practical application.
- **Formalism:** Use simple models (rest-to-rest route reading, basic block periodization) rather than advanced variants (undulating periodization, triphasic training).
- **Emphasis:** Balance systematic training with maintaining climbing enjoyment. Intermediate climbers risk over-systematizing and losing play. Include "wild card" lessons and play-oriented training.
- **Self-assessment:** Teach autoregulation and self-monitoring since intermediate climbers lack coach feedback. Emphasize developing internal calibration.

### Adjustments for Beginner Level
- **Simplify training science:** Focus on consistency and basic recovery rather than periodization. Energy system knowledge can be "short routes vs. long routes" rather than ATP-CP/anaerobic/aerobic.
- **More technique, less training:** Route reading and movement efficiency provide bigger gains than systematic strength training for beginners.
- **Injury prevention emphasis:** Build baseline durability before intensity. More conservative progressions.
- **Reduce competition focus:** Competition tactics less relevant; emphasize enjoying climbing and building practice habit.

### Adjustments for Advanced Level
- **Advanced periodization:** Introduce conjugate methods, undulating periodization, autoregulated progression models (RPE-based training).
- **Specificity:** Tailor to competition format (boulder vs. lead vs. speed) and individual limiters (strength vs. power vs. endurance vs. technical vs. mental).
- **Nuanced mental training:** Advanced arousal control, pre-performance routines, competitive mindset development.
- **Injury rehabilitation:** Not just prevention but managing chronic issues, return-to-climbing protocols, working around limitations.
- **Performance analysis:** Video analysis, force measurement (Tindeq), data-driven training decisions.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### When introducing energy systems (Lesson 6)
→ **Motor unit recruitment and the Henneman size principle** — why max hangs recruit high-threshold fibers while endurance climbing doesn't. Opens door to neural adaptations vs. structural adaptations.

### When discussing body position (Lesson 3)
→ **Physics of lever arms and moment forces** — why small hip adjustments create large changes in finger load. Can quantify with simple diagrams (center of mass, pivot points, force vectors).

### When teaching periodization (Lesson 8)
→ **Soviet sports science history and block periodization origins** — Verkhoshansky's work with track athletes, adapted to climbing by the Andersons. Historical context makes theory memorable.

### When covering fingerboard protocols (Lesson 10)
→ **Tendon adaptation timelines vs. muscle adaptation** — tendons adapt slowly (months) while muscles adapt quickly (weeks), explaining injury risk from rapid progression. Connects to rehab protocols.

### When exploring fear management (Lesson 17)
→ **Polyvagal theory and vagal tone** — breathing techniques work by engaging parasympathetic nervous system. Explains why breath control reduces fear response.

### When discussing visualization (Lesson 18)
→ **Mirror neurons and mental practice research** — visualizing movement activates similar neural pathways to actual execution. Explains why mental rehearsal improves performance.

### When teaching campus board (Lesson 11)
→ **Rate coding vs. recruitment in force production** — why explosive movements train rate of force development distinct from maximum force. Connects to power endurance vs. pure power.

### When covering flow state (Lesson 20)
→ **Challenge-skill balance and the flow channel** — Csikszentmihalyi's research on optimal experience. Can diagram anxiety/boredom zones around flow channel. Applies beyond climbing.

### When introducing pulley injuries (Lesson 24)
→ **Extracellular matrix remodeling and mechanotransduction** — how tissues adapt to stress at cellular level. Explains why progressive loading prevents injury while sudden spikes cause damage.

### When discussing competition psychology (Lesson 19)
→ **Yerkes-Dodson law of arousal and performance** — inverted U-curve showing optimal arousal level. Too little = boredom, too much = anxiety, middle = peak performance. Visual and memorable.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

- **Lessons 1-5 (Route Reading):** Start accessible (difficulty 2-3) since visual skills are intuitive. Peak at lesson 3 with body position analysis (spatial reasoning challenge).

- **Lessons 6-9 (Training Science):** Ramp to difficulty 4 at lesson 8 (periodization) — most conceptually dense. Follow with review to consolidate.

- **Lessons 10-16 (Physical Training):** Mix difficulty (2-4) based on complexity. Limit bouldering and core (2) are intuitive; periodized week design (4) requires synthesis. Review at lesson 16.

- **Lessons 17-20 (Mental Training):** Moderate difficulty (2-3) — introspective rather than cognitively complex. Flow state (3) requires self-awareness.

- **Lessons 21-23 (Competition Tactics):** Peak at lesson 22 (difficulty 4) — time/effort management synthesizes route reading, energy systems, and mental training. Review follows.

- **Lessons 24-26 (Injury Prevention):** Moderate (3) with synthesis challenge at lesson 26 (difficulty 4) — final teach-back integrates entire curriculum.

## Teaching Tone

- **Practical and evidence-based:** Ground recommendations in research and expert practice, but avoid academic jargon. Cite sources (Lattice, Hörst, Anderson brothers) to build credibility.
- **Empowering, not prescriptive:** Teach frameworks for self-assessment and program design rather than rigid rules. Intermediate climbers need autonomy to experiment.
- **Normalize struggle:** Climbing is hard. Fear is normal. Plateaus happen. Injury risk is real. Acknowledge challenges rather than toxic positivity.
- **Playful when appropriate:** Climbing is play. Route reading can be game-like. Don't lose joy in systematization. Include creative variations in lessons.
