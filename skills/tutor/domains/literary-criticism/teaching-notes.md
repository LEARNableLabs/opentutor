# Literary Criticism — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Literary criticism at the intermediate level requires balancing **abstract theory with concrete practice**. Students need to understand philosophical principles while actually using them to read texts. The pedagogy should emphasize:

1. **Practice before abstraction** — always introduce a theoretical approach by demonstrating it on a specific text before explaining its philosophical foundations
2. **Comparison as learning tool** — students grasp theories best by seeing how different approaches read the same passage differently
3. **Historicizing theory** — presenting theories in roughly chronological order helps students see how each movement responds to or challenges previous ones

At this level, avoid getting lost in philosophical fine print (save Derrida's full argument about différance for advanced students) while also pushing beyond superficial application ("this is a Marxist reading because it mentions class"). Students should understand not just *what* each theory says but *why* it makes those assumptions and *when* it's most useful.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "New Criticism means any close reading"
**Why students get this wrong**: Close reading is taught early and associated with formalism, so students think all careful attention to text is "New Critical."

**How to correct**: Emphasize that New Criticism is a *specific historical movement* with particular principles (intentional fallacy, organic unity, autonomy of text). You can do close reading from any theoretical perspective — a Marxist close reading looks for ideology in language choices, a feminist close reading examines gendered language, etc.

### 2. "Deconstruction means texts don't mean anything"
**Why students get this wrong**: They hear "undecidability" and "instability" and think deconstruction is nihilistic or says "anything goes."

**How to correct**: Deconstruction reveals *how* texts create meaning through contradiction and instability, not that they lack meaning. A deconstructive reading is rigorous and text-based — it shows what the text is *doing* even as it undermines what it's *saying*. Use clear examples of aporia (moments where text contradicts itself) to show this is analysis, not chaos.

### 3. "Reader-response means my interpretation is as good as anyone's"
**Why students get this wrong**: Emphasizing reader's role sounds like subjective free-for-all.

**How to correct**: Introduce Stanley Fish's "interpretive communities" immediately. Reader-response doesn't mean individual whim — it means readers use shared conventions and strategies. Some interpretations are better supported by textual evidence and interpretive norms. The question shifts from "what does the text objectively mean?" to "what interpretive work are we doing, and with what shared assumptions?"

### 4. "The unconscious is just hidden meaning"
**Why students get this wrong**: Psychoanalytic criticism sounds like finding "secret messages" in texts.

**How to correct**: The unconscious in psychoanalytic criticism isn't hidden content but *structural operations* — repetition, displacement, condensation, return of repressed. It's not "the author secretly meant X" but "the text's language patterns reveal tensions that resist conscious intention." Show how Freudian slips or dream logic work in language itself.

### 5. "Feminist criticism is just looking for sexism"
**Why students get this wrong**: They conflate political critique with literary analysis, or see feminism as only negative critique.

**How to correct**: Show the range: gynocriticism (celebrating women's writing tradition), examining gender as constructed category, analyzing patriarchal structures *and* resistance to them, intersectional approaches. Feminist criticism includes recovery, celebration, critique, and revision. Not just "spot the sexism" but "how does this text construct, challenge, or complicate gender?"

### 6. "Theory replaces interpretation"
**Why students get this wrong**: They think applying a theory means following a formula or checklist.

**How to correct**: Theory *enables* interpretation by giving you tools and questions to ask. Two feminist readers will produce different feminist interpretations of the same text. Theory provides frameworks, assumptions, and methods — interpretation is still creative, rigorous work. Emphasize that theory makes you a *better* reader, not a mechanical one.

### 7. "Each theory is totally separate"
**Why students get this wrong**: Pedagogically, we often teach one theory per week, making them seem isolated.

**How to correct**: Constantly show connections and debates. New Historicism extends Marxist thinking; queer theory builds on feminist criticism; trauma theory combines psychoanalytic and historical approaches. Real critics mix theories. Encourage students to see theoretical conversations, not isolated boxes.

### 8. "Historical context = biographical context"
**Why students get this wrong**: They conflate New Historicism with author biography.

**How to correct**: New Historicism examines cultural circulation of ideas, power relations, discourse networks — not individual author's life story (that's the intentional fallacy!). It's about how texts participate in broader cultural conversations and power structures, not about what was happening in the author's life when they wrote.

### 9. "Postcolonial criticism only applies to colonial literature"
**Why students get this wrong**: The name suggests limited scope.

**How to correct**: Postcolonial criticism analyzes power, representation, othering, hybridity, and resistance in *any* context where imperial or hegemonic dynamics operate. It can read Shakespeare, contemporary American fiction, science fiction — anywhere power constructs "us" vs. "them." It's a critical lens, not just a historical period.

### 10. "Using theory means ignoring the text"
**Why students get this wrong**: They worry that focusing on theory means not attending to the literary work itself.

**How to correct**: The best theoretical criticism is grounded in textual evidence — close reading specific passages, attending to language, form, structure. Theory gives you *better* questions to ask of the text, more nuanced ways to read carefully. Show examples of excellent theoretical criticism that stays close to textual detail.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner Level
- Focus on 4-5 major approaches (formalism, reader-response, Marxist, feminist, one contemporary)
- Emphasize practical application over philosophical foundations
- Use accessible texts and clear examples
- Avoid dense theoretical readings; use explanatory secondary sources instead

### Intermediate Level (this curriculum)
- Cover 8-10 major approaches with historical progression
- Balance theory and practice — read primary theoretical texts (accessible selections) plus literary applications
- Introduce debates between approaches (formalism vs. reader-response, structuralism vs. deconstruction)
- Expect students to compare approaches and recognize their assumptions
- Some philosophical depth but focus remains on interpretive practice

### Advanced Level
- All major approaches plus contemporary developments (affect theory, digital humanities, new materialism)
- Read primary theoretical texts in full (Derrida, Foucault, Butler, Spivak)
- Engage with ongoing theoretical debates
- Expect original synthesis — combining multiple frameworks or developing new critical approaches
- More emphasis on theoretical foundations and philosophical rigor

### Adjustments for this curriculum's intermediate level:
- **Emphasize**: How each theory reads differently, why assumptions matter, when each approach is most useful
- **Include**: Brief selections from primary theorists (Barthes on death of author, Fish on interpretive communities) but not full theoretical works
- **Practice**: Comparing how 2-3 different theories read the same passage
- **Skip**: Dense philosophical background (Saussure's full linguistic theory, Hegel's dialectic, Lacan's complete psychoanalytic system)
- **Introduce**: Theoretical pluralism and synthesis as goals, not just mastery of individual approaches

## Rabbit Holes

### 1. **Saussure's linguistics and structuralist foundations**
When students grasp binary oppositions and signifier/signified, some want to dive deep into structural linguistics. This can be fascinating but derails literary focus. **When to drop this**: If student shows strong interest in language theory or is also studying linguistics. Otherwise, keep it brief.

### 2. **Derrida's full deconstructive project**
*Of Grammatology*, différance, trace, supplement — deconstruction's philosophical depth is immense. **When to drop this**: Advanced students or those doing philosophy alongside literature. For most, stick to deconstructive reading practice.

### 3. **Lacanian psychoanalysis**
Mirror stage, the Real/Imaginary/Symbolic, objet petit a — Lacan takes psychoanalytic criticism to complex heights. **When to drop this**: Students with strong psychoanalytic interest or those analyzing particularly psychological texts (Kafka, Pynchon). Otherwise, Freudian basics suffice.

### 4. **Foucault and power/knowledge**
Foucault's archaeology, genealogy, biopower, disciplinary society are endlessly rich. **When to drop this**: When discussing New Historicism or postcolonial criticism if student wants more on how power operates. Great for students interested in historical approach.

### 5. **Butler on gender performativity**
*Gender Trouble* is landmark but challenging. Full argument about drag, parody, subversion can be explored deeply. **When to drop this**: Students especially engaged with gender theory or queer theory. Great for those analyzing performance, identity, or contemporary gender issues.

### 6. **Said's Orientalism**
The full argument about discourse, power, representation is extremely influential beyond postcolonial studies. **When to drop this**: Students analyzing anything involving representation of otherness — not just colonial texts but any us/them dynamics.

### 7. **The theory wars and science wars**
The 1990s debates about theory's value, the Sokal hoax, arguments about objectivity and relativism. **When to drop this**: If students worry whether theory is "legitimate" or wonder about theory's relationship to truth and evidence. Fascinating historical moment.

### 8. **Digital humanities and distant reading**
Computational text analysis, topic modeling, network analysis of literary history. **When to drop this**: Students interested in data science, coding, or large-scale patterns. Good contemporary rabbit hole showing theory evolving.

### 9. **Affect theory and emotion studies**
The "affective turn" and how feeling differs from emotion in reading. **When to drop this**: Students interested in embodied reading, reader experience, or contemporary theory. Good bridge to neuroscience and cognitive approaches.

### 10. **Ecocriticism and the Anthropocene**
Deep time, environmental justice, nonhuman perspectives, climate fiction. **When to drop this**: Students engaged with environmental issues or science fiction. Shows how theory responds to contemporary urgency.

## Difficulty Progression

The curriculum difficulty follows this arc:

**Lessons 1-4 (Difficulty 1-2)**: Foundations are accessible, establishing basic concepts without heavy theory.

**Lessons 5-8 (Difficulty 2-3)**: New Criticism introduces first real theoretical framework. Difficulty rises as students encounter intentional/affective fallacy and formalist principles.

**Lessons 9-12 (Difficulty 3-4, then review at 2)**: Structuralism is conceptually challenging (binary oppositions, narratology). Review lesson helps consolidate before moving on.

**Lessons 13-15 (Difficulty 3)**: Reader-response provides somewhat more accessible shift after structural abstraction.

**Lessons 16-18 (Difficulty 4-3-2)**: Deconstruction is peak conceptual difficulty. Review lesson compares formalism and deconstruction to consolidate understanding.

**Lessons 19-22 (Difficulty 3-4)**: Political criticism builds to high difficulty, especially New Historicism and race theory which require understanding power and ideology.

**Lessons 23-25 (Difficulty 3-2-4)**: Psychoanalytic starts moderate, review consolidates, trauma theory is challenging.

**Lessons 26-29 (Difficulty 3-4)**: Gender and identity approaches build in complexity, especially performativity and queer theory.

**Lessons 30-31 (Difficulty 3-2)**: Ecocriticism is moderate, final synthesis review consolidates everything.

**Peak difficulty moments** (lesson 11, 16, 20, 21, 22, 25, 27, 28): Narratology, deconstruction, New Historicism, postcolonial criticism, race theory, trauma theory, gender performativity, queer theory — these are conceptually dense and require synthesizing multiple ideas.

**Strategic reviews** (lessons 7, 12, 18, 24, 31): Placed after clusters of difficult material to consolidate learning and practice comparing approaches.

The progression builds from concrete (close reading) to abstract (deconstruction) to applied (political and identity approaches) to synthetic (contemporary and pluralist). This follows how most students develop theoretical sophistication.
