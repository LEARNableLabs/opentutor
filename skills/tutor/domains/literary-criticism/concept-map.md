# Literary Criticism — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Interpretation** — the act of making meaning from a text through analysis
2. **Close reading** — careful attention to textual details, language, form, and structure
3. **Literary criticism vs. theory** — distinction between interpretive practice and philosophical frameworks
4. **Theoretical frameworks** — structured approaches/lenses for reading texts
5. **Intentional fallacy** — New Critical principle that author's intent is not the measure of meaning. Depends on: close reading
6. **Form and organic unity** — how structural elements create coherent meaning. Depends on: close reading
7. **Affective fallacy** — New Critical principle that reader's emotional response is not the measure of meaning. Depends on: intentional fallacy
8. **Text-centered analysis** — formalist approach focusing only on what's on the page. Depends on: close reading, form
9. **Structuralism** — approach viewing literature as a language system with deep structures. Depends on: theoretical frameworks
10. **Binary oppositions** — structural principle of meaning through contrast. Depends on: structuralism
11. **Semiotics** — study of signs, signifiers, and signified. Depends on: structuralism
12. **Narratology** — systematic study of narrative structures and techniques. Depends on: structuralism
13. **Reader-response criticism** — approach emphasizing reader's active role in creating meaning. Depends on: theoretical frameworks
14. **Interpretive communities** — groups sharing reading conventions and interpretive strategies. Depends on: reader-response criticism
15. **Reception theory** — how historical context shapes reading and meaning. Depends on: reader-response criticism, interpretive communities
16. **Deconstruction** — poststructuralist approach revealing textual contradictions and instability. Depends on: structuralism, binary oppositions
17. **Aporia** — moment of undecidability or self-contradiction in a text. Depends on: deconstruction
18. **Death of the author** — Barthesian concept that author's biography is irrelevant to meaning. Depends on: deconstruction
19. **Intertextuality** — how texts reference and depend on other texts. Depends on: death of the author
20. **Marxist criticism** — approach analyzing literature through class struggle and ideology. Depends on: theoretical frameworks
21. **Base and superstructure** — Marxist model of economic foundation and cultural forms. Depends on: Marxist criticism
22. **Ideology** — system of beliefs that naturalizes power relations. Depends on: Marxist criticism
23. **New Historicism** — approach examining literature within historical power relations. Depends on: Marxist criticism
24. **Cultural poetics** — how literary and non-literary texts circulate social energy. Depends on: New Historicism
25. **Postcolonial criticism** — analysis of imperial power, resistance, and representation. Depends on: New Historicism, ideology
26. **Orientalism** — Western construction of the East as Other. Depends on: postcolonial criticism
27. **Subaltern voice** — question of representation for marginalized groups. Depends on: postcolonial criticism
28. **Critical race theory** — analysis of how texts construct and challenge racial categories. Depends on: postcolonial criticism
29. **Psychoanalytic criticism** — approach using Freudian/Lacanian concepts to read texts. Depends on: theoretical frameworks
30. **Unconscious** — hidden desires and repressions revealed in texts. Depends on: psychoanalytic criticism
31. **Trauma theory** — approach to representing and witnessing traumatic experience. Depends on: psychoanalytic criticism
32. **Feminist criticism** — analysis of gender, patriarchy, and women's writing. Depends on: theoretical frameworks, ideology
33. **Gynocriticism** — study of women's writing as distinct tradition. Depends on: feminist criticism
34. **Gender performativity** — Butler's theory that gender is enacted through repeated acts. Depends on: feminist criticism
35. **Queer theory** — analysis challenging heteronormativity and fixed identity. Depends on: feminist criticism, gender performativity
36. **Intersectionality** — analysis of multiple overlapping identity categories. Depends on: feminist criticism, critical race theory
37. **Ecocriticism** — approach examining literature's relationship to environment. Depends on: theoretical frameworks
38. **Theoretical pluralism** — using multiple critical approaches together. Depends on: all previous frameworks

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **All theoretical approaches** depend on foundational concepts of interpretation, close reading, and theoretical frameworks
- **Form and organic unity** require understanding of close reading to identify structural patterns
- **Text-centered analysis** builds on both close reading and formalist principles about form

### Formalism to Structuralism
- **Structuralism** challenges formalism's emphasis on individual texts by seeing texts as instances of larger systems
- **Binary oppositions** apply structuralist principles to textual analysis
- **Semiotics and narratology** extend structuralist thinking to signs and narrative structures

### Reader-Oriented Approaches
- **Reader-response criticism** emerges partly as reaction to formalist neglect of readers
- **Interpretive communities** explain why reader-response doesn't mean "anything goes"
- **Reception theory** historicizes reader-response by showing how reading contexts change

### Structuralism to Poststructuralism
- **Deconstruction** builds on and critiques structuralist binary oppositions
- **Aporia and death of the author** demonstrate deconstructive principles in practice
- **Intertextuality** replaces structuralist closed systems with endless textual networks

### Political Approaches
- **New Historicism** depends on Marxist concepts of ideology but adds cultural analysis
- **Postcolonial criticism** applies New Historicist methods to imperial contexts
- **Critical race theory** extends postcolonial insights to racial construction in all contexts
- All political approaches assume that texts participate in power relations

### Psychological Approaches
- **Psychoanalytic criticism** can work independently but often combines with other approaches
- **Trauma theory** builds on psychoanalytic concepts but focuses on historical trauma

### Identity Approaches
- **Feminist criticism** draws on Marxist ideology critique and psychoanalytic theory
- **Gender performativity** challenges essentialist feminism and opens to queer theory
- **Queer theory** depends on feminist groundwork but destabilizes gender categories
- **Intersectionality** requires understanding multiple critical traditions (feminist, race, class)

### Contemporary Synthesis
- **Ecocriticism** can draw on any theoretical tradition while focusing on environment
- **Theoretical pluralism** assumes familiarity with multiple frameworks and how they interact

## Critical Bottlenecks

### Bottleneck 1: Understanding structuralism
Both deconstruction and semiotics depend on grasping structuralist principles. Students who don't understand how binary oppositions work will struggle with deconstruction's critique of them.

### Bottleneck 2: Grasping ideology
Marxist, New Historicist, postcolonial, feminist, and race criticism all use "ideology" differently. Students need to understand this concept's flexibility and specificity in each context.

### Bottleneck 3: Moving beyond "anything goes"
Students often misunderstand reader-response and deconstruction as license for arbitrary interpretation. Understanding interpretive communities and textual constraints is crucial.

### Bottleneck 4: Distinguishing similar approaches
New Criticism vs. structuralism, reader-response vs. reception theory, Marxism vs. New Historicism — these pairs are often confused. Clear comparison is essential.

### Bottleneck 5: Synthesis without confusion
Using multiple approaches together (intersectionality, theoretical pluralism) requires first understanding each approach's distinct assumptions and methods.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic literary analysis** — needed for all concepts, especially close reading and interpretation
- **Understanding literary genres** — needed for narratology, feminist gynocriticism, genre-specific criticism
- **Historical awareness** — needed for reception theory, New Historicism, postcolonial criticism
- **Philosophical thinking** — needed for deconstruction, psychoanalytic theory, theoretical frameworks generally

## Common Misconceptions

See teaching-notes.md for detailed list, but key conceptual confusions include:
- Confusing formalism with all close reading
- Thinking deconstruction means "texts mean nothing"
- Assuming reader-response means "any interpretation is valid"
- Treating "feminist criticism" as a single unified approach
- Believing theory replaces interpretation rather than enabling it
