# Color Theory — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Wavelength and visible spectrum** — Light is electromagnetic radiation; color is our perception of different wavelengths
2. **Trichromatic theory** — We have three types of cone cells (S, M, L) that respond to different wavelength ranges. Depends on: wavelength
3. **Opponent process theory** — Color information is processed in three opponent channels (red-green, blue-yellow, black-white). Depends on: trichromatic theory
4. **Color constancy** — The brain adjusts color perception based on lighting context. Depends on: opponent process theory
5. **Color vision deficiency** — Missing or malfunctioning cone types lead to altered color perception. Depends on: trichromatic theory
6. **Additive color (RGB)** — Mixing colored light; combining wavelengths adds to create lighter colors. Depends on: trichromatic theory
7. **Subtractive color (CMYK)** — Mixing pigments; each pigment absorbs wavelengths, creating darker colors. Depends on: wavelength, additive color
8. **Munsell color system** — Organizes colors by hue (color family), value (lightness), and chroma (saturation). Depends on: color perception fundamentals
9. **HSL color model** — Hue-Saturation-Lightness model matches how designers think about color. Depends on: Munsell system
10. **CIE color spaces** — Mathematically defined color spaces designed for perceptual uniformity. Depends on: RGB, human perception
11. **Color wheel** — Circular arrangement of hues showing relationships. Depends on: hue concept from Munsell
12. **Complementary colors** — Colors opposite on the wheel that create maximum contrast. Depends on: color wheel, opponent process theory
13. **Simultaneous contrast** — Adjacent colors influence how we perceive each other. Depends on: opponent process theory, color constancy
14. **Color interaction (Albers)** — Context dramatically changes color perception; same color looks different in different surroundings. Depends on: simultaneous contrast
15. **Color harmony schemes** — Systematic approaches to combining colors (analogous, triadic, split-complementary). Depends on: color wheel
16. **Value contrast** — Light-dark relationships are often more important than hue differences. Depends on: Munsell value
17. **Color temperature** — Warm (red/orange/yellow) vs. cool (blue/green/purple) colors and their psychological effects. Depends on: color wheel
18. **Color psychology** — Biological and learned emotional associations with colors. Depends on: color temperature, cultural context
19. **Cultural color symbolism** — Color meanings vary across cultures and contexts. Depends on: color psychology
20. **Color gamut** — The range of colors a device can display or reproduce. Depends on: RGB, CMYK, color spaces
21. **Color profiles** — Standards for ensuring consistent color across devices. Depends on: color gamut, CIE spaces
22. **Accessibility and contrast ratios** — WCAG standards for ensuring readable color combinations. Depends on: value contrast, color vision deficiency

## Dependencies

### Perception Foundation
- **Opponent process theory** requires understanding **trichromatic theory** because it explains the next processing stage after photoreceptors
- **Color constancy** builds on **opponent process theory** because it's an adaptive mechanism in the visual pathway
- **Simultaneous contrast** depends on **opponent process theory** because it's driven by lateral inhibition in color-opponent channels

### Color Systems
- **HSL** builds on **Munsell system** because it's a computational simplification of hue-value-chroma organization
- **CIE color spaces** require understanding **RGB** and **human perception** because they mathematically model the human visual response
- **Subtractive color** makes sense only after understanding **additive color** because it's the complementary process

### Relationships and Harmony
- **Complementary colors** depend on **color wheel** and **opponent process theory** because opposing hues trigger maximum opponent response
- **Color interaction** requires understanding **simultaneous contrast** because Albers' work demonstrates how context creates simultaneous contrast effects
- **Value contrast** builds on **Munsell value** because it quantifies the lightness dimension

### Application
- **Color profiles** require **color gamut** and **CIE spaces** because they map device-specific RGB/CMYK to perceptually uniform spaces
- **Accessibility** depends on **value contrast** and **color vision deficiency** because standards account for both lightness differences and CVD

## Bottleneck Concepts

These concepts unlock major sections of the curriculum:

1. **Trichromatic theory** — Gates understanding of all color systems and models
2. **Additive vs. subtractive color** — Essential for digital/print applications
3. **Color wheel** — Unlocks all harmony and relationship discussions
4. **Simultaneous contrast** — Key to understanding context-dependent color perception
5. **Value** — Often overlooked but crucial for effective color use

## Mind-Blowing Moments

- **Same color, different appearance** — Albers squares demonstrate that physical color ≠ perceived color
- **The dress** — Viral demonstration of color constancy and individual differences in lighting interpretation
- **Complementary afterimages** — Stare at red, see cyan ghost; direct evidence of opponent processing
- **Benham's disc** — Black and white pattern creates color perception when spinning; perception isn't just physics
- **Impossible colors** — Yellowish blue and reddish green can be perceived under special conditions, revealing limits of opponent theory

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Color is a property of objects"** — Color is a perceptual experience; objects reflect wavelengths, brains create color
2. **"RGB are the primary colors"** — RGB are additive primaries for light; RYB is outdated; CMY are subtractive primaries
3. **"Complementary colors are red/green, blue/orange, yellow/purple"** — True on RYB wheel but not in perceptually accurate systems
4. **"Saturation and brightness are the same"** — Saturation is color purity; brightness/value is lightness; HSB confuses these
5. **"Color meanings are universal"** — Most color associations are culturally learned, not biological
6. **"Colorblind people see in grayscale"** — Most color vision deficiency is reduced discrimination, not complete absence
7. **"Equal RGB values create perceptually equal brightness"** — Green appears brighter than red or blue at equal RGB values
8. **"CMYK is just the opposite of RGB"** — It's subtractive, yes, but C≠-R; actual relationship is more complex

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic physics of light** — needed for wavelength and spectrum concepts
- **Basic physiology** — needed for understanding eye anatomy and photoreceptors
- **Visual observation skills** — needed for color mixing and comparison exercises
- **Design awareness** — needed for application modules (not required for perception/systems)

## Learning Path Notes

The curriculum follows a **perception → systems → relationships → application** progression:

1. Start with **how we see** (biological and perceptual foundation)
2. Build **systematic frameworks** for organizing and describing color (models and systems)
3. Explore **interactions and relationships** (harmony, contrast, context)
4. Apply knowledge to **real-world design and art** (practical skills)

This order ensures students understand *why* color behaves the way it does before learning *how* to use it effectively.
