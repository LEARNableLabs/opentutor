# Development Economics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Development economics sits at the intersection of theory, empirics, and policy. At the intermediate level, emphasize **evidence over ideology** — ground every claim in data, acknowledge when evidence is mixed, and teach students to distinguish causal claims from correlations. Use real-world examples liberally (specific RCTs, country cases, policy programs) to make abstract concepts concrete. Balance micro-level mechanisms (household decisions, market failures) with macro patterns (growth, inequality trends). This is an empirically-driven field, so students should learn to read regression tables, understand research designs, and critique studies.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Aid causes dependency"** — Students often bring strong priors about foreign aid without understanding the evidence. The reality is nuanced: some programs work (deworming, CCTs with good targeting), others don't (untargeted subsidies, poorly-monitored infrastructure). Teach them to ask: what's the mechanism? What does the evidence show? What are the effect sizes?

2. **"Microfinance lifts people out of poverty"** — The hype around microfinance (Yunus, Grameen Bank) led to inflated expectations. Recent RCTs show modest impacts: access to credit helps smooth consumption and invest in businesses, but rarely transforms livelihoods. The key lesson: financial access matters, but it's not a silver bullet.

3. **"People are poor because they make bad choices"** — This victim-blaming narrative ignores structural barriers (credit constraints, missing markets, information asymmetries). Use behavioral economics carefully: present bias and risk aversion are real, but they're shaped by poverty itself (scarcity mindset, cortisol from chronic stress). Poverty is a constraint, not just a preference.

4. **"Growth trickles down automatically"** — While growth correlates with poverty reduction, the relationship depends on inequality, sectors of growth (manufacturing vs. natural resources), and policy (pro-poor targeting, public goods). Teach the growth-poverty elasticity concept: how much poverty reduction per 1% GDP growth varies widely.

5. **"RCTs are the gold standard, always"** — RCTs answer causal questions well, but they have limitations: ethical constraints (can't randomize land reform), external validity (results from Kenya may not apply to India), general equilibrium effects (scaling up changes market prices). Teach students to value RCTs while recognizing their scope.

6. **"Conditional cash transfers create dependency"** — Evidence from Progresa/Oportunidades in Mexico, Bolsa Familia in Brazil shows CCTs increase school enrollment, health checkups, and nutrition without reducing work effort. The conditions matter (school attendance, health visits), and targeting matters (avoid inclusion errors).

7. **"Farmers are irrational for not adopting profitable technology"** — This ignores risk aversion, learning costs, credit constraints, and missing complementary inputs. A fertilizer requires knowledge, cash to buy it, and confidence it works on your soil. Social learning and demonstration plots help, but adoption is a process, not a one-shot decision.

8. **"Corruption is a cultural problem"** — Corruption is about incentives, monitoring, and accountability. Evidence shows transparency (publishing budgets, audit reports) and monitoring (community oversight, newspaper campaigns) reduce corruption. It's a governance problem, not a fixed cultural trait.

9. **"Education quality is about teacher training"** — Teacher training programs rarely move learning outcomes. What works: incentives (pay for performance, monitoring), inputs (textbooks, glasses for students), pedagogy (teaching at the right level). Quality is about systems, not just skills.

10. **"Inequality is bad for growth"** — The relationship is complex. High inequality can reduce growth (credit constraints prevent talent from investing) or spur it (savings by the rich fund investment). Focus on inequality of opportunity vs. inequality of outcomes, and the distributional consequences of growth.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate Level (current)
- **Formalism**: Use regression equations, simple models (Solow, poverty trap diagrams), but don't require proofs. Focus on interpreting coefficients, understanding identification strategies (RCTs, DID, RDD, IV).
- **Depth**: Cover mechanisms (why does X cause Y?) and policy implications. Expect students to critique studies, not just summarize them.
- **Empirics**: Teach how to read a paper: introduction (research question, contribution), data (sources, summary stats), methods (identification strategy), results (tables, robustness), discussion (external validity, policy relevance).
- **Examples**: Use landmark studies (Miguel-Kremer deworming, Progresa CCT, Kremer fertilizer adoption, Dupas bednets) as case studies.

### Advanced Adjustments (if student progresses)
- Add theoretical depth: dynamic poverty traps, stochastic models, GE effects
- Dive into econometrics: selection bias, attrition, spillovers, multiple hypothesis testing
- Read original papers, not summaries
- Engage with debates: RCT critics (Deaton, Rodrik), structural vs. reduced-form approaches

### Beginner Adjustments (if student struggles)
- Drop regression interpretation, focus on conceptual mechanisms
- Use more visualizations (Gapminder, Our World in Data charts)
- Emphasize stories and cases over formal models
- Simplify to "what works" summaries instead of study details

## Rabbit Holes

- **Esther Duflo's Nobel Prize lecture** — accessible overview of the RCT revolution in development. Drop in when discussing experimental methods (lesson 26).

- **Abhijit Banerjee's "Poor Economics" book** — narrative-driven, evidence-based. Recommend chapters aligned with modules (credit, health, education).

- **The "worm wars" debate** — controversy over the Miguel-Kremer deworming study (reanalysis, external validity, publication bias). Introduce during lesson 8 or 27 to show how science works.

- **M-Pesa revolution in Kenya** — how mobile money transformed financial inclusion. Tavneet Suri and William Jack's research. Drop in during lesson 16 as a success story.

- **The Progresa/Oportunidades CCT program** — Mexico's flagship anti-poverty program, extensively evaluated. Use during lesson 24 to show large-scale RCT design.

- **The Green Revolution** — HYV seeds, fertilizer, irrigation. Success in Asia, mixed results in Africa. Connects to technology adoption (lessons 19-21) and shows historical context.

- **China's poverty reduction** — 800 million lifted out of poverty since 1980. What role did growth, structural transformation, and policy play? Use to complicate the "what works" narrative.

- **Behavioral development economics** — Dean Karlan's work on commitment savings, Sendhil Mullainathan's scarcity framework. Drop in during lessons 14, 17 to show behavioral insights.

- **Political economy of development** — Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson's "Why Nations Fail". Institutions, extractive vs. inclusive systems. Introduce during governance module (lessons 23-25) as a broader framework.

- **Effective altruism and cost-effectiveness** — GiveWell's charity rankings, DALY calculations. Use during lesson 12 or 8 to show how evidence informs philanthropy.

## Difficulty Progression

- **Lessons 1-6** (Poverty & Inequality): Start accessible (difficulty 2-3). Build intuition for measurement and concepts.
- **Lessons 7-12** (Human Capital): Increase to 3-4. Introduce externalities, cost-effectiveness, subsidy design. Review at lesson 12.
- **Lessons 13-18** (Markets & Credit): Peak difficulty (3-4). Behavioral economics, adverse selection, moral hazard. Review at lesson 18.
- **Lessons 19-24** (Agriculture & Technology): Moderate difficulty (3-4). Technology adoption, social learning, property rights, market access. Review at lesson 24.
- **Lessons 25-28** (Governance & Policy): Moderate difficulty (3-4). Corruption, CCTs, transparency, participation.
- **Lessons 29-31** (Experimental Methods & Synthesis): High difficulty (3-4). Causal inference, external validity. Final review at lesson 31.

Use review lessons (6, 12, 18, 24, 31) to consolidate before advancing to new modules. Monitor for struggle at lessons 9 (education quality), 15 (informal credit), 17 (insurance), 20 (social learning), 27 (transparency), 30 (RCT limits) — these are conceptually dense. Be ready to split or add examples.
