# Epistemology — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Epistemology** — the philosophical study of knowledge and justified belief
2. **Knowledge** — standardly analyzed as justified true belief (JTB)
3. **Belief** — a mental state of accepting a proposition as true
4. **Truth** — the property of propositions that accurately represent reality
5. **Justification** — having good reasons or warrant for believing something
6. **Gettier problems** — counterexamples showing JTB is insufficient for knowledge. Depends on: knowledge, JTB
7. **Epistemic luck** — when true belief results from chance rather than epistemic virtue. Depends on: Gettier problems
8. **Post-Gettier conditions** — additional requirements proposed to fix the JTB analysis. Depends on: Gettier problems, epistemic luck
9. **Perception** — sensory experience as a source of knowledge
10. **A priori knowledge** — knowledge independent of experience, knowable through reason alone
11. **A posteriori knowledge** — knowledge dependent on sensory experience. Contrasts with: a priori knowledge
12. **Empiricism** — the view that experience is the primary source of knowledge. Depends on: perception, a posteriori knowledge
13. **Rationalism** — the view that reason is the primary source of knowledge. Depends on: a priori knowledge
14. **Testimony** — knowledge acquired through the reports of others
15. **Memory** — the cognitive capacity to retain and retrieve past knowledge
16. **Foundationalism** — the view that justified beliefs rest on basic beliefs that don't require further justification. Depends on: justification, regress argument
17. **Basic beliefs** — beliefs that are justified but not by other beliefs. Depends on: foundationalism
18. **Coherentism** — the view that justification emerges from mutual support among beliefs. Depends on: justification, regress argument
19. **Infinitism** — the view that justification involves an infinite chain of reasons. Depends on: justification, regress argument
20. **Internalism** — the view that justification factors must be accessible to the believer. Depends on: justification
21. **Externalism** — the view that justification factors need not be accessible to the believer. Depends on: justification
22. **Skepticism** — doubt about the possibility of knowledge
23. **Pyrrhonism** — ancient skeptical practice of suspending judgment to achieve tranquility
24. **Cartesian skepticism** — radical doubt about knowledge of the external world. Depends on: skepticism, evil demon argument
25. **External world skepticism** — doubt that we can know anything about reality beyond our minds. Depends on: skepticism, perception
26. **Brain in a vat** — modern version of Cartesian skepticism. Depends on: external world skepticism
27. **Anti-skeptical strategies** — various philosophical responses to skeptical challenges. Depends on: skepticism
28. **Contextualism** — the view that knowledge attributions vary with conversational context. Depends on: skepticism, anti-skeptical strategies
29. **Correspondence theory of truth** — truth as matching reality. Depends on: truth
30. **Coherence theory of truth** — truth as fitting with a system of beliefs. Depends on: truth, coherentism
31. **Reliabilism** — the view that justification requires reliable belief-forming processes. Depends on: justification, externalism
32. **Process reliabilism** — reliabilism focused on cognitive processes. Depends on: reliabilism
33. **Virtue epistemology** — knowledge as the product of intellectual virtues. Depends on: justification, reliabilism
34. **Social epistemology** — study of knowledge in social contexts. Depends on: testimony, epistemic communities
35. **Epistemic injustice** — wronging someone in their capacity as a knower. Depends on: testimony, social epistemology
36. **Testimonial injustice** — credibility deficit due to prejudice. Depends on: epistemic injustice, testimony
37. **Hermeneutical injustice** — lack of interpretive resources due to marginalization. Depends on: epistemic injustice
38. **Ethics of belief** — moral constraints on what we may believe. Depends on: justification, epistemic responsibility

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies

- **JTB analysis** provides the starting point for understanding knowledge — all subsequent epistemology either builds on or reacts to it
- **Gettier problems** depend on understanding JTB because they show that JTB alone is insufficient
- **Post-Gettier theories** require understanding both JTB and why Gettier cases undermine it

### Sources Cluster

- **Empiricism vs. rationalism** debate requires understanding both a priori and a posteriori knowledge
- **Testimony** builds on but challenges simple empiricism (we can't directly experience what others tell us)
- **Memory** presupposes prior knowledge acquisition through other sources

### Justification Structure Cluster

- **Foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism** all respond to the **regress argument** — the challenge of how justification doesn't fall into infinite regress or circular reasoning
- **Internalism vs. externalism** cuts across these structural theories and affects how each can be developed
- **Reliabilism** is a form of externalism that emphasizes process rather than reasons

### Skepticism Cluster

- **Ancient skepticism** (especially Pyrrhonism) provides historical context but is methodologically distinct from modern skepticism
- **Cartesian skepticism** depends on understanding foundationalism (Descartes sought certainty as a foundation)
- **External world skepticism** builds on Cartesian skepticism but is more general
- **Anti-skeptical strategies** require understanding the specific skeptical arguments they address

### Contemporary Extensions Cluster

- **Social epistemology** extends traditional epistemology beyond individual knowers
- **Epistemic injustice** requires both social epistemology and an understanding of testimony
- **Ethics of belief** connects epistemology to ethics and requires understanding justification

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic logic** — needed for: understanding argument structure, validity, soundness, counterexamples
- **Philosophical argumentation** — needed for: analyzing thought experiments, constructing and evaluating arguments
- **Comfort with abstract thinking** — needed for: all concepts, especially a priori knowledge, theories of truth

## Conceptual Bottlenecks

1. **The regress problem** — understanding why justification seems to lead to infinite regress, circularity, or arbitrary stopping points is crucial for Module 3. Students who don't grasp this find foundationalism/coherentism/infinitism unmotivated.

2. **Internalism vs. externalism** — this distinction is subtle and cuts across many debates. Students often confuse it with other distinctions (foundationalism vs. coherentism, or rationalism vs. empiricism).

3. **The relationship between skeptical scenarios and ordinary knowledge claims** — students sometimes think defeating skepticism requires proving skeptical scenarios are impossible, rather than understanding anti-skeptical strategies that allow knowledge despite uneliminated possibilities.

4. **Process reliabilism's generality problem** — understanding why it's hard to specify the relevant belief-forming process at the right level of generality requires grasping both the motivation for reliabilism and the challenge of making it precise.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Truth = justification** — students often conflate these. Truth is about correspondence to reality; justification is about having good reasons.
- **A priori = innate** — a priori knowledge is independent of experience, but not necessarily born with.
- **Skepticism = denying we know anything** — most skeptical arguments are conditional (IF we can't rule out the demon, THEN we don't know). Skeptics often suspend judgment rather than deny.
- **Foundationalism requires absolute certainty** — modern foundationalism allows for fallible basic beliefs.
- **Coherentism is viciously circular** — coherentism appeals to holistic, mutual support, not simple circular chains.
- **Reliabilism says any reliable process gives knowledge** — reliabilism is a theory of justification, not a complete theory of knowledge (still need truth).

## Learning Progression

**Early (Lessons 1-11)**: Build from intuitive notion of knowledge to technical analysis, introduce sources. Difficulty stays at 2-3.

**Middle (Lessons 12-21)**: Explore structural theories and skepticism — these are more abstract and challenging. Difficulty peaks at 3-4. Review days are crucial here.

**Late (Lessons 22-27)**: Apply frameworks to truth, reliability, and contemporary issues. Synthesize earlier material. Difficulty stabilizes at 3, with focus on application and integration.
