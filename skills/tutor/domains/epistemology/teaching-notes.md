# Epistemology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Epistemology is best taught through **cases and puzzles** rather than abstract definitions. Start with intuitive knowledge claims ("I know I have hands"), introduce puzzles that challenge these intuitions (Gettier cases, skeptical scenarios), and then show how systematic theories try to resolve the puzzles. At the intermediate level, students can handle both informal case analysis and more formal theoretical frameworks. Emphasize **skill development**: generating counterexamples, evaluating theories, and applying concepts to new cases. Use a mix of historical context (to show how problems evolved) and contemporary applications (to show why these questions still matter).

The progression moves from **analysis** (what is knowledge?) to **sources** (where does it come from?) to **structure** (how is it justified?) to **limits** (what can we know?) to **extensions** (social and applied epistemology). Each module builds conceptual sophistication while maintaining engagement through varied delivery types.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Knowledge is just belief that happens to be true"** — Students often miss the justification requirement. Emphasize that lucky guesses aren't knowledge. Use clear cases: a stopped clock showing the right time, a wild conspiracy theory that turns out to be true.

2. **"Truth and justification are the same thing"** — Students conflate having good reasons with being right. Stress that justified beliefs can be false (you have good evidence but are mistaken) and true beliefs can be unjustified (lucky guesses). Truth is about the world; justification is about reasons.

3. **"A priori knowledge means innate knowledge or knowledge before birth"** — Clarify that "independent of experience" means you don't need to go check the world, not that you're born knowing it. Use math examples: you learn arithmetic through experience, but "2 + 2 = 4" is justified a priori.

4. **"Gettier cases show we can never have knowledge"** — Students sometimes overgeneralize. Gettier cases challenge the sufficiency of JTB, not the possibility of knowledge. They show we need a better analysis, not that knowledge is impossible.

5. **"Skepticism means denying that we know anything"** — Most skeptical arguments are conditional: IF you can't rule out the evil demon, THEN you don't know about the external world. Pyrrhonian skeptics suspend judgment; they don't assert "we know nothing" (which would be self-defeating).

6. **"Foundationalism requires infallible or certain foundations"** — This was true for Descartes, but contemporary foundationalism allows fallible basic beliefs (modest foundationalism). The key is that basic beliefs don't require inferential justification, not that they're absolutely certain.

7. **"Coherentism is just circular reasoning"** — Students see mutual support as viciously circular. Emphasize holistic coherence: it's not A justifies B justifies A, but rather a whole system of beliefs supporting each other (like how pieces of a jigsaw puzzle mutually support each other's placement).

8. **"Reliabilism says any reliable process counts as knowledge"** — Reliabilism is a theory of **justification**, not a complete account of knowledge. You still need truth (and perhaps other conditions). A reliable process gives you justification, but if the belief is false, it's not knowledge.

9. **"Memory creates new knowledge"** — Memory preserves knowledge acquired by other means; it doesn't generate new knowledge. The knowledge comes from the original source (perception, reasoning, etc.).

10. **"Testimony is second-hand knowledge and therefore inferior"** — Much of our most important knowledge comes from testimony (science, history, most facts about the world). Epistemology of testimony shows when and why testimonial knowledge is legitimate.

11. **"Internalism means knowledge is 'in your head' while externalism means it's 'outside'"** — The debate is about **access to justifiers**, not the location of knowledge. Internalists say justification factors must be cognitively accessible; externalists say they need not be.

12. **"Virtue epistemology is about being a good person"** — Intellectual virtues (open-mindedness, attentiveness, intellectual courage) differ from moral virtues. You can be morally good but intellectually lazy, or intellectually rigorous but morally flawed.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students (Current Level)

- **Formalism**: Introduce technical terminology and formal distinctions, but always with clear examples first. Use symbolic logic sparingly, only where helpful.
- **Primary sources**: Include short excerpts from classic texts (Plato's *Theaetetus*, Gettier's paper, Descartes' *Meditations*), but with scaffolding and modern commentary.
- **Depth**: Go beyond "what do people believe?" to "why do they believe it?" and "what are the strongest objections?". Teach students to steelman positions before criticizing them.
- **Skills**: Emphasize constructing counterexamples, finding analogies, evaluating consistency, and applying theories to new cases.
- **Scope**: Cover the major positions and debates, but don't try to cover every variant. Focus on understanding core structures rather than exhaustive taxonomy.

### If Adjusting Down (Beginner)

- More examples, fewer formal definitions
- Focus on JTB, Gettier, basic skepticism — skip infinitism, formal epistemology, detailed history
- Emphasize intuitive understanding over technical precision
- Use more real-world cases, fewer thought experiments
- Simplify the structure: knowledge as JTB + something, sources of knowledge, skeptical challenges

### If Adjusting Up (Advanced)

- Engage with primary sources more directly (full papers, not excerpts)
- Introduce formal epistemology (probability, Bayesianism)
- Cover contemporary research: disagreement, pragmatic encroachment, knowledge-first epistemology
- Emphasize original argumentation and sustained critical engagement
- Connect to adjacent fields: philosophy of science, philosophy of mind, formal semantics

## Rabbit Holes

- **The Münchhausen trilemma** — the three-way choice between infinite regress, circular reasoning, and arbitrary stopping points. Drop this in Lesson 12 when introducing foundationalism to motivate the whole module.

- **Zhuangzi's butterfly dream** — "Am I a man dreaming I'm a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming I'm a man?" A non-Western skeptical puzzle that's more playful than Cartesian skepticism. Use in Lesson 18 to show skepticism isn't uniquely Western.

- **The Lottery Paradox** — you're justified in believing "my ticket won't win" (it almost certainly won't), and similarly for every ticket, but you know one ticket will win. Can you know a ticket won't win? Connects to statistical evidence and stakes. Drop in Lesson 15 (internalism/externalism) or Lesson 23 (reliabilism).

- **The Swamping Problem for reliabilism** — if reliability is what matters, why care whether true belief was reliably produced? A reliably produced true belief isn't better than a luckily true belief if all we care about is having the truth. Challenges the value of knowledge. Use in Lesson 23.

- **The KK principle** — "If you know P, do you know that you know P?" Connects to internalism/externalism and higher-order evidence. Drop in Lesson 15.

- **Epistemic closure** — If you know P, and you know P entails Q, do you know Q? Seems obvious, but leads to problems with skepticism. Use in Lesson 19 (external world skepticism).

- **Chisholm's problem of the criterion** — to know what's true, you need a criterion of truth; but to have a criterion, you first need to know some truths. Which comes first? Use in Lesson 12 (foundationalism).

- **Zagzebski's value problem** — why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief? If knowledge is just true belief + something, and the "something" (justification, reliability) can be present in false beliefs, how does it add value when the belief is true? Use in Lesson 24 (virtue epistemology).

- **The Gettier problem for perception** — can you have perceptual knowledge if you're hallucinating, but there happens to be an object where you hallucinate it? Use in Lesson 6 to show Gettier problems aren't limited to testimonial cases.

- **Phenomenal conservatism** — if it seems to you that P, you thereby have some justification for P. A controversial foundationalist view. Drop in Lesson 12.

## Difficulty Progression

**Lessons 1-5** (Difficulty 1-3): Start accessible (what is knowledge?) and build to Gettier (moderately challenging puzzle). Review at Lesson 5 to consolidate.

**Lessons 6-11** (Difficulty 2-3): Sources of knowledge are intuitive and relatable. Difficulty stays moderate. Review at Lesson 11.

**Lessons 12-16** (Difficulty 3-4): **Peak difficulty**. Abstract structural theories and the internalism/externalism distinction. Lesson 15 is the hardest single lesson (difficulty 4). Review at Lesson 16 to ensure students have it before moving on.

**Lessons 17-21** (Difficulty 2-4): Skepticism is moderately difficult. Ancient skepticism is more accessible (difficulty 2), modern skepticism peaks at difficulty 4 (Lesson 19). Review at Lesson 21.

**Lessons 22-24** (Difficulty 3-4): Truth and reliabilism are challenging but students now have conceptual tools from earlier modules. Difficulty 3-4 but feels easier because of built competence.

**Lessons 25-27** (Difficulty 3): Contemporary extensions apply earlier frameworks to social and ethical contexts. Difficulty 3 throughout — complex material but students are now skilled at epistemological thinking.

## Delivery Variations

- **Mini-lessons** — standard exposition with examples, used for core theoretical content
- **Questions** — pose a puzzle and let the student try to solve it before revealing standard answers. Use for Gettier problems, skeptical scenarios, epistemic dilemmas.
- **Resource-drops** — curate excellent external content (videos, articles) and frame it with guiding questions. Use for infinitism, virtue epistemology (less central topics where external resources are strong).
- **Teach-backs** — student explains a concept/debate back to you. Use after major modules to consolidate: empiricism vs rationalism (L10), responses to skepticism (L20), ethics of belief (L27).
- **Real-world** — apply epistemological concepts to everyday cases (trusting news, eyewitness testimony, memory in legal contexts). Use for perception (L6), testimony (L8), social epistemology (L25).
- **Review** — spaced repetition and synthesis. Use every 5-7 lessons and after major conceptual shifts.

## Assessment Strategies

At intermediate level, assess by:

1. **Case analysis** — give a new scenario (not from lessons) and ask: is this knowledge? Why or why not? Which theory handles it best?
2. **Counterexample generation** — can they construct Gettier-style cases for new theories?
3. **Theory comparison** — contrast two theories on a specific case or dimension
4. **Argument reconstruction** — take a passage and identify premises, conclusion, potential objections
5. **Application** — use epistemological frameworks to analyze real-world issues (fake news, expert disagreement, scientific evidence)

Students should be able to explain concepts clearly, evaluate arguments, and apply theories to novel cases — not just recite definitions.
