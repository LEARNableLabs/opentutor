# Epistemology — Resources

## Primary Sources (for lesson content)

### Essential Textbooks

- **Robert Audi, *Epistemology: A Contemporary Introduction to the Theory of Knowledge*** (3rd ed., Routledge) — Clear, comprehensive, suitable for intermediate students. Covers all major topics with good balance of history and contemporary debates.
  
- **Laurence BonJour, *Epistemology: Classic Problems and Contemporary Responses*** (2nd ed., Rowman & Littlefield) — Problem-driven approach, great for seeing how theories respond to puzzles.

- **Duncan Pritchard, *What is This Thing Called Knowledge?*** (4th ed., Routledge) — Accessible introduction with focus on Gettier problems, skepticism, and contemporary developments.

- **Matthias Steup and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*** (2nd ed., Wiley-Blackwell) — Paired essays by leading philosophers debating key issues. Great for seeing live disagreements.

### Reference Works

- **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (https://plato.stanford.edu/) — Gold standard. Every major topic has a comprehensive, peer-reviewed entry. Update regularly. Essential entries:
  - Epistemology: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology/
  - The Analysis of Knowledge: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-analysis/
  - Justification: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justep-foundational/
  - Skepticism: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/skepticism/

- **Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (https://iep.utm.edu/) — More accessible than SEP, good for initial orientation.

- **1000-Word Philosophy** (https://1000wordphilosophy.com/) — Very short, clear introductions to philosophical topics. Great for quick overviews.

### Classic Primary Texts

- **Plato, *Theaetetus*** — First systematic attempt to define knowledge. Explores knowledge as perception, as true belief, as justified true belief.

- **René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*** — Foundationalism, the cogito, skeptical scenarios (evil demon, dream argument). Meditation I-II essential for understanding Cartesian skepticism.

- **David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*** — Empiricism, skepticism about causation and induction, problem of the external world.

- **Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*** — Synthetic a priori knowledge, transcendental arguments, limits of knowledge. Difficult but influential.

- **Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"** (*Analysis*, 1963) — The 3-page paper that revolutionized epistemology. Free online: https://www.jstor.org/stable/3326922

- **Alvin Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?"** (*Justification and Knowledge*, 1979) — Introduces reliabilism. https://philpapers.org/rec/GOLWIJ

### Contemporary Philosophy

- **Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*** (Oxford, 2000) — Knowledge-first epistemology, influential and challenging.

- **Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice* (Oxford, 2007)** — Groundbreaking work on testimonial and hermeneutical injustice.

- **Ernest Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology* (Oxford, 2007)** — Leading account of virtue epistemology.

- **Keith DeRose, *The Case for Contextualism* (Oxford, 2009)** — Defense of contextualism about knowledge attributions.

## Unexpected Connections

### Cross-Disciplinary Links

- **Cognitive science and psychology** — Research on heuristics and biases (Kahneman & Tversky) challenges assumptions about human rationality. Connects to naturalized epistemology.

- **Neuroscience of perception** — How visual processing works informs debates about perceptual knowledge. See V.S. Ramachandran's work on perception and consciousness.

- **Artificial intelligence** — What would it mean for an AI to "know" something? Machine learning and epistemology. Connect to reliabilism (is pattern recognition a reliable process?).

- **Law and evidence** — Legal standards of proof (beyond reasonable doubt, preponderance of evidence) are applied epistemology. Eyewitness testimony research connects to reliability of memory and perception.

- **Science studies and STS** — How scientific communities produce knowledge. Connects to social epistemology, testimony, and peer disagreement. See Helen Longino's work.

- **Journalism and media literacy** — How to evaluate sources, detect misinformation, understand expertise. Applied epistemology for the information age.

- **Indigenous epistemologies** — Alternative frameworks for understanding knowledge (e.g., knowledge as relationship in many Indigenous traditions). Challenges Western assumptions about individual knowers.

### Literature & Film

- **Philip K. Dick novels** — *Ubik*, *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?*, *A Scanner Darkly* — all explore skepticism about reality, perception, and memory.

- **The Matrix** (1999) — Modern brain-in-a-vat scenario. What if this is all a simulation?

- **Memento** (2000) — Can you trust your memory? What if you can't form new memories?

- **Blade Runner** (1982) — Do replicants with implanted memories have genuine knowledge? What makes knowledge yours?

- **Jorge Luis Borges stories** — "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius", "The Library of Babel" — playing with idealism, coherence, and the nature of truth.

- **Plato's *Republic*, Book VII (The Cave)** — Allegory showing levels of knowledge and the journey from ignorance to understanding.

## Tools for Self-Study

- **Argument mapping software** — Rationale, Argunet — visualize logical structure of epistemological arguments
- **Spaced repetition (Anki)** — Create flashcards for key concepts, distinctions, examples
- **Philosophy discussion forums** — r/askphilosophy (Reddit), Philosophy Stack Exchange — ask questions, see how experts explain concepts
- **Note-taking with concept maps** — Obsidian, Roam, or paper — map dependencies between concepts as you learn

## Suggested Reading Order

For self-directed deep dive beyond the curriculum:

1. Start with **1000-Word Philosophy** introductions to get oriented
2. Read **Gettier's paper** (3 pages, revolutionary)
3. Work through **Audi's textbook** chapters as you go through lessons
4. Watch **Wireless Philosophy** videos for visual reinforcement
5. Dive into **Stanford Encyclopedia** entries when you want depth on specific topics
6. Read **Descartes' Meditations I-II** when you reach skepticism module
7. Explore **contemporary papers on PhilPapers** on topics that grab you
8. Try **Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice*** when you reach contemporary extensions

## Tips for Engaging with Resources

- **Read actively** — Ask: What's the main claim? What reasons support it? What are the objections? Can I think of a counterexample?
- **Use examples** — Always ground abstract concepts in concrete cases
- **Compare theories** — Don't just learn theories in isolation; see how they differ on specific cases
- **Write** — Summarize arguments in your own words, construct your own examples
- **Discuss** — Find a study partner or online community; philosophy is a conversation
- **Be patient** — Epistemology is cumulative; early concepts are building blocks for later ones
