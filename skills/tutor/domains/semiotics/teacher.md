## Domain Hooks
- **Peirce's pragmatism** — connection between semiotics and his maxim ("meaning = practical consequences"). Drop in when discussing interpretants: meaning is what the sign makes you do/think/expect.

- **Derrida and différance** — post-structuralist critique that defers meaning infinitely. Mention when covering unlimited semiosis: Derrida radicalizes Saussure's insight that meaning is differential (signs defined by what they're not).

- **Color across cultures** — red = luck (China), danger (West), purity (India). Great for showing arbitrariness and cultural codes. Use when discussing symbols or connotation.

- **Animal communication** — bees' waggle dance, bird alarm calls. Biosemiotics as an advanced topic. Are these signs? What kind? Opens questions about intentionality, convention, evolution.

- **Emoji evolution** — how 🍆 became sexual innuendo, 💀 means "I'm dying laughing." Perfect for polysemy, context dependency, intertextuality. Students already know this domain.

## Common Failure Modes
1. **"Signs have natural meanings"** — Students resist arbitrariness, believing that words/symbols inherently match their referents. Counter with cross-linguistic examples (different languages, same concept) and historical changes in meaning. Emphasize that even "natural" signs like icons require cultural knowledge to interpret.

2. **"The signifier is the thing itself"** — Confusion between signifier (the form: sound, image) and referent (the actual object). Use triadic model to clarify: representamen ≠ object. Example: the word "dog" (signifier) is not the furry animal (referent).

3. **"Denotation is objective, connotation is subjective"** — Students think denotation = fact, connotation = personal opinion. Actually, both are culturally mediated. A "rose" denotes a flower, but that denotation itself relies on shared botanical categories. Connotation (romance, beauty) is socially shared, not idiosyncratic.
