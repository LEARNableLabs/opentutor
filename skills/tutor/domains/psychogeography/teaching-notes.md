# Psychogeography — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Psychogeography is **theory-heavy but practice-essential**. At the intermediate level, students need enough theoretical grounding to understand why psychogeography is a radical critique (not just "cool walks"), but they must also do actual derives and create maps. The sweet spot: **theory-informed practice**. Each theoretical concept should lead to a practical exercise; each walk should test or reveal theoretical insights. Use the Situationists' own method — learning through playful experimentation, not passive study. Emphasize that psychogeography is **embodied knowledge** — you can't understand it by reading alone.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Psychogeography is just wandering around cities"**
   - **Why**: The term sounds whimsical; contemporary usage often flattens it to "urban walking"
   - **Correction**: The dérive is structured by attention to emotional geography and unities of ambiance. It has a political purpose (critique of capitalist urbanism) and theoretical framework. Wandering is aimless; drifting is guided by psychic terrain.

2. **"Any personal map is psychogeographic"**
   - **Why**: Students conflate all subjective mapping with psychogeography
   - **Correction**: Psychogeographic mapping has specific commitments — it reveals how space shapes affect and behavior, often with critical or political intent. Not all autobiographical cartography is psychogeographic.

3. **"You need to get lost to do a dérive"**
   - **Why**: Debord emphasized "disorientation" and dérives often happen in unfamiliar areas
   - **Correction**: The goal is **re-orientation** according to emotional terrain, not lostness itself. You can derive in familiar neighborhoods by attending to ambiance rather than habit.

4. **"Psychogeography is anti-urban or nostalgic"**
   - **Why**: Some contemporary psychogeographers critique modern cities with elegiac tone
   - **Correction**: The Situationists were radically pro-urban — they wanted to transform cities into sites of play and collective creation (unitary urbanism), not retreat from them. Nostalgia is a later distortion.

5. **"Digital tools contradict psychogeographic principles"**
   - **Why**: GPS tracking seems to negate the dérive's spontaneity
   - **Correction**: This is a productive tension. Digital tools enable new forms of mapping and pattern recognition but raise questions about surveillance and datafication. Engage the contradiction rather than resolve it.

6. **"Psychogeography is only about big, historic cities like Paris or London"**
   - **Why**: Most famous examples come from Paris (Debord) and London (Sinclair, Self)
   - **Correction**: Psychogeographic methods work in any city or urban space, including suburbs, smaller towns, and even rural areas. The specific textures differ, but the principle (space shapes affect) is universal.

7. **"The Situationists were just artists or pranksters"**
   - **Why**: Some Situationist tactics (détournement, provocations) seem playful
   - **Correction**: They were serious political revolutionaries influenced by Marxism and committed to overthrowing capitalist society. The playfulness had strategic purpose — unmasking the spectacle through subversion.

8. **"Psychogeography is a solo practice"**
   - **Why**: Much psychogeographic writing emphasizes individual wandering
   - **Correction**: Debord emphasized **collective derives** and group discussion. Contemporary participatory mapping is inherently collaborative. Solo practice is valid but not definitive.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner adaptations
- Focus on **practice over theory** — start with simple derives before introducing Debord
- Use contemporary examples (street art, gentrification) before historical Situationism
- Simplify mapping exercises (draw feelings-based map of your neighborhood)
- Minimal French terminology; translate concepts into plain language

### Intermediate (this level)
- **Balance theory and practice** — establish historical/theoretical grounding, then apply
- Introduce key Situationist texts (excerpts from Society of the Spectacle, "Theory of the Dérive")
- Multi-layered mapping projects (temporal overlays, emotional data)
- Engage contemporary applications (tactical urbanism, spatial justice)
- Expect students to read some challenging theory but emphasize experiential learning

### Advanced adaptations
- Deep theoretical engagement — read full Debord texts, Lefebvre, de Certeau
- Research-level projects (original psychogeographic research in specific cities)
- Engage academic debates (is digital psychogeography possible? post-Situationist critiques)
- Historical scholarship on Situationist International
- Theoretical extensions (psychogeography of non-urban spaces, climate change implications)

## Rabbit Holes

### The Spectacle and contemporary digital media
- Debord's 1967 critique of mass media feels prophetic in the age of smartphones and social media
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 3 (spectacle theory) or Lesson 19 (digital psychogeography)
- Students often have strong reactions — does Instagram make cities into spectacles? Is a GPS-tracked dérive self-contradictory?

### New Babylon and utopian urbanism
- Constant Nieuwenhuys's visionary architectural project for a city of pure play
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 9 (unitary urbanism) or Lesson 21 (contemporary urban design)
- Visual feast — show images of the models and drawings. Prompt: "What would your day look like in New Babylon?"

### Gentrification and psychogeographic resistance
- How psychogeography informs anti-gentrification organizing and memory preservation
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 23 (resistance to gentrification)
- Connect to students' local contexts — where is their city changing? Who benefits, who's displaced?

### Indigenous place-based knowledge and psychogeography
- Unexpected connection: Indigenous relationships to land as emotional/spiritual geography
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 8 (ambiance) or Lesson 25 (local application)
- Raises questions: Is psychogeography just rediscovering what colonialism erased? How can it avoid extractive appropriation?

### The flâneur tradition and its limits
- Baudelaire's 19th-century urban observer vs. Situationist derive
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 4 (historical context) or Lesson 11 (observation practices)
- Critical angle: The flâneur is often a privileged male figure. Who gets to wander cities safely? How does gender, race, and class shape psychogeographic access?

### Psychogeography and climate change
- How rising seas, heat islands, and climate migration transform emotional geography
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 21 (urban design) or Lesson 25 (future directions)
- Emerging frontier — very little written on this yet, space for original student thinking

### Walking as political act
- From protest marches to long-distance activist walks (e.g., climate pilgrimages)
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 11 (walking practice) or Lesson 23 (political applications)
- Rebecca Solnit's *Wanderlust* is great companion reading

## Difficulty Progression

**Early lessons (1-6)**: Build foundations — introduce core concepts with low cognitive load. Mix accessible theory with immediate practice.

**Middle arc (7-19)**: Complexity increases. Harder theoretical concepts (détournement, unitary urbanism), more sophisticated practices (night dérives, multi-layered mapping). Peak difficulty around Lessons 9, 17, and 19.

**Late lessons (20-26)**: Shift to application and synthesis. Difficulty moderates as students apply learned concepts to contemporary contexts and personal practice. Final review (26) consolidates learning.

**Review placement**: Lessons 6, 13, 20, 26 provide consolidation points. Use these to check comprehension, revisit misconceptions, and connect theory to practice.

## Practical Requirements

### Students should complete at least:
- **3 dérives** — one solo, one in small group, one focused (e.g., night dérive or edgeland exploration)
- **2 psychogeographic maps** — one hand-drawn (emotions/ambiance), one using digital tools
- **1 documentation project** — photos, audio, video, or field notes from a dérive
- **Reading** — 3-4 core texts (Debord excerpts, Coverley chapters, contemporary essays)

### Accessibility considerations
- Walks can be shortened or adapted for mobility limitations
- Virtual dérives using Google Street View are valid alternatives
- Emphasize that psychogeography is about **attention**, not physical endurance
- Group dérives can provide safety and support

## Assessment Strategies

Rather than traditional exams:
- **Reflective field notes** from derives (what did you notice? how did space affect you?)
- **Psychogeographic maps** with artist's statements (what are you representing? what method did you use?)
- **Teaching exercises** — "design a dérive for a friend who's never heard of psychogeography" (Lesson 24)
- **Critical essays** — apply psychogeographic analysis to a specific urban issue (gentrification, smart cities, pandemic urbanism)

Success = students can **conduct independent derives, create meaningful maps, and connect practice to theory**. They should be able to explain psychogeography to others and apply its methods to their own cities.
