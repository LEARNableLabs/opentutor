# Psychogeography — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Emotional geography** — cities have invisible landscapes of feeling, memory, and atmosphere distinct from physical geography
2. **Psychogeography (definition)** — study of how geographical environments affect emotions and behavior
3. **Situationist International** — 1950s-1970s avant-garde movement that developed psychogeography as radical critique
4. **Guy Debord** — key theorist who formalized psychogeographic theory and method. Depends on: Situationist International
5. **Society of the Spectacle** — Debord's critique of capitalist urbanism as passive consumption. Depends on: Guy Debord
6. **Dérive (drift)** — unplanned journey through urban space guided by emotional terrain. Depends on: psychogeography definition
7. **Rapid passage** — moving quickly through boring or repellent zones during a dérive. Depends on: dérive
8. **Détournement** — subversive repurposing of urban signs and elements. Depends on: Situationist International
9. **Ambiance / atmosphere** — affective qualities that make urban zones attractive or repellent. Depends on: emotional geography
10. **Unities of ambiance** — distinct zones of consistent emotional character within a city. Depends on: ambiance
11. **Unitary urbanism** — Situationist vision of cities designed for play and collective creation. Depends on: Situationist International, dérive
12. **Liminal spaces** — thresholds, edges, and transitional zones with unique psychogeographic properties. Depends on: dérive, ambiance
13. **Edgelands** — forgotten urban margins and non-places. Depends on: liminal spaces
14. **Temporal psychogeography** — how time of day, season, and historical period affect emotional geography. Depends on: psychogeography definition
15. **Psychogeographic mapping** — representing emotional and atmospheric data cartographically. Depends on: psychogeography definition, dérive
16. **The Naked City** — Debord's iconic map showing Paris as fragmented zones of ambiance. Depends on: psychogeographic mapping, unities of ambiance
17. **Subjective cartography** — mapping personal, emotional, and phenomenological experience. Depends on: psychogeographic mapping
18. **Urban palimpsest** — layered historical traces visible in contemporary cities. Depends on: temporal psychogeography
19. **Digital psychogeography** — using GPS, location-based media, and digital tools for derives and mapping. Depends on: psychogeographic mapping, dérive
20. **Tactical urbanism** — small-scale interventions to transform urban space. Depends on: détournement, unitary urbanism
21. **Right to the city** — political claim to participate in shaping urban space. Depends on: Situationist International, unitary urbanism
22. **Participatory mapping** — collaborative psychogeographic mapping for community organizing. Depends on: psychogeographic mapping, right to the city

## Dependencies

### Core theoretical chain
**Situationist International → Guy Debord → Society of the Spectacle → psychogeography as critique**

The historical and theoretical foundation must be established before methods make sense. Psychogeography isn't just "interesting walks" — it's a political practice born from specific mid-20th century radical movements.

### Practice depends on theory
**Dérive** requires understanding **emotional geography** and **ambiance** — otherwise it's just wandering. Students need to recognize they're looking for something specific (unities of ambiance, rapid passages) even though the route is unplanned.

### Mapping requires experience
**Psychogeographic mapping** depends on having conducted **dérives** and identified **unities of ambiance**. You can't map what you haven't felt. The Naked City makes sense only after students have experienced how cities fragment into distinct emotional zones.

### Contemporary applications build on foundations
**Tactical urbanism**, **right to the city**, and **participatory mapping** inherit Situationist politics but adapt methods for contemporary contexts. Students need the historical grounding to understand why these practices are political, not just aesthetic.

### Digital tools transform practice
**Digital psychogeography** enables new kinds of mapping and documentation but also raises questions about surveillance, dataveillance, and whether GPS tracking contradicts the dérive's spirit. This tension is productive but requires understanding traditional methods first.

## Bottlenecks

### Ambiance / unities of ambiance
Students often struggle to **recognize and articulate** what makes an urban zone have distinct emotional character. This is partly experiential (requires practice walking and noticing) and partly linguistic (lacks vocabulary for affects). If students can't identify unities of ambiance, they can't do meaningful dérives or mapping.

**Teaching strategy**: Provide concrete examples, use visual/sensory language, compare to familiar spaces, practice articulating feelings about place.

### The Naked City map
Debord's most famous artifact is also confusing on first encounter — it looks abstract and arbitrary. Students need to understand it represents **subjective experience** of Paris, not objective layout. The fragmentation and arrows show how you actually move through the city emotionally, not geometrically.

**Teaching strategy**: Show before/after of conventional Paris map vs. Naked City. Ask: "How do you actually experience your city — as continuous space or as distinct zones you jump between?"

### Theory-practice integration
Students often gravitate to either **pure theory** (reading Debord, discussing spectacle) or **pure practice** (walking, making maps) without integrating them. Psychogeography's power comes from **theory-informed practice** — walks that test hypotheses, maps that make political arguments.

**Teaching strategy**: Every theoretical concept should lead to a practical exercise; every practice should connect back to theory.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic geography** — needed for understanding maps, spatial concepts, urban form
- **Some history** — 1950s-1960s context helps (Cold War, postwar reconstruction, consumer capitalism, May 1968)
- **Comfort with abstraction** — concepts like "spectacle" and "ambiance" are not concrete
- **Walking ability** — practical derives require physical capacity to walk for 1-3 hours (can be adapted for accessibility)

## Common Misconceptions

### "Psychogeography is just walking aimlessly"
**No** — the dérive is structured by attention to emotional geography, even though the route is unplanned. There's a difference between wandering and drifting.

### "Any subjective map is psychogeographic"
**No** — psychogeography has specific theoretical commitments (critique of spectacle, attention to ambiance, political dimension). Not all personal mapping is psychogeographic.

### "Psychogeography is about getting lost"
**Partially true** — disorientation can be productive, but the goal is **re-orientation** according to emotional terrain, not lostness for its own sake.

### "Digital tools are anti-psychogeographic"
**Debatable** — while GPS tracking seems to contradict the dérive's openness, digital tools enable new forms of mapping and documentation. The tension is productive but not absolute.

### "Psychogeography is only for cities"
**Mostly true** — it emerged as urban critique, but principles can apply to any landscape. However, the core concerns (capitalism, spectacle, rapid urban change) are specifically urban.
