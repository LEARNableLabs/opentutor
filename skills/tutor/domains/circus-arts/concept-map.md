# Circus Arts: Aerial Silks Physics and Rigging — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Tension** — the pulling force transmitted through fabric when loaded
2. **Weight distribution** — how body mass creates forces at contact points
3. **Equilibrium** — balanced forces in static positions
4. **Friction** — resistance between fabric and skin/fabric that enables gripping
5. **Normal force** — perpendicular force between surfaces that contributes to friction
6. **Coefficient of friction** — material property determining grip effectiveness. Depends on: friction, normal force
7. **Wrap mechanics** — how multiple fabric wraps multiply gripping force. Depends on: friction, coefficient of friction
8. **Static load** — constant force from stationary mass
9. **Safe working load (SWL)** — maximum recommended load for equipment
10. **Safety factor** — ratio of breaking strength to working load. Depends on: SWL
11. **Dynamic loading** — time-varying forces from movement and acceleration
12. **Impact force** — peak force during sudden deceleration. Depends on: dynamic loading
13. **Deceleration** — rate of slowing down during catches or drops
14. **Load factor** — multiplier relating static weight to dynamic force. Depends on: dynamic loading, impact force, deceleration
15. **Carabiner ratings** — strength specifications for hardware (major axis, minor axis, gate open)
16. **Gate types** — screw-lock vs auto-lock mechanisms for security
17. **Strength specifications** — numerical force limits for equipment
18. **Vector resolution** — breaking forces into directional components. Depends on: tension
19. **Angle dependency** — how rigging geometry affects force magnitude
20. **Bridle geometry** — configuration of multi-point attachments. Depends on: vector resolution, angle dependency
21. **Cosine losses** — force increase from non-vertical rigging angles. Depends on: vector resolution, angle dependency
22. **Force multiplication** — how poor geometry amplifies loads. Depends on: angle dependency, bridle geometry
23. **Stress concentration** — localized high force at specific points
24. **Safe angle limits** — maximum allowable bridle angles (typically <120°). Depends on: force multiplication, stress concentration
25. **System redundancy** — backup components to prevent single-point failure
26. **Load path analysis** — tracing forces from performer to structure. Depends on: system redundancy
27. **Weak link identification** — finding the lowest-rated component. Depends on: load path analysis
28. **Material properties** — physical characteristics of fabrics (strength, stretch, durability)
29. **Elasticity** — ability to deform and return to original shape
30. **Stretch characteristics** — how much and how quickly fabric extends. Depends on: material properties, elasticity
31. **Pressure distribution** — force per unit area on body surfaces. Depends on: weight distribution
32. **Contact area** — surface area where body touches fabric
33. **Force per unit area** — pressure calculation. Depends on: pressure distribution, contact area
34. **Knot strength reduction** — percentage loss when fabric is tied (~50%)
35. **Abrasion damage** — surface wear from friction and rubbing
36. **Inspection criteria** — standards for evaluating equipment condition. Depends on: abrasion damage, knot strength reduction
37. **Failure analysis** — investigating why equipment broke
38. **UV degradation** — weakening from sunlight exposure
39. **Cyclic loading fatigue** — damage from repeated stress cycles. Depends on: dynamic loading
40. **Visual inspection** — looking for visible damage signs
41. **Tactile assessment** — feeling for texture/stiffness changes
42. **Climbing biomechanics** — muscle and joint forces during upward movement. Depends on: friction, wrap mechanics
43. **Muscular force** — strength generated by muscles
44. **Friction-assisted movement** — using fabric wraps to reduce required grip. Depends on: friction, wrap mechanics
45. **Rotational mechanics** — physics of spinning and twisting movements
46. **Angular momentum** — quantity of rotation in motion
47. **Torque on joints** — twisting forces at shoulders, hips, knees. Depends on: rotational mechanics
48. **Asymmetric loading** — uneven force distribution between body sides. Depends on: torque on joints
49. **Video analysis** — using recorded movement to study forces
50. **Force estimation** — calculating approximate loads from observable motion
51. **Peak load timing** — when maximum forces occur in a sequence. Depends on: dynamic loading, force estimation
52. **Injury risk assessment** — evaluating movement danger. Depends on: peak load timing, asymmetric loading
53. **Momentum control** — managing speed and direction changes
54. **Deceleration distance** — length over which slowing occurs (longer = lower force)
55. **Impulse-momentum theorem** — force × time = change in momentum. Depends on: momentum control, deceleration distance
56. **Active vs passive catches** — controlled vs uncontrolled deceleration. Depends on: impulse-momentum theorem
57. **Redundancy principles** — design strategies for backup systems. Depends on: system redundancy
58. **Backup systems** — secondary load-bearing components
59. **Failure modes** — ways equipment can break or fail. Depends on: failure analysis
60. **SPOF elimination** — removing single points of failure. Depends on: redundancy principles, weak link identification
61. **Inspection protocols** — systematic checking procedures
62. **Systematic checks** — ordered examination routines
63. **Documentation** — recording inspection results and equipment history
64. **Go/no-go criteria** — pass/fail standards for safety. Depends on: inspection protocols
65. **Comprehensive safety assessment** — full system evaluation. Depends on: inspection protocols, load path analysis, failure modes

## Dependencies

### Foundational Chains

- **Friction mechanics** — friction → normal force → coefficient of friction → wrap mechanics → climbing biomechanics. This chain underpins most aerial movement.
- **Load analysis** — static load → dynamic loading → impact force → load factor → peak load timing. Essential for rigging design.
- **Geometry and forces** — tension → vector resolution → angle dependency → bridle geometry → force multiplication. Critical for rigging configuration.

### Critical Dependencies

- **Load factor** requires understanding dynamic loading, impact force, and deceleration because you need to know how movement creates force beyond static weight.
- **Bridle geometry** builds on vector resolution and angle dependency because multi-point rigging requires breaking forces into components and understanding angle effects.
- **Failure analysis** depends on understanding stress concentration, UV degradation, and cyclic loading fatigue because identifying failure causes requires knowing all damage mechanisms.
- **Injury risk assessment** builds on peak load timing and asymmetric loading because predicting injury requires knowing when and where maximum forces occur.
- **SPOF elimination** requires redundancy principles and weak link identification because you need to understand both backup design and where failures might occur.

### Bottleneck Concepts

- **Vector resolution** — gates access to all rigging geometry topics. Without this, angle calculations are impossible.
- **Load factor** — central to translating between static weights and real-world dynamic forces.
- **Pressure distribution** — key to understanding both comfort and injury mechanics.
- **Impulse-momentum theorem** — unlocks understanding of how timing and distance reduce impact forces.

### Common Misconceptions

- Students often think **safety factor** and **load factor** are the same — they're orthogonal (one is design margin, the other is dynamic amplification)
- Many believe friction only depends on **contact area** — actually depends on normal force and coefficient, not area size
- **Stretch characteristics** are often assumed to be purely a function of material type — but construction (weave, width) matters as much
- **Knot strength reduction** is frequently underestimated — knotting can reduce strength by 50% or more
- Students may think **deceleration distance** doesn't matter much — but it's the primary factor in impact force (longer distance = much lower force)

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic physics** — needed for: tension, friction, equilibrium, vector resolution, momentum. Without this, force calculations are inaccessible.
- **Fundamental anatomy and kinesiology** — needed for: climbing biomechanics, torque on joints, injury risk assessment. Required to understand body loading patterns.
- **Beginner-level aerial silks practice** — needed for: understanding terminology, movement vocabulary, practical context. Without hands-on experience, many concepts remain abstract.

## Advanced Extensions (beyond this curriculum)

- **Advanced materials science** — polymer chemistry, degradation kinetics
- **Sports biomechanics research** — motion capture, EMG analysis
- **Structural engineering** — building integration, permanent installation design
