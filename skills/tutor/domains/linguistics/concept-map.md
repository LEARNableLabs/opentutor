# Linguistics — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Syntax, Semantics, Pragmatics (disciplinary boundaries)** — Three approaches to studying meaning: structure, reference, use
2. **Structural ambiguity** — How identical word sequences can have different syntactic structures leading to different meanings
3. **Grammaticality vs meaningfulness vs felicity** — Three dimensions of linguistic well-formedness
4. **Constituency** — Words grouping into phrases that act as units
5. **Tree diagrams** — Hierarchical representations of sentence structure
6. **Phrase structure rules** — Formal rules generating syntactic structures (S → NP VP, etc.)
7. **X-bar theory** — Universal structural template for phrases (every phrase has head, complement, specifier)
8. **Movement operations** — Syntactic transformations displacing constituents (wh-movement, passive, etc.)
9. **Binding theory** — Constraints on pronoun and reflexive interpretation based on syntactic structure
10. **C-command** — Structural relation governing binding, scope, and other phenomena
11. **Sense vs Reference** — Frege's distinction between meaning and denotation. Depends on: basic semantic intuitions
12. **Semantic relations** — Synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy structuring the lexicon. Depends on: sense
13. **Lexical decomposition** — Breaking complex word meanings into semantic primitives. Depends on: semantic relations
14. **Compositionality** — Meanings of complex expressions derived systematically from parts. Depends on: lexical meaning
15. **Truth conditions** — Specification of when a sentence is true. Depends on: compositionality
16. **Quantifiers** — Expressions like 'every', 'some', 'no' and their logical properties. Depends on: truth conditions
17. **Scope** — Structural domain of quantifiers and operators. Depends on: tree structure, quantifiers
18. **Modality** — Necessity and possibility expressed linguistically. Depends on: truth conditions, possible worlds
19. **Literal vs intended meaning** — Gap between sentence meaning and speaker meaning
20. **Gricean maxims** — Cooperative principles governing conversation (quality, quantity, relation, manner)
21. **Conversational implicature** — Meanings conveyed by flouting or observing maxims. Depends on: Gricean maxims
22. **Speech acts** — Actions performed through language (promising, requesting, declaring). Depends on: literal vs intended meaning
23. **Illocutionary force** — Type of speech act performed by an utterance. Depends on: speech acts
24. **Presupposition** — Background assumptions taken for granted by utterances. Depends on: truth conditions
25. **Presupposition projection** — How presuppositions behave under negation, questions, modals. Depends on: presupposition
26. **Deixis** — Context-dependent reference (I, here, now, this). Depends on: reference, context
27. **Syntax-semantics interface** — How syntactic structure constrains semantic interpretation. Depends on: tree structure, compositionality
28. **Argument structure** — Mapping between syntactic positions and semantic roles. Depends on: syntax-semantics interface
29. **Pragmatic enrichment** — Context filling in underspecified semantic content. Depends on: compositionality, implicature
30. **Discourse structure** — Organization of multi-sentence texts and conversations. Depends on: pragmatic principles

## Dependencies

### Major dependency chains

1. **Syntactic analysis track**: Constituency → Tree diagrams → Phrase structure rules → X-bar theory → Movement → Binding
2. **Semantic composition track**: Sense/reference → Lexical relations → Decomposition → Compositionality → Truth conditions → Quantifiers/Scope
3. **Pragmatic inference track**: Literal/intended meaning → Gricean maxims → Implicature → Speech acts → Presupposition → Deixis
4. **Integration track**: All three foundations → Interface phenomena → Unified analysis

### Critical dependencies

- **Scope** requires understanding both syntactic structure (where quantifiers appear) and semantic interpretation (what domains they range over)
- **Binding theory** requires both syntactic concepts (c-command) and semantic concepts (reference)
- **Presupposition projection** requires understanding how semantic content behaves under syntactic embedding
- **Argument structure** bridges syntax (grammatical functions like subject/object) and semantics (semantic roles like agent/patient)
- **Pragmatic enrichment** shows how syntactic and semantic underspecification is resolved through contextual reasoning

## Bottleneck Concepts

1. **Tree diagrams** — Students must be fluent in syntactic tree notation before proceeding to X-bar theory, movement, or binding. This is the representational foundation for all syntactic analysis.

2. **Compositionality** — Understanding how meanings combine is essential for both formal semantics and the syntax-semantics interface. Without this, students can't progress to truth conditions or quantification.

3. **Gricean maxims** — These underpin all pragmatic reasoning. Implicature, indirect speech acts, and many aspects of discourse interpretation depend on this framework.

4. **Scope** — This concept requires integrating syntactic structure with semantic interpretation, making it a critical test of whether students understand how the two systems interact.

5. **The three-way distinction (syntax/semantics/pragmatics)** — Students must maintain clear boundaries between structural patterns, truth-conditional meaning, and context-dependent interpretation. Confusion here derails everything.

## Mind-Blowing Moments

1. **Structural ambiguity reveals hidden hierarchy** — Realizing that "old men and women" has two meanings not because words are ambiguous but because structure is.

2. **Movement leaves invisible traces** — The idea that "What did you see ___?" has a silent element at the gap position revolutionizes how we think about sentence structure.

3. **Compositionality paradox** — If "furiously sleep" has a compositional meaning but "colorless green ideas" doesn't, where's the boundary? Challenges naive views of meaningfulness.

4. **Gricean reasoning creates meaning from nothing** — "Some students passed" implicates "not all" through pure reasoning about conversational cooperation, not word meaning.

5. **Presupposition survives negation** — "The king of France is not bald" still presupposes France has a king, showing meaning isn't just about truth conditions.

6. **Syntax constrains interpretation** — Quantifier scope shows that meaning isn't just about words and world, but about invisible structural relations (c-command, etc.).

7. **Pragmatics can override semantics** — "The ham sandwich wants his check" is semantically bizarre but pragmatically transparent in context.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Syntax is just grammar rules from school"** — Students conflate prescriptive rules (don't split infinitives) with descriptive structural patterns. Syntax is about mental representations, not etiquette.

2. **"Semantics is about dictionary definitions"** — Lexical semantics is much more than listing definitions; it involves systematic relations, decomposition, and compositionality.

3. **"Pragmatics is just politeness and inference"** — Pragmatics is a systematic domain with formal theories (Grice, Relevance Theory, etc.), not just common-sense reasoning.

4. **"Tree diagrams are arbitrary notation"** — Students see trees as mere visual aids, missing that they make empirical claims about constituent structure and hierarchical relations.

5. **"Ambiguity means a word has multiple meanings"** — Scope ambiguity and structural ambiguity don't involve lexical ambiguity at all; the confusion reveals lack of understanding about levels of representation.

6. **"Presupposition and entailment are the same"** — Both involve meaning relations, but they behave completely differently under negation and questioning (projection problem).

7. **"Implicature is just reading between the lines"** — Conversational implicature follows systematic patterns based on maxim-flouting, not random guessing about speaker intent.

8. **"Speech acts are just obvious (questions ask, commands order)"** — Indirect speech acts show the relationship between form and function is complex and context-dependent.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic grammar** (parts of speech, clause types, phrase types) — needed for constituency, tree diagrams, phrase structure rules
- **Formal logic** (quantifiers, truth values, logical operators) — needed for truth conditions, quantification, modal semantics
- **Set theory basics** (sets, membership, relations) — needed for formal semantics, semantic relations
- **Symbolic notation comfort** — needed for all formal representations (trees, lambda calculus, logical forms)

## Integration Points

### Within linguistics
- **Syntax-semantics**: Argument structure, quantifier scope, binding and anaphora
- **Semantics-pragmatics**: Presupposition vs implicature, literal vs speaker meaning, context-dependency
- **Syntax-pragmatics**: Information structure (topic/focus), word order variation, ellipsis

### Cross-disciplinary connections
- **Philosophy of language**: Frege, Russell, Austin, Searle, Grice — linguistic analysis informed by philosophical questions about meaning and reference
- **Logic**: First-order logic, modal logic, lambda calculus — formal tools adapted for natural language semantics
- **Psychology**: Language processing, ambiguity resolution, garden path effects — how syntactic and semantic representations are computed in real time
- **Computer Science**: Formal grammars, parsing algorithms, computational semantics — NLP applications of linguistic theory
- **Anthropology**: Cross-linguistic variation in syntax, semantics, and pragmatics reveals universal vs language-specific patterns
