# Linguistics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Linguistics (syntax, semantics, pragmatics) at intermediate level requires balancing **formal precision** with **linguistic intuition**. Students need hands-on practice with tree diagrams and logical notation, but also need to develop the linguist's skill of constructing minimal pairs and diagnostic tests. Use real examples from everyday language, news articles, and conversations — not invented academic sentences. Encourage students to become "data collectors" who notice ambiguities, implicatures, and structural patterns in the wild. The three subfields should be taught as **interconnected but distinct**: syntax provides structure, semantics maps structure to meaning, pragmatics bridges meaning to use.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Syntax is about correctness; linguists judge which sentences are wrong"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Confusion between prescriptive grammar (school rules) and descriptive syntax (mental representations)
   - **How to correct it**: Show that linguists study all varieties of language equally. "I ain't got none" is syntactically systematic. The question isn't "is it correct?" but "what's its structure?"

2. **"Tree diagrams are just a fancy way to diagram sentences like in elementary school"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Superficial similarity to traditional sentence diagramming
   - **How to correct it**: Emphasize that trees make empirical claims. They predict constituency tests, movement possibilities, binding domains. Show how different tree structures for "old men and women" predict different meanings.

3. **"Words have fixed meanings you look up in the dictionary"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Everyday experience with dictionaries; conflating lexical knowledge with encyclopedic knowledge
   - **How to correct it**: Demonstrate sense vs reference (Frege's "morning star" and "evening star"). Show how context shifts reference ("I", "here", "now"). Explore semantic relations (synonymy, hyponymy) that go beyond definitions.

4. **"Semantics is truth and pragmatics is everything else"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Oversimplified boundary drawn in introductory presentations
   - **How to correct it**: Show interface phenomena. Presupposition straddles the boundary (semantic in projection behavior, pragmatic in accommodation). Quantifier domain restriction is semantic but contextually determined. The boundary is empirical, not definitional.

5. **"Implicature is just guessing what the speaker means"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Conflating Gricean reasoning with folk psychology
   - **How to correct it**: Demonstrate that implicatures are **cancellable, calculable, and non-detachable** — systematic properties that distinguish them from entailments. Walk through the derivation: "She said X, but the maxim of Quantity says she should have said Y if she could, so she must believe not-Y."

6. **"Ambiguity means you can't tell what the speaker meant"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Everyday use of "ambiguous" for vagueness or uncertainty
   - **How to correct it**: Distinguish **structural ambiguity** (two parses), **lexical ambiguity** (polysemy), **scope ambiguity** (quantifier relations), and **vagueness** (underspecification). Show how context resolves ambiguity pragmatically.

7. **"Speech acts are just obvious from sentence type: questions ask, imperatives command"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Prototypical cases seem to map form to function directly
   - **How to correct it**: Introduce indirect speech acts. "Can you pass the salt?" is interrogative form but directive force. "I'd like you to clean your room" is declarative form but directive force. The mapping is conventional but defeasible.

8. **"Movement in syntax is just rearranging words"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Surface appearance that movement = different word order
   - **How to correct it**: Show that movement leaves traces/copies and creates dependencies. Island constraints show it's not arbitrary rearrangement. "What do you wonder whether John bought?" is ungrammatical because of structure, not word order.

9. **"'Every student read a book' is ambiguous because it's unclear how many books"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Pragmatic vagueness about quantities masks structural scope ambiguity
   - **How to correct it**: Force the two readings with continuations. "Every student read a book — in fact, they all read Romeo and Juliet" (surface scope, one book) vs "Every student read a book — some read novels, others read textbooks" (inverse scope, multiple books). The ambiguity is structural.

10. **"Binding is just about what pronouns refer to"**
    - **Why students get this wrong**: Focus on interpretation rather than structural constraints
    - **How to correct it**: Show that binding theory predicts **impossibilities**. "Himself likes John" is ungrammatical no matter who you want to refer to. The constraints are syntactic (c-command, domains) not just pragmatic.

11. **"Presupposition is background information"**
    - **Why students get this wrong**: Informal use of "presuppose" in everyday language
    - **How to correct it**: Demonstrate projection: "John stopped smoking" presupposes he used to smoke, and this survives under negation, questions, modals. Entailments don't project this way. Presupposition has formal properties.

12. **"Natural languages are too messy for formal analysis"**
    - **Why students get this wrong**: Exposure to language variation, performance errors, genuine ambiguity
    - **How to correct it**: Emphasize competence vs performance. Linguists study the systematic patterns (competence) abstracting away from noise. Show how formal tools (phrase structure rules, truth conditions, Gricean maxims) capture real patterns despite surface messiness.

## Level Adjustments

### For intermediate students

- **Assume**: Basic grammar terminology (noun, verb, phrase, clause), comfort with abstract notation, willingness to engage with formal systems
- **Emphasize**: 
  - Diagnostic tests and argumentation (how do we know X?), not just learning rules
  - Integration across the three subfields
  - Real linguistic data from diverse sources
  - Cross-linguistic variation to test theoretical claims
- **Skip**: 
  - Full formal semantics (lambda calculus, type theory) — save for advanced level
  - Highly technical syntactic frameworks (Minimalist Program details)
  - Formal pragmatics (dynamic semantics, game theory)
- **Depth of formalism**: Use labeled trees, logical notation for truth conditions, and Gricean reasoning. Stop short of full derivational frameworks or model-theoretic semantics.

### How this differs from beginner level

Beginners focus on **existence proofs** (ambiguity exists, structure matters, context matters). Intermediates focus on **systematic analysis** (how to diagnose structure, how to calculate truth conditions, how to derive implicatures). More formal notation, more integration, more cross-linguistic data.

### How this differs from advanced level

Advanced students engage with **theoretical debates** (is syntax autonomous? what's the semantics-pragmatics boundary? which semantic framework is correct?), read primary literature, and work with formal tools (lambda calculus, dynamic semantics, computational implementations). Intermediate level presents established frameworks as analytical tools, not contested theories.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

1. **Garden path sentences reveal real-time parsing**
   - Example: "The horse raced past the barn fell" is grammatical but hard to parse
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 27 (interface phenomena) or during syntax review
   - Why it's fascinating: Shows how syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic information interact during sentence processing. Connects to psychology and computational linguistics.

2. **Montague's revolutionary claim: "English is a formal language"**
   - Example: Natural language semantics uses same tools as logic — functions, types, composition
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 14 (compositionality) or Lesson 15 (truth conditions)
   - Why it's fascinating: Bridges humanities and formal sciences. Inspired computational semantics and natural language understanding.

3. **Austin's performatives: saying is doing**
   - Example: "I hereby pronounce you married" creates the marriage by being uttered
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 22 (speech acts)
   - Why it's fascinating: Challenges truth-conditional semantics. Some sentences aren't evaluated as true/false but as felicitous/infelicitous. Led to entire field of speech act theory.

4. **Chomsky's poverty of stimulus argument**
   - Example: Children know constraints (island violations, binding principles) despite never hearing negative evidence
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 8 (binding) or when discussing movement
   - Why it's fascinating: Connects syntax to cognitive science and philosophy of mind. Motivates Universal Grammar hypothesis.

5. **Cross-linguistic variation in pragmatics: honorifics and politeness systems**
   - Example: Japanese, Korean, Thai have grammaticalized honorific systems encoding social relationships
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 22 (speech acts) or Lesson 30 (cross-linguistic variation)
   - Why it's fascinating: Shows pragmatics isn't just inference — some pragmatic distinctions are encoded in grammar. Connects to anthropology and sociolinguistics.

6. **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis: does language shape thought?**
   - Example: Do speakers of languages without absolute direction terms (left/right) think about space differently?
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 30 (cross-linguistic variation) or Lesson 31 (research frontiers)
   - Why it's fascinating: Classic debate in linguistics and cognitive science. Recent experimental work provides nuanced answers. Connects semantics to cognition and culture.

7. **The colorless green ideas sentence and degrees of deviance**
   - Example: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is syntactically fine, semantically contradictory, pragmatically bizarre
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 3 (grammaticality vs meaningfulness vs felicity)
   - Why it's fascinating: Chomsky's famous example separating syntax from semantics. Raises deep questions about compositionality and selectional restrictions.

8. **Russell's analysis of definite descriptions**
   - Example: "The king of France is bald" analyzed as quantified structure, not subject-predicate
   - When to drop this in: Lesson 16 (quantifiers) or Lesson 23 (presupposition)
   - Why it's fascinating: Shows how logical form can diverge from surface syntax. Central to philosophy of language and the semantics-pragmatics boundary.

## Difficulty Progression

**Lessons 1-4 (Foundations)**: Difficulty 1-2. Build intuitions about the three subfields and why we need separate frameworks. Concrete examples, minimal formalism.

**Lessons 5-10 (Syntactic Structure)**: Difficulty 2-4. Ramp up quickly through tree diagrams (3), X-bar theory (4), movement (4), binding (4). This is the steepest climb. Students need extensive practice with notation and constituency tests. Review at lesson 10 consolidates.

**Lessons 11-18 (Semantics)**: Difficulty 2-4. Start accessible (sense/reference, semantic relations), build to truth conditions (4), quantifiers (4), modals (4). The formalism is different from syntax (logical notation vs trees), which can be both relief and challenge. Review at lesson 18.

**Lessons 19-25 (Pragmatics)**: Difficulty 2-4. More intuitive entry point (indirect speech acts), but Gricean reasoning (3), implicature (3), and presupposition (4) require careful argumentation. Students often find this material engaging because it connects to everyday experience. Review at lesson 25.

**Lessons 26-32 (Integration)**: Difficulty 3-4. Hardest material requires synthesizing all three frameworks. Interface phenomena, integrated analysis, and theoretical debates. Final review (32) at difficulty 2 to end on confident note.

**Overall arc**: Steep climb into syntax (lessons 5-8), plateau during semantics and pragmatics (lessons 11-25 with three reviews), peak difficulty during integration (lessons 26-30), final consolidation.

## Assessment Strategies

1. **Tree diagram construction**: Give a novel sentence, ask for tree. Tests syntactic analysis skills. Look for correct constituency, labeling, and X-bar structure.

2. **Constituency test application**: Give a sentence and ask student to apply substitution, movement, or coordination tests to prove a constituent. Tests understanding of diagnostics.

3. **Truth condition specification**: Give a sentence with quantifiers or modals, ask for precise truth conditions. Tests semantic analysis. Look for scope handling.

4. **Implicature derivation**: Give a conversation exchange, ask what is implicated and how (which maxim is relevant). Tests pragmatic reasoning.

5. **Minimal pair construction**: Ask student to create two minimally different sentences that differ in a specific way (scope ambiguity, binding possibility, implicature). Tests generative understanding.

6. **Cross-linguistic comparison**: Give data from an unfamiliar language, ask student to analyze using syntactic, semantic, or pragmatic tools. Tests transfer of skills.

7. **Integrated analysis**: Give a complex real-world example (newspaper headline, conversation, advertisement), ask for analysis at all three levels. Tests synthesis.

8. **Argumentation**: Present a claim (e.g., "presupposition is semantic, not pragmatic"), ask student to provide evidence for or against. Tests theoretical understanding.

### What to look for

- **Precision**: Do they use technical terms correctly? Do they distinguish entailment from implicature, scope from ambiguity, c-command from linear order?
- **Systematicity**: Do they apply tests consistently? Can they extend patterns to new cases?
- **Integration**: Can they see how syntax constrains semantics, how semantics underdetermines pragmatics, how all three interact?
- **Argumentation**: Do they give evidence for claims, or just assert opinions? Do they consider counterexamples?

### Common weak spots

- Conflating levels (treating structural phenomena as semantic, semantic phenomena as pragmatic)
- Overgeneralizing from prototypes (thinking all ambiguity is lexical, all speech acts match sentence type)
- Difficulty with formalism (notation obscures rather than clarifies)
- Missing the point of diagnostics (memorizing tests without understanding what they prove)
