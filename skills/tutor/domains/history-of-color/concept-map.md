# History of Color — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Pigments vs. Dyes** — fundamental material distinction: particles vs. molecules
2. **Iron Oxides (Ochres)** — simplest natural pigments, prehistoric use
3. **Pigment Analysis** — how we identify ancient materials through science
4. **Synthesis** — creating colors that don't exist in nature. Depends on: Chemistry fundamentals
5. **Egyptian Blue** — first synthetic pigment (calcium copper silicate)
6. **Tyrian Purple** — organic dye from mollusks, labor-intensive extraction. Depends on: Pigments vs. Dyes
7. **Vermilion** — mercury sulfide synthesis, alchemy to chemistry. Depends on: Synthesis
8. **Indigo** — plant-based dye, fermentation chemistry. Depends on: Pigments vs. Dyes
9. **Madder/Alizarin** — root-based red dye, color fastness. Depends on: Pigments vs. Dyes
10. **Ultramarine** — ground lapis lazuli, extreme value. Depends on: Pigments vs. Dyes
11. **Alchemy** — proto-chemistry, experimental method contributing to color innovation. Depends on: Synthesis
12. **Binders** — oil vs. tempera, how medium affects color. Depends on: Pigments vs. Dyes
13. **Pigment Degradation** — chemical instability over time. Depends on: Synthesis, Binders
14. **Synthetic Dyes** — mauveine as breakthrough (1856). Depends on: Synthesis, Alchemy
15. **Industrial Chemistry** — coal tar derivatives, mass production. Depends on: Synthetic Dyes
16. **Economic Disruption** — synthetic colors collapse natural dye markets. Depends on: Industrial Chemistry
17. **Democratization of Color** — how synthetics changed fashion and class. Depends on: Economic Disruption
18. **Color Symbolism** — cultural meanings vary (white = purity vs. mourning)
19. **Gender and Color** — pink/blue reversal in 20th century. Depends on: Color Symbolism
20. **Religious Color** — saffron, purple, ritual significance. Depends on: Color Symbolism
21. **Political Color** — red flags, symbolic resistance. Depends on: Color Symbolism
22. **Cross-Cultural Perception** — color naming, linguistic relativity. Depends on: Color Symbolism
23. **Color Theory** — Newton's prism vs. Goethe's phenomenology
24. **Color Vision** — cones, rods, opponent process. Depends on: Color Theory
25. **Additive vs. Subtractive** — RGB vs. CMYK, screen vs. print. Depends on: Color Theory
26. **Conservation** — preserving degraded pigments. Depends on: Pigment Degradation, Pigment Analysis

## Dependencies

### Material Science Chain
**Pigments vs. Dyes** → **Synthesis** → **Synthetic Dyes** → **Industrial Chemistry** → **Economic Disruption**

This is the primary technical arc: understanding the fundamental difference between pigments and dyes enables understanding synthesis, which unlocks the synthetic dye revolution and its economic consequences.

### Cultural Chain
**Color Symbolism** → branches into **Gender and Color**, **Religious Color**, **Political Color**, **Cross-Cultural Perception**

Cultural meanings are independent of chemistry but interconnect with material availability (e.g., Tyrian purple's rarity reinforced its royal symbolism).

### Perception Chain
**Color Theory** → **Color Vision** → **Additive vs. Subtractive**

Understanding how humans perceive color (theory) requires understanding the biology (vision), which explains why different color systems exist.

### Cross-Dependencies
- **Ultramarine** depends on **Pigments vs. Dyes** (material distinction) and connects to **Color Symbolism** (why it was reserved for sacred subjects)
- **Conservation** depends on both **Pigment Degradation** (chemical processes) and **Pigment Analysis** (detection methods)
- **Economic Disruption** connects to **Democratization of Color**, which relates to **Color Symbolism** (when anyone can afford purple, does it lose royal meaning?)

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic Chemistry** — needed for: Synthesis, Vermilion, Egyptian Blue, Synthetic Dyes, Industrial Chemistry, Pigment Degradation
- **Art History Awareness** — needed for: Ultramarine, Binders, Renaissance context, Religious Color
- **Cultural History Interest** — needed for: Color Symbolism, Gender and Color, Political Color, Cross-Cultural Perception

## Bottlenecks

### Synthesis (Concept 4)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Every later chemical innovation (Egyptian Blue, Synthetic Dyes, Industrial Chemistry) depends on understanding that humans can create colors not found in nature.

**How to teach**: Use Egyptian Blue as the exemplar — concrete, ancient, and demonstrable. Don't get abstract about "synthesis" in general.

### Pigments vs. Dyes (Concept 1)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: Students who don't grasp this distinction will struggle with every material-specific lesson (Tyrian Purple, Indigo, Ultramarine, Synthetic Dyes).

**How to teach**: Use physical analogy — sand in water (pigment suspension) vs. sugar in water (dye solution). Emphasize binding mechanism differences.

### Color Symbolism (Concept 18)
**Why it's a bottleneck**: All cultural meaning lessons depend on accepting that color associations are learned, not universal.

**How to teach**: Lead with a strong counter-example (white = mourning vs. purity) to disrupt assumptions early.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Pigments and dyes are the same thing"** — addressed by Concept 1, reinforced throughout
2. **"Ancient people didn't have chemistry"** — addressed by Egyptian Blue, Vermilion, showing systematic experimentation
3. **"Color meanings are universal"** — addressed by Color Symbolism, Cross-Cultural Perception
4. **"Synthetic always means inferior"** — addressed by Economic Disruption (synthetic alizarin chemically identical to natural)
5. **"RGB and CMYK are just different formats"** — addressed by Additive vs. Subtractive (fundamentally different physics)
