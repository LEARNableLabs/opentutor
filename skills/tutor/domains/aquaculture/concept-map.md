# Aquaculture and Fisheries Science — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Fisheries value** — economic, ecological, and social importance of fish populations
2. **Trophic levels** — energy flow through marine food webs
3. **Energy transfer** — efficiency of energy movement between trophic levels
4. **Population dynamics** — how birth, death, and growth determine population size
5. **Recruitment** — addition of new individuals to a population. Depends on: population dynamics
6. **Mortality** — death from natural causes and fishing. Depends on: population dynamics
7. **Stock assessment** — methods to estimate population abundance and trends. Depends on: population dynamics, recruitment, mortality
8. **Catch data** — fisheries-dependent information. Depends on: stock assessment
9. **Survey methods** — fisheries-independent sampling. Depends on: stock assessment
10. **Overfishing** — harvest rate exceeding sustainable levels. Depends on: mortality, population dynamics
11. **Stock collapse** — population crash from overfishing. Depends on: overfishing, recruitment failure
12. **Surplus production** — growth available for harvest. Depends on: population dynamics, recruitment
13. **Maximum sustainable yield (MSY)** — largest catch that can be taken indefinitely. Depends on: surplus production, population dynamics
14. **Precautionary principle** — err on the side of conservation when uncertain. Depends on: stock assessment uncertainty
15. **Reference points** — biological and fishing mortality thresholds. Depends on: MSY, stock assessment
16. **Quota systems** — catch limits to prevent overfishing. Depends on: MSY, reference points
17. **ITQs (Individual Transferable Quotas)** — market-based quota allocation. Depends on: quota systems
18. **Common pool resources** — tragedy of the commons in fisheries. Depends on: overfishing, quota systems
19. **Bycatch** — non-target species caught incidentally. Depends on: fishing methods
20. **Ecosystem-based management** — managing whole systems, not just target species. Depends on: trophic levels, food web structure, bycatch
21. **Trophic cascades** — top-down effects through food webs. Depends on: trophic levels, ecosystem-based management
22. **Aquaculture systems** — controlled fish production environments
23. **Freshwater culture** — ponds, raceways, lakes. Depends on: aquaculture systems
24. **Marine aquaculture** — cages, offshore farms. Depends on: aquaculture systems
25. **Recirculating systems (RAS)** — intensive indoor systems with water recycling. Depends on: aquaculture systems, water quality management
26. **Biofilters** — biological waste treatment in RAS. Depends on: recirculating systems
27. **Aquaculture feeds** — nutritional inputs for farmed fish. Depends on: aquaculture systems
28. **Fishmeal** — rendered fish used in feeds. Depends on: aquaculture feeds, wild fisheries
29. **Feed conversion ratio (FCR)** — efficiency of feed use. Depends on: aquaculture feeds
30. **Alternative proteins** — non-fishmeal feed ingredients. Depends on: aquaculture feeds, sustainability
31. **Supply chains** — movement from harvest to consumer
32. **Certification schemes** — MSC, ASC standards for sustainability. Depends on: overfishing, aquaculture impacts
33. **Eco-labels** — consumer-facing sustainability markers. Depends on: certification schemes
34. **Small-scale fisheries** — community-based, artisanal fishing
35. **Food security** — reliable protein supply. Depends on: fisheries value, aquaculture, small-scale fisheries
36. **Social equity** — fair access to fishery resources. Depends on: quota systems, small-scale fisheries
37. **Ocean acidification** — CO2 absorption lowering pH. Depends on: climate change
38. **Range shifts** — species moving poleward with warming. Depends on: climate change, population dynamics
39. **Climate adaptation** — adjusting management to changing conditions. Depends on: range shifts, ocean acidification
40. **IMTA (Integrated Multi-Trophic Aquaculture)** — polyculture using waste from one species as input for another. Depends on: trophic levels, aquaculture systems
41. **Sustainability synthesis** — balancing ecology, economics, and social needs. Depends on: ecosystem-based management, food security, certification schemes

## Dependencies

### Ecological Foundation
- **Trophic levels** and **energy transfer** are foundational to understanding marine food webs and ecosystem-based management
- **Population dynamics** builds on basic ecology and requires understanding of recruitment and mortality
- **Ecosystem-based management** requires understanding trophic levels, food webs, and trophic cascades

### Stock Assessment Chain
- **Stock assessment** depends on population dynamics, recruitment, and mortality
- **Catch data** and **survey methods** are two approaches to stock assessment
- **Reference points** and **MSY** require stock assessment data and models
- **Quota systems** are built on reference points and MSY calculations

### Fisheries Management Progression
- **Overfishing** concept builds on mortality and population dynamics
- **Stock collapse** is an outcome of sustained overfishing combined with recruitment failure
- **ITQs** and **quota systems** are management responses to overfishing
- **Ecosystem-based management** extends beyond single-species quotas to consider bycatch and food web effects

### Aquaculture Technology Stack
- **Aquaculture systems** (general concept) branches into freshwater, marine, and recirculating systems
- **RAS** requires understanding biofilters and water quality management
- **Aquaculture feeds** connect to wild fisheries through fishmeal, creating sustainability tension
- **Feed conversion ratio** measures efficiency and sustainability of feeds
- **IMTA** integrates trophic levels concept into aquaculture design

### Economics & Sustainability
- **Certification schemes** (MSC, ASC) require understanding both overfishing and aquaculture impacts
- **Supply chains** connect production to markets
- **Food security** depends on both wild fisheries and aquaculture productivity
- **Social equity** intersects with quota allocation and small-scale fisheries access

### Climate & Adaptation
- **Ocean acidification** and **range shifts** are climate impacts on marine ecosystems
- **Climate adaptation** strategies must account for shifting stocks and changing recruitment patterns

## Critical Bottlenecks

### Bottleneck 1: Population Dynamics
Students must grasp the **interplay of recruitment, growth, and mortality** before stock assessment makes sense. This is often counterintuitive (e.g., why fishing can sometimes increase per-capita growth rates in dense populations).

### Bottleneck 2: MSY Limitations
**Maximum sustainable yield** is both central to fisheries management AND deeply flawed. Students need to understand the concept before critiquing it (via precautionary principle and ecosystem-based management).

### Bottleneck 3: Fishmeal Paradox
**Aquaculture feeds** create a paradox: farming fish to reduce wild harvest pressure, but using wild fish as feed inputs. This connects wild fisheries, aquaculture, and sustainability in a complex loop. Students often miss this until explicitly pointed out.

### Bottleneck 4: Trade-offs in Sustainability
There is no "perfect" system. Intensive RAS has low environmental impact but high energy use. Extensive ponds are energy-light but land-intensive. Ocean cages can pollute but use no freshwater. Students struggle with multi-dimensional trade-offs.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Farmed fish don't impact wild populations"** — FALSE. Feed production, escapes, disease transmission, and market substitution all create links.

2. **"MSY is the goal of fisheries management"** — OUTDATED. Modern management uses precautionary reference points and ecosystem considerations, not just MSY.

3. **"Stock assessment is just counting fish"** — OVERSIMPLIFIED. It's statistical inference from multiple data streams, with significant uncertainty.

4. **"Bycatch is a minor issue"** — FALSE for many fisheries. It can be larger than target catch and drive ecosystem changes.

5. **"Aquaculture is always more sustainable than wild fishing"** — DEPENDS. Wild fisheries for low-trophic species (sardines) can be highly sustainable; intensive carnivorous fish farming may not be.

6. **"Climate change just means fish move north"** — INCOMPLETE. Also affects phenology, recruitment, food webs, ocean chemistry, and extreme events.

7. **"Small-scale fisheries are unimportant"** — FALSE. They employ more people and provide more food security in developing nations than industrial fisheries.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic ecology** — needed for trophic levels, food webs, ecosystem concepts
- **Population biology** — needed for population dynamics, recruitment, mortality
- **Introductory economics** — needed for supply chains, market dynamics, quota systems, common pool resources

## Cross-Connections to Other Domains

- **Marine ecology** — deep overlap on food webs, ecosystems
- **Conservation biology** — overfishing, stock collapse, habitat protection
- **Environmental economics** — quota systems, ITQs, common pool resources, valuation
- **Climate science** — ocean acidification, warming, range shifts
- **Food systems** — supply chains, food security, protein sources
- **Policy & governance** — quota allocation, certification, international agreements (UNCLOS, RFMOs)
