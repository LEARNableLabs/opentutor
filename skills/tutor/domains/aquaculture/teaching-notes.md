# Aquaculture and Fisheries Science — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Aquaculture and fisheries science sits at the intersection of **ecology, economics, and policy** — pure science alone won't capture it. At the intermediate level, students should move beyond "save the fish" narratives to grapple with **trade-offs, uncertainty, and multi-stakeholder conflicts**. Use real-world case studies (cod collapse, salmon farming debates, tuna quota wars) to make abstract models concrete. The field is inherently applied and data-driven, so lean into datasets (FishBase, RAM Legacy, FAO stats) and practical tools (stock assessment software, certification schemes). Balance biological rigor with social context — fisheries management is as much about people as fish.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Aquaculture solves the overfishing problem"
**Why students get this wrong:** Intuitive logic — if we farm fish, we don't need to catch wild ones.

**Why it's false:** Most farmed carnivorous fish (salmon, tuna, sea bass) are fed fishmeal and fish oil from wild-caught forage fish. The feed conversion ratio means it takes 2-5 kg of wild fish to produce 1 kg of farmed salmon. Aquaculture can *reduce* pressure on specific stocks (e.g., Atlantic salmon) but shifts it to forage fisheries (anchovies, sardines).

**How to correct:** Show the feed production chain. Discuss alternative proteins (insect meal, algae, soy) and herbivorous farmed species (tilapia, carp) as partial solutions. Use the "fishmeal paradox" framing.

### 2. "Maximum sustainable yield (MSY) is the scientific goal"
**Why students get this wrong:** MSY is ubiquitous in fisheries literature and sounds scientifically rigorous.

**Why it's false:** MSY is a single-species, equilibrium concept that ignores ecosystem interactions, assumes perfect data, and maximizes catch rather than economic or social value. Modern management uses precautionary reference points (e.g., BMSY as a limit, not a target) and ecosystem-based approaches.

**How to correct:** Teach MSY as a historical concept, then immediately critique it. Introduce precautionary principle and ecosystem-based management as responses to MSY's limitations. Use the cod collapse as a case study of MSY failure.

### 3. "Stock assessment is just counting fish"
**Why students get this wrong:** The term "assessment" sounds empirical and straightforward.

**Why it's false:** Stock assessment is **statistical inference** from noisy, incomplete data (catch records, survey samples, age structure). It involves complex models (surplus production, age-structured, ecosystem models) with significant uncertainty. You never count all the fish.

**How to correct:** Emphasize the challenge of observing underwater populations. Show how catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) is a proxy, not a direct measure. Discuss why fisheries scientists report confidence intervals and management must account for uncertainty.

### 4. "Bycatch is a minor side effect of fishing"
**Why students get this wrong:** Media and marketing focus on target species (tuna, salmon), not incidentals.

**Why it's false:** In some fisheries (shrimp trawls, tuna longlines), bycatch can exceed target catch. Bycatch drives population declines in sea turtles, seabirds, sharks, and marine mammals. It's a major driver of ecosystem change.

**How to correct:** Provide bycatch ratios (e.g., 10:1 for some shrimp fisheries). Discuss ecosystem-based management as a response. Show how gear modifications (turtle excluder devices, circle hooks) can reduce bycatch.

### 5. "Wild fisheries are always better for the environment than aquaculture"
**Why students get this wrong:** "Wild" sounds natural and sustainable; "farming" sounds industrial.

**Why it's false:** It depends on the species, system, and metric. Wild fisheries for low-trophic species (sardines, anchovies) with good management can be highly sustainable. But overfished stocks, destructive gear (bottom trawls), and high bycatch can make wild fisheries worse than well-managed aquaculture. Conversely, intensive salmon farming can have high disease, parasite, and pollution impacts.

**How to correct:** Frame as a trade-off matrix. Compare specific systems (wild Alaskan salmon vs. Norwegian farmed salmon vs. Peruvian anchoveta) on multiple dimensions (carbon, land use, bycatch, escapes, feed inputs). Avoid blanket statements.

### 6. "Climate change just means fish move to cooler water"
**Why students get this wrong:** Range shifts are the most visible and intuitive climate impact.

**Why it's false:** Climate change also affects **recruitment timing** (phenology mismatches with plankton blooms), **growth rates** (metabolic stress), **ocean chemistry** (acidification harming shellfish and larval fish), **oxygen levels** (hypoxia), and **extreme events** (marine heat waves). Range shifts are one piece of a multi-faceted problem.

**How to correct:** Present climate impacts as a syndrome, not a single effect. Use case studies (Pacific cod collapse in Gulf of Alaska 2019, lobster range shift in New England). Discuss adaptation challenges when stocks cross national boundaries.

### 7. "Small-scale fisheries are economically marginal"
**Why students get this wrong:** Industrial fisheries dominate trade and media coverage.

**Why it's false:** Small-scale fisheries employ **~90% of the world's fishers** and provide **critical food security** in developing nations. They often have lower bycatch, less habitat damage, and better local economic distribution than industrial fleets.

**How to correct:** Show FAO statistics on employment and nutrition. Discuss equity issues (ITQs favoring large operators, IUU fishing by industrial fleets in developing nations' waters). Introduce community-based management and co-management models.

### 8. "Recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) are the future"
**Why students get this wrong:** RAS sounds high-tech, land-based, and environmentally clean.

**Why it's partially true but incomplete:** RAS eliminates ocean pollution, escapes, and wild fish interactions — major wins. BUT it requires **high energy inputs** (pumping, filtration, temperature control), **high capital costs**, and **expertise**. It's economically viable for high-value species (salmon, barramundi) but not for bulk protein (tilapia, catfish). It's part of the solution, not the whole solution.

**How to correct:** Present RAS as one system in a portfolio. Compare energy/carbon footprint to other systems. Discuss economic feasibility and where it fits (urban markets, land-locked regions, high-value species).

### 9. "Certification (MSC, ASC) guarantees sustainability"
**Why students get this wrong:** Eco-labels are marketed as definitive judgments.

**Why it's false:** Certification schemes have thresholds, trade-offs, and limitations. MSC has been criticized for certifying fisheries with bycatch issues (Antarctic krill, some tuna). ASC standards vary by species and region. Certification is a **tool for improvement**, not a perfect binary (sustainable/unsustainable).

**How to correct:** Explain how certification works (audits, standards, thresholds). Discuss critiques and improvements. Frame as "better than uncertified" rather than "perfect."

### 10. "Overfishing is a problem of the past"
**Why students get this wrong:** Some high-profile stocks have recovered (striped bass, Pacific halibut, some tuna stocks).

**Why it's false:** According to FAO (2022 SOFIA report), **~35% of global stocks are overfished**, and many recovering stocks are still below target levels. Overfishing persists in developing nations with weak governance, illegal fishing (IUU), and unmanaged fisheries. Climate change is also creating new pressures.

**How to correct:** Show current FAO statistics. Discuss IUU fishing, subsidies that enable overcapacity, and climate-driven stock changes. Celebrate successes (New Zealand, Iceland, Alaska) but emphasize ongoing challenges.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate Level (This Curriculum)
- **Depth:** Balance conceptual understanding with quantitative reasoning. Students should interpret stock assessment graphs, understand MSY models, and analyze trade-offs, but don't need to derive equations or run full assessments.
- **Formalism:** Introduce key equations (surplus production, MSY) conceptually, not derivationally. Focus on interpretation over calculation.
- **Case studies:** Use real examples (cod collapse, salmon farming, tuna quota debates) to ground abstract concepts.
- **Data literacy:** Students should be comfortable reading FishBase, interpreting stock status plots, and comparing fisheries statistics.

### If Teaching at Beginner Level
- Skip stock assessment models (stick to "we estimate populations from catch and surveys").
- Focus on overfishing, basic aquaculture systems, and sustainability narratives.
- Use more visual/narrative resources (videos, infographics) over datasets.
- Simplify trade-offs to binary choices rather than multi-dimensional matrices.

### If Teaching at Advanced Level
- Dive into stock assessment math (surplus production models, age-structured models, Bayesian approaches).
- Introduce bioeconomic models (Gordon-Schaefer, optimal harvest).
- Discuss governance structures (RFMOs, co-management, common pool resource theory).
- Use R or Python for data analysis (RAM Legacy, NOAA stock assessments).
- Engage with primary literature (ICES reports, NOAA stock assessments, Nature/Science papers).

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### When to drop them in:

- **Shifting baselines syndrome** (Daniel Pauly) — Lesson 5 or 10 (overfishing/stock collapse). Each generation accepts a degraded baseline as normal. Connects to psychology and environmental history.

- **The tragedy of the commons** (Garrett Hardin) vs. **Ostrom's commons governance** — Lesson 8 (ITQs). Classic economic framing vs. evidence that communities *can* self-manage. Connects to political economy and institutional design.

- **Trophic cascades and sea otters** — Lesson 9 (ecosystem-based management). Sea otters eat urchins, urchins eat kelp. Remove otters → urchin barrens → no kelp → no fish habitat. Shows food web complexity.

- **The Pauly-Watson debate** — Lesson 10 or 23. Daniel Pauly (academic, data-driven pessimist) vs. Ray Hilborn (academic, management optimist). Genuine scientific disagreement on global fisheries status. Shows that science isn't monolithic.

- **Ghost fishing** — Lesson 9 (bycatch). Lost nets continue catching fish for years. Combines ecology, pollution, and ethics.

- **Aquaponics as a closed-loop system** — Lesson 24 (IMTA). Growing plants using fish waste nutrients. Connects to urban agriculture, food systems, circular economy.

- **Bluefin tuna as a luxury commodity** — Lesson 18 (supply chains). Auction prices >$3M for a single fish. Connects to markets, globalization, and cultural values (sushi).

- **The collapse of Peruvian anchoveta in the 1970s** — Lesson 10 or 23. El Niño + overfishing. Was once the largest fishery in the world. Ties climate, management failure, and international geopolitics.

- **The Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch** — Lesson 19 (certification). Influential consumer guide. Discuss power of markets vs. regulation.

- **Fish welfare in aquaculture** — Lesson 15 or 16. Emerging ethical issue. Do fish feel pain? What are welfare standards for farmed fish? Connects to animal ethics.

- **The Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and EEZs** — Lesson 8 or 20. How ocean property rights work. 200-mile exclusive economic zones. High seas governance. Connects to international law.

- **Jellyfish blooms as a sign of overfishing** — Lesson 23. Removing fish predators can trigger jellyfish population explosions. Regime shifts in ecosystems.

## Difficulty Progression

### Early Lessons (1-6): Gentle Ramp
- Difficulty 1-2: Build foundational concepts (food webs, population dynamics, overfishing basics).
- Mix mini-lessons and questions to engage without overwhelming.
- Review at Lesson 6 to consolidate.

### Middle Lessons (7-17): Build to Peaks
- Difficulty 2-4: Introduce complex concepts (MSY, ITQs, bycatch, RAS, feed paradox).
- Peak difficulty at Lessons 10 (collapse case study), 15 (RAS technology), 21-22 (climate).
- Intersperse real-world and resource-drop lessons to vary cognitive load.
- Reviews at Lessons 11 and 17 to reset.

### Late Lessons (18-25): Integration and Synthesis
- Difficulty 2-4: Economics, certification, climate adaptation, IMTA.
- Peaks at Lessons 21-22 (climate impacts/adaptation) and 24 (IMTA complexity).
- Lesson 23 is a synthesis review (difficulty 2) to pull threads together.
- Final lesson (25) is teach-back at difficulty 3 — students articulate their own vision.

### Use of Review Lessons
- **Lesson 6:** Consolidate population dynamics and stock assessment basics.
- **Lesson 11:** Practice designing a management plan (apply quota/monitoring concepts).
- **Lesson 17:** Compare aquaculture systems on multiple dimensions (trade-off thinking).
- **Lesson 23:** Big-picture synthesis before final integration lessons.

### Adaptive Strategies
- If students struggle with stock assessment (Lessons 4, 7), add a mini-lesson on CPUE or age-structure sampling.
- If they breeze through aquaculture systems (Lessons 12-16), combine or go deeper (biofilter microbiology, feed formulation chemistry).
- If climate impacts (Lessons 21-22) are too abstract, use local case studies (regional fishery shifts).
