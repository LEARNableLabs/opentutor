# Sign Language Linguistics — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Sign language linguistics is fundamentally **visual and spatial**, which makes it both accessible and challenging. The field demands constant code-switching between abstract linguistic analysis and concrete visual examples. At the intermediate level, emphasize **formal analysis** and **cross-linguistic comparison** while maintaining engagement through video examples and real-world applications. This is a field where debates are alive and ongoing — embrace the controversy around iconicity, classifiers, and modality effects rather than presenting settled answers.

The key pedagogical challenge: students need enough familiarity with a sign language to follow examples, but shouldn't over-rely on intuitions from a single language (usually ASL). **Balance concrete examples with theoretical abstraction.**

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Sign languages are universal"
**Why students believe this:** Intuition that visual gestures transcend linguistic boundaries; confusion with international gesture systems.

**Reality:** Sign languages are mutually unintelligible, with distinct grammars, lexicons, and language families (ASL ≠ BSL ≠ JSL, etc.).

**How to correct:** Show minimal pairs across sign languages; discuss historical divergence and language families. Use Nicaraguan Sign Language emergence as a case study — even in the same community, different cohorts can develop different linguistic systems.

### 2. "Sign languages are just manual codes for spoken languages"
**Why students believe this:** Exposure to Signed English or other manual codes; conflation of sign languages with fingerspelling.

**Reality:** Natural sign languages have independent grammars, often with different word orders, morphological strategies, and syntactic structures than surrounding spoken languages.

**How to correct:** Compare ASL and English syntax directly (ASL is more topic-prominent, uses spatial agreement differently, etc.). Discuss the history of oralism and why manual codes were created as educational tools, distinct from natural linguistic evolution.

### 3. "Iconicity means sign languages are less abstract/systematic"
**Why students believe this:** Overemphasis on iconic examples in introductory materials; assumption that form-meaning transparency = lack of arbitrariness.

**Reality:** Iconicity is gradient, conventionalized, and coexists with arbitrariness. Even highly iconic signs have language-specific forms and grammatical constraints.

**How to correct:** Show cross-linguistic variation in iconic signs (e.g., "tree" differs across sign languages despite shared visual referent). Discuss grammaticalization pathways where iconic forms become arbitrary. Use the "iconicity paradox" — if iconicity were primary, sign languages would be mutually intelligible (they're not).

### 4. "Classifiers are just gestures"
**Why students believe this:** High degree of iconicity; productive, non-lexicalized nature; gradient properties.

**Reality:** This is an **active research debate**. Evidence for linguistic status includes: morphological structure, language-specific constraints, categorical handshape systems, grammatical functions. Evidence for gestural status includes: gradient properties, iconic motivation, lack of full conventionalization.

**How to correct:** Present both sides fairly. Most contemporary researchers use the term "depicting signs" to avoid theoretical commitment. Emphasize that the debate reflects deeper questions about the language-gesture boundary, not a simple binary.

### 5. "Non-manual signals are just emotional expressions"
**Why students believe this:** Facial expressions look like affective displays; intuition that body language is paralinguistic.

**Reality:** Non-manual signals have **dual functions**: some are linguistic (syntactic markers, adverbials), some are affective. Crucially, linguistic non-manuals are obligatory, conventionalized, and have scopal properties.

**How to correct:** Show minimal pairs where only the non-manual differs (e.g., raised eyebrows can mark yes/no questions in ASL). Discuss scope: a raised-eyebrow non-manual can extend over an entire relative clause, showing syntactic integration. Compare to prosody in spoken languages — also phonetic but linguistically functional.

### 6. "Role shift is just acting or quotation"
**Why students believe this:** Superficial resemblance to theatrical performance; confusion with direct speech reporting.

**Reality:** Constructed action has **grammatical properties**: obligatory eye gaze shift, body positioning, integration with agreement system. It's not optional stylistic choice.

**How to correct:** Demonstrate grammatical constraints (e.g., obligatoriness in certain contexts, interaction with verb agreement, scope effects). Compare to logophoric pronouns in spoken languages — a grammaticalized perspective-shift system.

### 7. "Spatial grammar is just pointing at things in the room"
**Why students believe this:** Initial examples often use concrete referents; conflation with deictic pointing.

**Reality:** Spatial loci are **abstract grammatical positions**, not physical locations. Abstract entities, hypotheticals, and generic arguments can all be assigned spatial positions.

**How to correct:** Show examples with abstract referents (e.g., "justice" assigned to a locus, then referred back to via agreement). Discuss verb agreement patterns that use space grammatically, not deictically.

### 8. "Sign languages have no word order"
**Why students believe this:** Flexibility in word order; topicalization; high frequency of null arguments.

**Reality:** Sign languages have **basic word orders** (often SOV or SVO depending on the language), with information-structure-driven flexibility. Free word order ≠ no word order.

**How to correct:** Present experimental evidence for basic word order preferences. Discuss topicalization as a marked structure, not the default. Compare to spoken languages with rich topic-prominence (Japanese, Korean) — similar flexibility doesn't mean absence of structure.

### 9. "Sign language phonology is just the four parameters from Stokoe"
**Why students believe this:** Textbook simplification; historical legacy of Stokoe's pioneering work.

**Reality:** Modern phonological models are much richer: orientation, non-manual features, two-handed symmetry constraints, hand-internal movements, etc. Autosegmental and feature geometry models reveal complex structure.

**How to correct:** Present contemporary models (Brentari's Prosodic Model, for example). Show phenomena Stokoe's model can't explain (e.g., movement epenthesis, assimilation patterns).

### 10. "All deaf people use the same sign language"
**Why students believe this:** Conflation of "deaf community" with linguistic homogeneity; lack of awareness of global diversity.

**Reality:** There are 300+ documented sign languages worldwide, with variation by country, region, ethnicity, age, and Deaf community.

**How to correct:** Show maps of sign language families. Discuss BASL (Black ASL) as regional/ethnic variation. Present emerging sign languages (NSL, ABSL, ISN) as evidence of ongoing linguistic diversification.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Learners (Current Target)

**Emphasize:**
- Formal linguistic analysis using standard terminology
- Cross-linguistic comparison (not just ASL)
- Reading primary research papers (with scaffolding)
- Debates and open questions (iconicity, classifiers, modality effects)
- Connections to general linguistic theory

**De-emphasize:**
- Language learning goals (focus on analysis, not fluency)
- Deaf culture and history (mention when relevant, but not the focus)
- Applied/clinical topics (unless student interest emerges)

**Appropriate depth:**
- Can engage with formal notation (glossing conventions, feature matrices)
- Can critique arguments from research papers
- Can identify cross-linguistic patterns and exceptions
- Understands that many questions are unresolved

### Compared to Beginner Level

Beginners need more:
- Concrete examples with videos
- Comparison to spoken language analogues
- Simpler readings (textbook chapters, not research papers)
- Less formalism, more intuitive explanations
- Focus on one sign language (usually ASL) for consistency

Beginners need less:
- Theoretical debates and controversies
- Cross-linguistic typology
- Formal phonological/syntactic models
- Engagement with primary literature

### Compared to Advanced Level

Advanced students engage with:
- Experimental methodologies (corpus analysis, elicitation, neurolinguistics)
- Theoretical frameworks (Generative, Cognitive, Usage-based)
- Independent reading of research papers
- Contributing to ongoing debates
- Specialization in subfields (phonology, morphology, acquisition, etc.)

Advanced students have:
- Deeper familiarity with multiple sign languages
- Experience with data annotation and analysis
- Background in formal linguistics (syntax, semantics, phonology)

## Rabbit Holes

### 1. Emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language
**When to introduce:** Lesson 3 (emerging sign languages)

**Why fascinating:** Real-time language creation; cohort effects; nativization by children; implications for innateness debates.

**Resources:** Ann Senghas's work, Susan Goldin-Meadow's research on homesign systems.

**Danger:** Can dominate discussion; students may overgeneralize from one case. Keep it as one example among many.

### 2. Neural organization of sign languages
**When to introduce:** Lesson 26 (future directions)

**Why fascinating:** Same left-hemisphere dominance as spoken languages; modality-independent language areas; right-hemisphere contributions for spatial processing.

**Resources:** Karen Emmorey, David Corina, and Aaron Newman's neurolinguistic work.

**Danger:** Requires neuroscience background; can distract from linguistic analysis. Keep it at a high level unless student has relevant background.

### 3. Gestural origins of language
**When to introduce:** Lesson 3 (language evolution)

**Why fascinating:** Sign languages as potential model for protolanguage; gesture-to-sign transitions; multimodal communication.

**Resources:** Michael Arbib, Susan Goldin-Meadow on co-speech gesture.

**Danger:** Highly speculative; can lead to unproductive "just-so" stories. Ground in empirical data on contemporary systems.

### 4. The iconicity debate
**When to introduce:** Throughout, especially Lessons 13, 16, 20

**Why fascinating:** Challenges Saussurean arbitrariness; implications for language-thought interface; productivity vs. conventionalization.

**Resources:** Taub's "Iconicity in ASL", Perniss et al. on iconicity cross-linguistically.

**Danger:** Can become circular; students may revert to "iconicity explains everything" or dismiss it entirely. Maintain nuance.

### 5. Classifier/depicting sign controversy
**When to introduce:** Lesson 13

**Why fascinating:** Active debate among top researchers; touches on fundamental questions about linguistic structure, gradience, categoricality.

**Resources:** Emmorey (pro-classifier), Liddell (pro-gesture blending), Schembri (depicting signs).

**Danger:** Can bog down in terminological disputes. Focus on the empirical phenomena and theoretical stakes, not just labels.

### 6. Code-blending in bimodal bilinguals
**When to introduce:** Lesson 25

**Why fascinating:** Simultaneous production of sign and speech; challenges language separation models; unique to bimodal bilingualism.

**Resources:** Emmorey's work on bimodal bilingualism, Petitto on language separation.

**Danger:** Requires understanding of spoken language prosody and bilingualism theory. Can be complex for students without that background.

### 7. Sign language technology and AI
**When to introduce:** Lesson 26

**Why fascinating:** Automated recognition and translation; avatar signing; accessibility applications; computational challenges (3D space, non-manual signals).

**Resources:** SignAll, Sign Language Translation systems, recent NLP workshops.

**Danger:** Can be overly optimistic about technological solutions; may underestimate linguistic complexity. Emphasize ongoing challenges.

### 8. Home sign systems and language deprivation
**When to introduce:** After Lesson 3 (emergence)

**Why fascinating:** Linguistic capacity in the absence of input; critical period effects; ethical implications of language deprivation.

**Resources:** Goldin-Meadow on home sign, research on late L1 learners.

**Danger:** Deeply sensitive topic involving language rights and access; requires careful, respectful framing. Don't treat as just a scientific curiosity.

## Difficulty Progression

### Arc 1: Foundations (Lessons 1-5)
**Difficulty:** Low to moderate (1-3)
- Build confidence with accessible concepts
- Establish shared terminology
- Motivate the field with interesting questions

### Arc 2: Phonology (Lessons 6-11)
**Difficulty:** Moderate to high (2-4)
- Most formal analysis so far
- Requires abstract thinking about visual-gestural units
- Peak difficulty at Lessons 9-10 (non-manuals, syllables)
- Review at Lesson 11 consolidates

### Arc 3: Morphology (Lessons 12-17)
**Difficulty:** Moderate to high (2-4)
- Complexity from simultaneity
- Classifier debate is conceptually challenging (Lesson 13)
- Iconicity thread is subtle (Lesson 16)
- Review at Lesson 17 consolidates

### Arc 4: Syntax & Discourse (Lessons 18-23)
**Difficulty:** Moderate to high (3-4)
- Most linguistically complex material
- Role shift (Lesson 19) is a peak
- Clause structure (Lesson 21) is peak
- Review at Lesson 23 consolidates

### Arc 5: Variation & Beyond (Lessons 24-26)
**Difficulty:** Moderate (2-3)
- Easier after syntax
- More descriptive, less formal
- Ends on forward-looking note

### Review Cadence
- Lesson 11 (after phonology)
- Lesson 17 (after morphology)
- Lesson 23 (after syntax)

These are strategically placed after the most cognitively demanding material, giving students time to consolidate before moving to the next domain.
