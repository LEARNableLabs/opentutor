# Bonsai — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Vascular system (xylem/phloem)** — the plumbing that moves water and nutrients through the tree
2. **Transpiration pull** — the engine that drives water upward. Depends on: vascular system
3. **Root respiration** — why roots need oxygen, not just water. Depends on: vascular system
4. **Container constraints** — how pots change root behavior vs. natural soil
5. **Mycorrhizal fungi** — symbiotic partners that extend root function. Depends on: root respiration, container constraints
6. **Apical dominance** — why the top controls the bottom. Depends on: vascular system
7. **Auxin distribution** — hormonal control of branching patterns. Depends on: apical dominance
8. **Photosynthesis under constraint** — balancing leaf reduction with energy needs. Depends on: vascular system
9. **Carbohydrate storage** — how trees bank energy for growth and recovery. Depends on: photosynthesis
10. **Dormancy cycles** — seasonal shutdown in temperate species. Depends on: carbohydrate storage
11. **Chilling requirements** — why some trees need winter. Depends on: dormancy cycles
12. **Soil particle size** — controls water, air, and root penetration. Depends on: root respiration
13. **Water retention vs. drainage** — the critical balance in container culture. Depends on: soil particle size, container constraints
14. **Osmotic pressure** — the physics of when trees can't drink. Depends on: vascular system, water retention
15. **Field capacity and wilting point** — the water availability spectrum. Depends on: osmotic pressure, soil particle size
16. **NPK ratios** — matching fertilizer to growth phase. Depends on: photosynthesis, carbohydrate storage
17. **Micronutrients** — the trace elements that enable enzyme function. Depends on: NPK ratios
18. **Root pruning physiology** — triggering fine root development through controlled damage. Depends on: root respiration, auxin distribution
19. **Root-shoot balance** — the feedback loop between top and bottom. Depends on: root pruning physiology, carbohydrate storage
20. **Wabi-sabi** — finding beauty in imperfection and age
21. **Classical bonsai styles** — formal categories based on trunk angle and movement. Depends on: wabi-sabi
22. **Visual balance** — asymmetric composition that feels stable. Depends on: classical styles
23. **Negative space (yohaku)** — the power of what's not there. Depends on: visual balance
24. **Seasonal display** — designing for transformation across the year. Depends on: dormancy cycles, classical styles
25. **Pruning theory** — manipulating apical dominance for design. Depends on: apical dominance, auxin distribution
26. **Structural vs. maintenance pruning** — building form vs. refining detail. Depends on: pruning theory, visual balance
27. **Wiring mechanics** — bending wood without breaking cambium. Depends on: vascular system, structural pruning
28. **Repotting technique** — coordinating root work with seasonal phenology. Depends on: root pruning physiology, dormancy cycles
29. **Branch selection** — choosing which growth to keep for visual hierarchy. Depends on: visual balance, structural pruning
30. **Jin and shari (deadwood)** — creating aged character through controlled decay. Depends on: wabi-sabi, branch selection
31. **Air layering** — propagation by forcing roots on branches. Depends on: vascular system, auxin distribution
32. **Species-specific phenology** — timing techniques to match each tree's calendar. Depends on: dormancy cycles, repotting technique, pruning theory
33. **Climate matching** — selecting species for sustainable culture. Depends on: chilling requirements, species phenology
34. **Seasonal care integration** — assembling a coherent year-long plan. Depends on: all technical concepts, seasonal display

## Dependencies

### Critical Foundation Sequences

**Vascular system → All physiology concepts**  
Understanding how water and nutrients move is the foundation for every horticultural decision. Without this, pruning, watering, and fertilizing are just superstition.

**Apical dominance → Pruning → Design**  
You can't effectively design a tree until you understand how cutting one branch affects all the others. Hormonal control (auxin) explains why "cutting makes things grow" — which seems paradoxical without the mechanism.

**Root respiration → Soil science → Water management**  
Container culture fails when students think soil is just "dirt." Particle size controls air availability, which controls root function, which controls the entire tree. This chain must be built in order.

**Wabi-sabi → Aesthetic principles → Technique application**  
Japanese aesthetic concepts are abstract until grounded in observing real trees. Students need the philosophical frame before they can evaluate specific design choices. Then techniques (jin, branch selection) make sense as tools for aesthetic goals.

**Photosynthesis/carbohydrate storage → Dormancy → Seasonal timing**  
Timing is everything in bonsai, but "do X in spring" is meaningless without understanding why. The energy budget (photosynthesis, storage, dormancy) explains when a tree can afford to lose roots, leaves, or branches.

### Common Bottlenecks

**Root-shoot balance** is where many intermediate students get stuck. It requires integrating root physiology, hormonal control, carbohydrate storage, and pruning theory. It's the pivot between "care" and "design."

**Negative space (yohaku)** is conceptually difficult for Western students trained to focus on objects, not emptiness. It requires visual retraining and benefits from comparison exercises with masterwork examples.

**Repotting technique** combines root physiology, seasonal phenology, soil science, and aftercare protocols. It's the highest-stakes technique (easy to kill a tree) and requires confidence in multiple domains. Students should not attempt it until lessons 10-12 are solid.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic plant biology** — students should know photosynthesis, roots/stems/leaves, and sexual reproduction at a high school level
- **Tree anatomy** — understanding hardwood vs. softwood, cambium, bark, sapwood/heartwood, annual rings
- **Container gardening basics** — experience with potting mix, watering, and basic fertilization (even with houseplants)

## Misconceptions to Anticipate

1. **"Bonsai are starved or tortured trees"** — students arrive thinking miniaturization = deprivation. Lesson 4 (photosynthesis constraints) directly addresses this: bonsai are healthy, well-fed trees managed for compact growth.

2. **"Bonsai soil is a type of dirt"** — many think you can dig up garden soil. Lesson 7 reframes soil as an engineered matrix of particle sizes designed for container culture.

3. **"More water = healthier tree"** — overwatering kills more bonsai than underwatering. Lessons 2 and 8 build the physiology to understand why.

4. **"Pruning wounds the tree"** — students fear cutting. Lessons 3 and 19 reframe pruning as a communication tool (hormonal signaling), not damage.

5. **"All techniques work year-round"** — beginners want to practice immediately. Lessons 5, 10, 24, and 26 emphasize phenological timing as non-negotiable.

6. **"Bonsai styles are arbitrary rules"** — styles feel like memorization until grounded in natural tree forms (lesson 14) and visual balance principles (lesson 15).

7. **"Pines are good beginner trees because they look like bonsai"** — lesson 25 directly counters this. Pines are slow, unforgiving, and require advanced timing. Start with deciduous.
