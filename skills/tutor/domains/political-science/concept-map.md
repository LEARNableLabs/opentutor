# Political Science — Institutions and Voting Theory
## Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Institutional variety** — democracies structure power differently (presidential, parliamentary, federal, unitary)
2. **Separation of powers** — dividing authority among branches to prevent concentration
3. **Presidential systems** — elected executive independent of legislature, fixed terms. Depends on: 1, 2
4. **Parliamentary systems** — executive chosen by and accountable to legislature. Depends on: 1, 2
5. **Federalism** — vertical division of power between national and subnational governments. Depends on: 1, 2
6. **Judicial review** — courts checking legislative/executive actions against constitution. Depends on: 2
7. **Veto players** — actors whose agreement is needed for policy change. Depends on: 2, 3, 4
8. **Electoral formulas** — rules for translating votes into seats (plurality, majority, PR)
9. **Duverger's Law** — single-member districts tend toward two-party systems. Depends on: 8
10. **Proportional representation** — seats allocated in proportion to vote shares. Depends on: 8
11. **Ranked-choice voting** — voters order candidates; votes transfer if first choice eliminated. Depends on: 8
12. **Mixed electoral systems** — combining district and PR seats. Depends on: 8, 10
13. **Electoral manipulation** — gerrymandering, malapportionment, strategic districting. Depends on: 8
14. **Preference orderings** — voters' ranked preferences over alternatives
15. **Condorcet paradox** — cyclical collective preferences from transitive individual preferences. Depends on: 14
16. **Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)** — adding/removing non-winning options shouldn't change winner. Depends on: 14
17. **Fairness criteria** — properties a voting system might satisfy (Condorcet, monotonicity, IIA). Depends on: 14, 15, 16
18. **Arrow's impossibility theorem** — no ranked voting system satisfies all fairness criteria without dictatorship. Depends on: 15, 16, 17
19. **Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem** — all non-dictatorial voting systems are manipulable. Depends on: 17, 18
20. **Strategic voting** — voting insincerely to achieve better outcomes. Depends on: 14, 19
21. **Median voter theorem** — candidates converge to median voter's position under certain conditions. Depends on: 20
22. **Agenda-setting** — power to control which alternatives are voted on. Depends on: 14
23. **Coalition formation** — parties combining to form majority governments. Depends on: 4, 10
24. **Voting power indices** — measuring influence beyond seat counts (Shapley-Shubik, Banzhaf). Depends on: 23
25. **Democracy indices** — quantitative measures of democratic quality and institutional performance
26. **Path dependence** — historical choices constraining current institutional options. Depends on: 7
27. **Institutional reform** — changing rules and structures to improve governance. Depends on: 1-26

## Dependencies

### Foundational Dependencies
- **Presidential vs. parliamentary systems** both depend on understanding separation of powers — they're different solutions to organizing executive-legislative relations
- **Veto players** makes sense only after understanding institutional structures (presidential, parliamentary, federal) that create them
- **Electoral formulas** are foundational to everything in voting theory — must come before analyzing their strategic effects

### Critical Paths
- **Arrow's theorem path**: Preference orderings → voting paradoxes (Condorcet) → fairness criteria → impossibility result. Each step builds directly on the previous.
- **Strategic behavior path**: Electoral formulas → Duverger's Law → strategic voting → median voter theorem. Understanding how rules shape strategy requires this sequence.
- **Social choice path**: Preference orderings → IIA violations → fairness criteria → Arrow's theorem → Gibbard-Satterthwaite. The impossibility results cascade.

### Bottlenecks
- **Preference orderings (concept 14)** — bottleneck for all social choice theory. Students must be comfortable with abstract preference notation before voting paradoxes make sense.
- **Electoral formulas (concept 8)** — bottleneck for understanding electoral systems. Without clarity on plurality vs. PR, everything else is confusing.
- **Arrow's theorem (concept 18)** — conceptual peak. Requires synthesis of paradoxes, fairness criteria, and proof logic. Students often struggle here.

### Enabling Clusters
- **Institutional foundations (1-7)** enable comparative analysis. Understanding these structures lets students analyze real-world systems.
- **Voting paradoxes (15-16)** enable impossibility theorems. Condorcet paradox and IIA violations motivate Arrow's theorem.
- **Strategic models (20-24)** enable predictions about political behavior. Game theory meets institutions.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic probability and logic** — needed for understanding voting paradoxes, strategic calculations, and proof structures
- **Democratic government basics** — needed for context on institutions (what legislatures/executives/courts do)
- **Abstract reasoning** — needed for social choice theory, especially impossibility theorems and preference orderings

## Common Misconceptions to Watch For

1. **"More veto players = more democracy"** — students often think checks and balances are always good, missing the policy gridlock tradeoff
2. **"Proportional representation is always fairer"** — missing trade-offs with accountability, stability, and coalition formation
3. **"Arrow's theorem means democracy is impossible"** — conflating "perfect aggregation" with "workable democracy"
4. **"Strategic voting is cheating"** — confusing strategic behavior with electoral fraud
5. **"Parliamentary systems have no separation of powers"** — missing that fusion of executive/legislative doesn't eliminate checks
6. **"The Condorcet paradox shows voters are irrational"** — paradox arises from aggregation, not individual irrationality
7. **"There exists a 'best' electoral system"** — missing that all systems involve trade-offs (Arrow's theorem applies!)

## Conceptual Peaks

- **Lesson 18: Arrow's impossibility theorem** — the hardest conceptual climb. Requires synthesis of multiple fairness criteria and proof logic.
- **Lesson 20: Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem** — another peak, building on Arrow but adding strategic manipulation.
- **Lesson 23: Agenda-setting power** — subtle and counterintuitive; students struggle to see how proposal power differs from voting power.

## Review Integration

- **Lesson 12** reviews electoral systems (concepts 8-13) before jumping to social choice theory
- **Lesson 19** reviews impossibility results and paradoxes (concepts 14-19) before moving to strategic behavior
- Both reviews are timed to consolidate before conceptual shifts to new modules
