# HEMA Research Summary

## Overview

Historical European Martial Arts (HEMA) is the study and practice of combat systems documented in European fight books and treatises from roughly 1300-1800. At the intermediate level, students move beyond basic technique drilling to engage with primary sources, understand pedagogical systems of historical masters, and develop interpretive skills.

## Major Subtopics

### 1. **Source Study & Interpretation**
- German longsword tradition (Liechtenauer, Meyer, Ringeck)
- Italian rapier and sidesword (Capo Ferro, Fabris, Marozzo)
- German wrestling and dagger (Ott, Auerswald)
- English systems (Silver, Swetnam)
- Manuscript interpretation methodology

### 2. **Advanced Technical Concepts**
- Timing systems (Indes, Vor/Nach, tempo)
- Measure and footwork theory
- Blade mechanics (leverage, binding, winding)
- Cross-weapon principles
- Historical pedagogy vs modern sport

### 3. **Historical Context**
- Social role of martial arts in medieval/Renaissance Europe
- Evolution of weapons technology
- Master-student transmission models
- Civilian vs military applications
- Guild systems and Fechtschulen

### 4. **Modern Practice**
- Tournament formats (cutting, sparring, forms)
- Safety protocols and equipment
- Instructor development
- Community organizations (HEMA Alliance, national federations)
- Research vs sport balance

## Key Sources Found

### Primary Historical Treatises
- **Wiktenauer** (wiktenauer.com) — largest open-source collection of digitized fight books with translations
- **Joachim Meyer's "Art of Combat" (1570)** — comprehensive German system
- **Fiore dei Liberi's "Fior di Battaglia" (1409)** — Italian master's complete system
- **MS I.33** — oldest surviving European fight book (sword and buckler, c. 1300)
- **Johannes Liechtenauer tradition** — foundational German longsword system

### Modern Educational Resources
- **YouTube: Scholagladiatoria** (Matt Easton) — source analysis, technique breakdowns, historical context
- **YouTube: Blood and Iron HEMA** — technical instruction, sparring analysis
- **YouTube: Skallagrim** — equipment reviews, technique demonstrations
- **YouTube: Akademia Szermierzy** — Polish school's technical studies
- **HEMA Alliance** (hemaalliance.com) — club finder, event calendar, standards
- **Historical Fencing** podcast by Matt Galas

### Books and Modern Interpretations
- **"Medieval Swordsmanship" by John Clements** — foundational text for many HEMA practitioners
- **"The Art of Longsword Fighting" by Christian Tobler** — Liechtenauer interpretation
- **"Joachim Meyer Frei Fechter" by Jeffrey Forgeng (translation)** — scholarly edition
- **"The Secret History of the Sword" by J. Christoph Amberger** — rapier fencing history
- **"The Swordsman's Companion" by Guy Windsor** — practical Fiore training manual

### Interactive Tools & Communities
- **WMA forums and Reddit r/wma** — active discussion, technique debates
- **HEMA Scorecard** — tournament results database
- **Purpleheart Armory, SPES, Blackfencer** — equipment suppliers with technical guides

### Key Researchers and Practitioners
- **Matt Easton** — British instructor, historian, YouTuber
- **Christian Tobler** — Liechtenauer scholar and instructor
- **Guy Windsor** — Fiore specialist
- **Jessica Finley** — tournament competitor and instructor
- **Keith Farrell** — instructor and author
- **Jake Norwood** — founder of Longpoint tournament
- **Axel Pettersson** — Swedish instructor and researcher

## Available Educational Formats

- **Video instruction** — abundant on YouTube, technique breakdowns and sparring
- **Written treatises** — originals digitized on Wiktenauer with translations
- **In-person workshops** — major events (Longpoint, Swordfish, HEMAC)
- **Online courses** — some instructors offer structured video curricula
- **Tournament footage** — sparring and cutting competitions for analysis
- **Academic papers** — growing body of historical research on martial culture

## Gaps Identified

- Limited university-level formal courses (mostly club-based)
- Uneven translation quality for some treatises
- Ongoing debates about interpretation methodology
- Need for more biomechanical analysis
- Limited archaeology of technique (vs weapons/armor)
