# History of Photography — Teaching Notes

## Approach

History of photography is uniquely positioned between art history, technical history, and cultural history. At intermediate level, we emphasize **seeing connections** — how technical innovations enabled new aesthetics, how social changes drove photographic applications, how debates about photography's nature persist across eras. The pedagogy is **image-centered**: every lesson should involve looking closely at specific photographs, not just reading about movements. Photography history is also **contested territory** — encourage critical thinking about truth claims, authorship, and photography's changing roles rather than presenting a single narrative.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Photography's invention happened in a single moment (1839)"** — Students often think Daguerre "invented photography," ignoring decades of experiments (Niépce, Wedgwood, Talbot) and parallel development of multiple processes. **Correction**: Frame invention as convergence of chemistry, optics, and cultural desire for mechanical imaging; emphasize competing processes.

2. **"Documentary photography shows objective reality"** — The medium's indexical nature (light actually touched the subject) creates false sense of neutrality. **Correction**: Analyze how framing, moment selection, cropping, captioning, and context all shape meaning. Use FSA examples where same subject photographed differently tells different story.

3. **"Early photographers were just technicians; artistic photography came later"** — Students underestimate aesthetic sophistication of 19th century work. **Correction**: Show how Julia Margaret Cameron, Roger Fenton, and others made deliberate artistic choices within technical constraints; discuss how technical mastery was creative problem-solving.

4. **"Modernists rejected all manipulation; postmodernists embraced it"** — Oversimplifies complex relationships to darkroom work. **Correction**: Show how Ansel Adams extensively manipulated prints through dodging/burning (Zone System), while some postmodernists use straight photography. Distinguish between in-camera, darkroom, and digital manipulation.

5. **"Digital photography killed film photography"** — Assumes complete replacement rather than coexistence. **Correction**: Discuss how film persists for aesthetic reasons, how hybrid workflows combine analog and digital, how computational photography changes what "taking a picture" means.

6. **"Color photography wasn't used until recently"** — Students often don't realize Autochrome (1907), Kodachrome (1935) existed. **Correction**: Distinguish between technical availability and institutional acceptance; explain why black-and-white dominated fine art until 1970s despite color's earlier existence.

7. **"Photojournalism is neutral reporting"** — Ignores editorial choices, 'the decisive moment' as construction, and power of captioning. **Correction**: Analyze how same image can support different narratives depending on context. Discuss ethics of intervention, staging, digital manipulation in news.

8. **"Photography democratized image-making immediately"** — Overlooks how early photography required significant resources and knowledge. **Correction**: Show how democratization happened in waves (dry plates 1870s-80s, Kodak 1888, Polaroid 1947, digital 1990s, smartphones 2000s), each expanding access.

9. **"Abstract photography has no subject"** — Students struggle with New Vision and modernist experiments. **Correction**: Explain how cropping, perspective, and lighting can transform recognizable subjects into formal studies; distinguish abstraction from abstracted.

10. **"Appropriation art is just copying"** — Misses how recontextualization creates new meaning. **Correction**: Use Richard Prince's rephotography, Sherrie Levine's "After Walker Evans" to discuss how identical images mean differently in different contexts; connect to questions about authorship and originality.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students

**Emphasize**:
- Connections between technical and aesthetic developments
- Close visual analysis of specific photographs
- Recurring debates (art vs. document, objectivity vs. subjectivity, access vs. expertise)
- How photography both reflects and shapes culture
- Primary sources (photographers' writings, manifestos, criticism)

**Depth of engagement**:
- Can handle some theory (Sontag, Barthes, Benjamin) but keep excerpts focused and contextual
- Should learn to identify movements by visual characteristics, not just memorize names
- Analyze photographs for formal choices and historical context
- Understand technical processes conceptually (don't need to demonstrate them)

**De-emphasize**:
- Exhaustive technical details of chemical processes (focus on capabilities enabled)
- Comprehensive chronology (focus on key moments and shifts)
- Market/collecting/auction dynamics (unless student interest)
- Highly specialized contemporary theory debates

### Compared to Other Levels

**vs. Beginner**: More emphasis on contested narratives rather than single story; deeper engagement with primary sources; stronger connections to broader art history and cultural history; more challenging theoretical concepts.

**vs. Advanced**: Less emphasis on theoretical frameworks (phenomenology, semiotics, psychoanalysis); less archival research; fewer deep dives into single photographer's complete oeuvre; less attention to overlooked figures or alternative histories.

## Rabbit Holes

### When to Drop Them In

1. **Photography and colonialism** — How photography documented empire-building and shaped Western views of "exotic" places. Drop in around Lesson 8 (documentary emergence) or when discussing travel photography. Resources: Edward Said's work, studies of expeditionary photography.

2. **Women photographers' erasure and recovery** — How women like Anna Atkins, Berenice Abbott, Dorothea Lange were marginalized in histories then rediscovered. Drop in organically when discussing any movement; challenge "great men" narrative throughout.

3. **Camera as weapon / camera as shield** — Susan Sontag's ideas about photography and violence, voyeurism, distancing. Drop in around Lesson 18 (documentary ethics) or when discussing war photography.

4. **Surveillance and facial recognition** — How photographic technologies serve state control (mug shots, passports, surveillance cameras, facial recognition AI). Drop in Lesson 28-29 (digital/computational) as contemporary extension.

5. **Vernacular photography** — Snapshots, photo albums, family pictures as alternative history. Drop in when discussing democratization (Lesson 9) or challenging art/non-art boundaries.

6. **Photography without a camera** — Photograms, chemigrams, scanner photography, AI-generated images. Drop in when discussing experimental techniques (Lesson 15, New Vision) or computational photography (Lesson 28).

7. **Copyright and authorship battles** — From Burrow-Giles v. Sarony (1884) establishing photography as copyrightable to contemporary Instagram disputes. Drop in when discussing appropriation (Lesson 25) or social media (Lesson 29).

8. **Daguerreotype as mirror** — How daguerreotypes' reflective surface created unique viewing experience; metaphor for photography's reflexivity. Drop in Lesson 3 to deepen understanding of medium specificity.

## Difficulty Progression

### Arc Overview
- **Lessons 1-7**: Build foundation (difficulty 1-3) — technical processes, invention contexts, basic movements
- **Lessons 8-13**: First complexity peak (difficulty 3-4) — documentary emergence, Pictorialism, art/document debates
- **Lesson 14**: Review and consolidation (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 15-20**: Second complexity peak (difficulty 3-4) — modernist experiments, documentary ethics, photojournalism
- **Lesson 21**: Review (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 22-25**: Third complexity peak (difficulty 3-4) — color acceptance, postmodern challenges
- **Lesson 26**: Review (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 27-29**: Synthesis and contemporary (difficulty 3-4) — digital transformations, computational futures

### Difficulty Factors

**Lower difficulty** (1-2):
- Single clear concept per lesson
- Direct visual examples
- Concrete technical or biographical information
- Review and consolidation lessons

**Higher difficulty** (3-4):
- Abstract concepts (objectivity, authorship, conceptualism)
- Contested interpretations or values
- Multiple simultaneous developments
- Theoretical frameworks
- Ethical dilemmas without clear answers

### Adjustment Triggers

**If student struggles**:
- Add more visual comparison exercises (same subject, different approaches)
- Provide historical context timelines
- Break theoretical concepts into concrete examples
- Extend review intervals
- Focus on fewer photographers in greater depth

**If student excels**:
- Introduce primary theoretical texts (Benjamin's "Work of Art," Barthes' "Camera Lucida")
- Assign comparative analysis across movements
- Explore overlooked photographers or alternative histories
- Add critical writing exercises
- Connect to contemporary photographic practice
