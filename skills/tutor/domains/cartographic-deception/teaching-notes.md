# Cartographic Deception — Teaching Notes

## Approach

This topic is **visual, critical, and interdisciplinary** — it combines geometry, history, semiotics, and political analysis. The most effective teaching strategy is to **show, don't tell**: present students with actual propaganda maps and guide them to discover the manipulation themselves before providing the theoretical framework. Use interactive tools (The True Size, XKCD comparisons) to make abstract distortion concrete, then transition to historical examples where distortion becomes deliberate deception.

At the intermediate level, students should move beyond "maps can be wrong" to **"all maps encode choices, and those choices serve interests."** This requires cultivating skepticism without cynicism — maps are useful, necessary tools that are also inherently rhetorical. The goal is critical literacy: students should finish able to decode visual arguments in cartography while still appreciating good map design.

Pedagogy should alternate between **technical analysis** (how projections work, what gets distorted) and **political analysis** (who benefits, what's being hidden). Don't let it become purely abstract geometry or purely political critique — the power of this topic is in their intersection.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Mercator projection was designed for propaganda"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Because Mercator's area distortion benefits colonial powers, students assume it was intentional political manipulation.
   - **How to correct it**: Explain Mercator's original purpose (navigation, preserving angles for sailing) and emphasize that *consequences* and *continued use* can be political even when original intent wasn't. The propaganda isn't in Mercator's 1569 design — it's in choosing Mercator for classroom walls in the 20th century.

2. **"There's a 'correct' or 'unbiased' projection"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: They want a solution to the problem. If Mercator is biased, there must be an unbiased alternative.
   - **How to correct it**: Emphasize the mathematical impossibility: **every projection involves trade-offs**. The Peters projection trades shape accuracy for area equality — that's a different set of values, not neutrality. The question is always "what matters more for this map's purpose?" not "which projection is objectively right?"

3. **"Digital maps (Google/Apple) are neutral because they're algorithmic"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Technology feels objective. Algorithms seem to remove human bias.
   - **How to correct it**: Show that **algorithm design encodes choices** (what appears at which zoom level, which landmarks are labeled, how borders are drawn for disputed territories). Engineers choose what data to prioritize, what to omit, how to visualize. Automation scales decisions; it doesn't eliminate them.

4. **"Propaganda maps are easy to spot because they're obviously manipulated"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: They expect propaganda to be crude, blatant, cartoonish (like wartime posters).
   - **How to correct it**: Show subtle examples: election maps that use area instead of population, COVID maps that use linear color scales for exponential data, geopolitical maps that center "us" and marginalize "them." **The most effective propaganda looks informational, not persuasive.**

5. **"Counter-mapping is 'objective' mapping"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: If dominant maps are biased, then resistance maps must be revealing the truth.
   - **How to correct it**: Counter-maps are also rhetorical — they serve different political goals (indigenous rights, environmental justice), not no political goals. The difference is **whose perspective** is centered, not whether perspective exists. All maps are arguments; counter-maps argue back.

6. **"Color is just aesthetic preference"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: They don't realize how deeply color psychology shapes interpretation unconsciously.
   - **How to correct it**: Show the same data visualized in different color schemes and ask what story each tells. Red/green, light/dark, warm/cool — each pairing carries emotional and cultural weight. **Color is content, not decoration.**

7. **"Omission is usually accidental (space constraints, data gaps)"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: They assume mapmakers are trying to be complete and fall short, not that exclusion is strategic.
   - **How to correct it**: Analyze colonial maps that show "empty" lands where indigenous communities lived, or modern maps that omit Palestine, or climate maps that exclude small island nations. **What's missing often reveals the map's purpose more than what's included.**

8. **"Projections only matter for world maps"**
   - **Why students get this wrong**: Local/regional maps don't look obviously distorted.
   - **How to correct it**: Show how even local maps involve choices (north-up orientation, scale, what's in/out of frame). Distortion is less about geometric warping and more about **what the frame includes and excludes**. A city map that omits certain neighborhoods is still practicing cartographic deception.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner
- Focus on obvious examples (Mercator size distortion, red/green color manipulation)
- Stick to technical explanations (math of projections, mechanics of visual perception)
- Less emphasis on historical complexity; more on clear before/after comparisons
- Avoid deep semiotics; keep symbol analysis concrete

### Intermediate (this curriculum)
- Introduce historical context (colonialism, Cold War) and connect it to technical choices
- Teach rhetorical analysis — students should be able to deconstruct a propaganda map's strategies
- Balance technical and political: understand **how** maps distort and **why** those distortions serve interests
- Engage with counter-mapping and critical cartography as alternatives, not just critiques

### Advanced
- Deep dive into critical cartography theory (Harley, Wood, Crampton)
- Analyze power/knowledge frameworks (Foucault applied to mapmaking)
- Explore GIS and geospatial algorithms as sites of bias
- Compare cartographic deception across cultures (not just Western propaganda)
- Students create their own counter-maps or propaganda map pastiches

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### 1. "The Map Is Not the Territory" (Korzybski)
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 3-4 (after establishing projection basics)
- **Why it's compelling**: Connects cartography to broader epistemology — maps are models, and all models simplify. The question is which simplifications serve which purposes.

### 2. Borges' "On Exactitude in Science" (1-to-1 scale map)
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 4 or 6 (when discussing trade-offs)
- **Why it's compelling**: Absurdist parable that makes the point about utility vs. accuracy. A perfect map is useless; all useful maps lie selectively.

### 3. Terra nullius and the cartographic erasure of indigenous peoples
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 16-17 (omission and erasure techniques)
- **Why it's compelling**: Shows how "empty" maps justified legal doctrines of conquest. Cartographic omission had material consequences (dispossession, genocide).

### 4. The Mercator-Peters controversy as culture war
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 8 (Peters projection)
- **Why it's compelling**: The 1980s-90s fight over classroom wall maps became a proxy battle for Eurocentrism, political correctness, and disciplinary authority (cartographers vs. activists). Shows how technical debates become ideological.

### 5. GPS and the shift from projection to raster tiles
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 23 (digital mapping)
- **Why it's compelling**: Explains why Web Mercator dominates — it's computationally efficient for tiling, not because it's accurate. **Algorithmic convenience perpetuates colonial bias.**

### 6. Disputed borders and "truth" in digital maps
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 24 (satellite imagery)
- **Why it's compelling**: Google/Apple show different maps to users in different countries (Kashmir, Crimea, South China Sea). There's no single authoritative digital map — it's geopolitically localized. Reveals that even "objective" satellite views are filtered through political realities.

### 7. Heatmaps and the surveillance cartography of daily life
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 26 (algorithmic bias)
- **Why it's compelling**: Fitness tracker heatmaps revealed secret military bases; phone location data tracks protests. Modern cartography isn't just representing space — it's surveilling bodies. Deception shifts from what's shown to what's collected.

### 8. Upside-down maps (Turnabout Map, McArthur's Universal Corrective Map)
- **When to drop this in**: Lesson 3 (changing map center)
- **Why it's compelling**: Flipping the map north-south disorients students and makes them realize how arbitrary "north = up" is. Immediate, visceral lesson in perspective.

## Difficulty Progression

### Lessons 1-6 (Foundations): Difficulty 1-3
Start gently with the **geometry of projection** — students need to understand the technical problem before they can see the political dimension. Lessons 1-2 are concrete and interactive (play with The True Size). Lessons 3-5 introduce interpretation and bias, building to the first review.

### Lessons 7-11 (Projection Politics): Difficulty 3-4
Ramp up to the **politics of projection choice**. Lesson 8 (Peters controversy) is a peak — it synthesizes technical understanding with ideological conflict. Students need to argue multiple perspectives. Lesson 11 (Greenwich meridian) extends the critique beyond projection to the entire coordinate system.

### Lessons 12-17 (Visual Rhetoric): Difficulty 2-4
Oscillate between accessible (**color psychology** in Lesson 12) and challenging (**data manipulation** in Lesson 14, **omission** in Lesson 16). Lesson 13 review lets students consolidate before the harder analytical work of deconstructing visual arguments.

### Lessons 18-22 (Historical Propaganda): Difficulty 3-4
Sustained high difficulty because students are **applying** everything they've learned to specific historical cases. Lesson 18 (Nazi maps) and Lesson 22 (rhetorical analysis teach-back) are peaks. Lesson 20 review is critical — students need to synthesize geometric, visual, and political analysis before moving to digital.

### Lessons 23-26 (Digital Deception): Difficulty 3-4
Maintain complexity but shift to **contemporary applications**. Lesson 24 (deepfake geography) is a conceptual peak (can we trust satellite imagery?). Lesson 25 (counter-mapping) offers a hopeful turn. Lesson 26 wraps with algorithmic bias — the future of cartographic deception.

### Overall Arc
The curriculum builds from **concrete** (distortion you can see) to **abstract** (ideology you must infer) to **applied** (analyzing real propaganda) to **contemporary** (recognizing ongoing deception). Reviews at lessons 6, 13, 20 give students breathing room before adding new layers of complexity.
