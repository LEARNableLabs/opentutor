# Cartographic Deception — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Projection problem** — the mathematical impossibility of representing a sphere on a flat surface without distortion
2. **Geometric distortion** — how different projections distort area, shape, distance, or direction. Depends on: projection problem
3. **Mercator projection** — a conformal projection that preserves angles but distorts area, especially at high latitudes. Depends on: projection problem, geometric distortion
4. **Area distortion** — the exaggeration or compression of relative sizes in map projections. Depends on: geometric distortion
5. **Perceptual bias** — how visual distortions shape mental models of geography and geopolitics. Depends on: area distortion
6. **Eurocentrism** — the centering of Europe in standard world maps, reflecting colonial power structures. Depends on: Mercator projection, perceptual bias
7. **Trade-offs** — how every projection preserves some properties at the expense of others. Depends on: geometric distortion
8. **Political choices** — how selecting a projection, center, or orientation encodes values and power. Depends on: trade-offs, eurocentrism
9. **Colonial power** — how European empires used maps to legitimize expansion and dominance. Depends on: Mercator projection, political choices
10. **Projection controversy** — debates over "fair" vs "accurate" projections (Peters vs Mercator). Depends on: area distortion, political choices
11. **Web Mercator** — the projection used by digital mapping platforms, inheriting Mercator's biases. Depends on: Mercator projection
12. **Prime meridian** — the arbitrary choice of Greenwich as 0° longitude, a colonial legacy. Depends on: colonial power
13. **Color psychology** — how color choices (red = danger, green = safe) manipulate emotional response. Depends on: perceptual bias
14. **Cartographic rhetoric** — the use of visual elements to persuade, not just inform. Depends on: color psychology, political choices
15. **Data manipulation** — how the same data can be visualized to tell opposing stories. Depends on: cartographic rhetoric
16. **Choropleth bias** — how area-based data visualization misleads when regions vary in size. Depends on: area distortion, data manipulation
17. **Omission** — the strategic exclusion of information to guide interpretation. Depends on: cartographic rhetoric
18. **Selective labeling** — choosing what to name (and what to leave unnamed) on a map. Depends on: omission
19. **Erasure techniques** — how maps make people, borders, or claims disappear. Depends on: omission, colonial power
20. **Semiotic power** — how symbols carry cultural and political meaning beyond their literal form. Depends on: cartographic rhetoric
21. **Lebensraum** — Nazi ideology of "living space" visualized through expansionist maps. Depends on: political choices, cartographic rhetoric
22. **Territorial claims** — using maps to assert ownership or legitimacy over disputed regions. Depends on: political choices, lebensraum
23. **Encirclement narrative** — Cold War maps depicting the USSR or USA as surrounded by threats. Depends on: cartographic rhetoric, territorial claims
24. **Domino theory** — visual representation of ideological spread as cascading geographic contagion. Depends on: encirclement narrative
25. **Threat visualization** — using color, symbols, and scale to depict geopolitical danger. Depends on: color psychology, domino theory
26. **Visual argument** — how maps function as persuasive documents, not neutral records. Depends on: cartographic rhetoric, threat visualization
27. **Rhetorical analysis** — techniques for deconstructing a map's persuasive strategies. Depends on: visual argument, semiotic power
28. **Algorithmic curation** — how digital platforms choose what geographic information to display. Depends on: Web Mercator, data manipulation
29. **Digital gatekeeping** — platform power to determine what appears on the map. Depends on: algorithmic curation
30. **Deepfake geography** — manipulated satellite or map imagery presenting false realities. Depends on: digital gatekeeping
31. **Counter-mapping** — creating alternative maps that challenge official narratives. Depends on: erasure techniques, visual argument
32. **Decolonial practice** — using cartography to resist imperial spatial logic. Depends on: counter-mapping, colonial power
33. **Algorithmic bias** — how automated systems encode and perpetuate cartographic inequities. Depends on: algorithmic curation, perceptual bias

## Dependencies

### Foundation Layer (Lessons 1-6)
- **Area distortion** requires understanding **geometric distortion** because you need to know that all projections distort something before you can analyze which properties are distorted where
- **Perceptual bias** builds on **area distortion** because the way we mentally model geography is shaped by the visual size relationships we repeatedly see
- **Political choices** depends on **trade-offs** because understanding that no projection is neutral requires first knowing that every projection sacrifices some accuracy

### Projection Politics Layer (Lessons 7-11)
- **Projection controversy** requires **area distortion** and **political choices** because the Peters-Mercator debate is fundamentally about whether preserving area matters more than preserving shape
- **Web Mercator** inherits **Mercator projection** biases because digital platforms adopted the same projection for technical reasons, carrying forward its political implications
- **Prime meridian** connects to **colonial power** because the Greenwich standard reflects British imperial dominance, not geographic necessity

### Visual Rhetoric Layer (Lessons 12-17)
- **Data manipulation** builds on **cartographic rhetoric** because strategic use of visual variables (color, scale, classification) is the mechanism of manipulation
- **Choropleth bias** combines **area distortion** with **data manipulation** because area-based visualization misleads specifically when geographic size doesn't correlate with the variable being mapped
- **Erasure techniques** depend on both **omission** and **colonial power** because strategic exclusion was central to imperial cartography

### Historical Propaganda Layer (Lessons 18-22)
- **Territorial claims** builds on **lebensraum** (and broader **political choices**) because Nazi expansionism is a specific case of using maps to justify conquest
- **Encirclement narrative** requires **cartographic rhetoric** and **territorial claims** because depicting geopolitical threat requires both visual persuasion techniques and spatial framing
- **Rhetorical analysis** synthesizes **visual argument** and **semiotic power** because deconstructing a propaganda map requires understanding both its overall persuasive strategy and its symbolic details

### Digital Layer (Lessons 23-26)
- **Digital gatekeeping** extends **algorithmic curation** because platform power to include/exclude is the consequence of automated selection systems
- **Counter-mapping** directly opposes **erasure techniques** and uses **visual argument** — it's the practice of making visible what dominant cartography hides
- **Algorithmic bias** combines **algorithmic curation** with **perceptual bias** because automated systems can encode the same prejudices as human-made maps, at scale

## Critical Bottlenecks

### Bottleneck 1: Projection Problem → Trade-offs
Students must internalize that **there is no perfect projection** before they can understand that projection choice is inherently political. If they think "better maps" can solve distortion, they'll miss the entire argument about power and perspective.

### Bottleneck 2: Visual Rhetoric → Propaganda Analysis
Understanding **cartographic rhetoric** as a deliberate persuasive practice (not accidental design) is essential before analyzing 20th-century propaganda. If students think "bad maps" just have errors, they won't see intentional deception.

### Bottleneck 3: Historical Propaganda → Digital Platforms
The leap from **Nazi/Cold War maps** to **algorithmic bias** requires connecting human intent to automated systems. Students need to see that algorithms encode political choices, even when they feel neutral or technical.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Better projections eliminate bias"** — false. All projections involve choices; the question is which values those choices serve.
2. **"Digital maps are objective because they're data-driven"** — false. Algorithmic curation and platform design encode political decisions.
3. **"Propaganda maps are historical artifacts"** — false. Visual persuasion through cartography continues in election maps, geopolitical infographics, and platform interfaces.
4. **"Color is just aesthetic"** — false. Color choices carry emotional and political weight (red = danger, blue = calm, green = safe).
5. **"Omission is accidental"** — false. What's left off a map is often as deliberate as what's included.

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic geography** — needed for understanding projections, coordinates, and spatial relationships
- **Awareness of historical propaganda techniques** — needed for recognizing persuasive intent in visual media
- **Critical reading skills** — needed for deconstructing visual arguments and rhetorical strategies

## Learning Pathway Notes

The curriculum follows a spiral: **geometric → perceptual → political → historical → digital**. Students start with the math (why all maps distort), move to perception (how distortion shapes thinking), then to politics (how choices encode power), then to history (specific cases of propaganda), and finally to present (digital continuation).

Review lessons (6, 13, 20) occur at module boundaries to consolidate before adding complexity.
