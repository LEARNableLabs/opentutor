# Narrative Theory — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Narrative theory at the intermediate level is fundamentally about **systematic analysis** — moving from intuitive story appreciation to rigorous structural examination. The pedagogy should balance abstract conceptual frameworks (Genette's analytical vocabulary) with concrete application to diverse texts. This is a **dual-mode topic**: students need to (1) master terminology as precise analytical tools, and (2) apply those tools to texts they care about (novels, films, games, TV series). Start every concept with a concrete example, then extract the principle, then apply it elsewhere. Narrative theory is **highly portable** — once students grasp focalization in a novel, they can see it in film, games, graphic novels. Exploit this transmedia applicability to sustain engagement.

The difficulty is largely **conceptual rather than computational** — there are no equations, but there are abstract distinctions (focalization ≠ narration, story ≠ discourse) that require mental reorientation. Use visual diagrams (timeline reconstructions, narrative level diagrams, focalization schematics) to make the abstract concrete.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Focalization is just point of view"
**Why students get this wrong**: Traditional "point of view" terminology (first-person, third-person limited/omniscient) conflates who perceives with who narrates. Students arrive with this vocabulary ingrained.

**How to correct it**: Introduce Genette's focalization explicitly as *who sees* (focalization) vs. *who speaks* (narrative voice). Use examples where they diverge: "She watched him nervously" — third-person narrator (speaks) with external focalization on "him" (sees). Practice separating the two in every analysis.

### 2. "Story means the text's sequence"
**Why students get this wrong**: In everyday usage, "story" means the narrative as presented. Students don't naturally reconstruct chronology.

**How to correct it**: Use mystery or nonlinear narratives (*Memento*, *Arrival*, detective fiction) where reconstruction is necessary for comprehension. Have students create dual timelines: fabula (chronological) vs. syuzhet (as presented). The gap between them is the point.

### 3. "Flashbacks are always character memories"
**Why students get this wrong**: Conflating narrative technique (analepsis) with story content (memory). Most flashbacks *are* framed as memories, so the association sticks.

**How to correct it**: Show analepsis without memory framing — omniscient narration that moves backward in time without a character remembering. Distinguish structural description (analepsis) from story motivation (why the order changed).

### 4. "Unreliable narrator = liar"
**Why students get this wrong**: Famous unreliable narrators (Patrick Bateman, Humbert Humbert) often deceive, so students equate unreliability with deception.

**How to correct it**: Expand unreliability to include limited knowledge, bias, values misalignment with implied author, naivety (*Huck Finn*), madness (*The Yellow Wallpaper*). Unreliability is any gap between narrator's account and implied author's norms. Not all unreliability is intentional lying.

### 5. "The narrator is the author"
**Why students get this wrong**: In memoir, essay, autobiography, the narrator often *is* the author, creating a default association.

**How to correct it**: Introduce the **implied author** concept — the author-image constructed by the text, distinct from the actual author. Even in first-person fiction, the "I" is a construct. Use examples of authors writing across genres/voices to show narrator as role, not person.

### 6. "Characters are people"
**Why students get this wrong**: We naturally empathize with characters as if they're real. Psychological realism encourages this.

**How to correct it**: Present structuralist character theory (Propp, Greimas) explicitly as **one lens** among many. Characters can be analyzed as functions (helpers, opponents) *and* as psychological beings. Neither is the "true" view — they're complementary frameworks. Acknowledge the tension between formalist and humanist approaches.

### 7. "Narrative time is like real time"
**Why students get this wrong**: Students often don't consciously notice duration manipulation. A chapter covering five minutes and a chapter covering five years seem equivalent while reading.

**How to correct it**: Quantify duration explicitly. Count pages vs. story time. Show how summary compresses, scene matches real-time, pause (description) stops story time, ellipsis erases it. Make students estimate ratios: 10 pages = 2 minutes, 1 sentence = 10 years.

### 8. "Genre is just for categorization"
**Why students get this wrong**: Genre feels like a filing system (mystery, romance, sci-fi), not a structural force.

**How to correct it**: Show how genre creates **narrative schemas** — expectations, conventions, interpretive strategies. Mystery genre demands withholding information; romance demands union-obstacle-reunion; tragedy demands downfall. Genre isn't superficial; it's structural.

### 9. "Film POV works like literary POV"
**Why students get this wrong**: Terminology overlap ("point of view") between literature and film, but mechanisms differ.

**How to correct it**: Distinguish camera perspective (visual focalization) from narrative voice (voiceover, intertitles). Film can show character subjective shots (internal focalization) or objective camera (external), but the narrator might not be visible. Adaptation studies make this clear: *The Great Gatsby* film must externalize Nick Carraway's narration somehow.

### 10. "Serial narratives are just longer stories"
**Why students get this wrong**: TV series or comic series seem like extended novels, not structurally different forms.

**How to correct it**: Identify serial-specific features: **episodic closure** (each episode resolves something), **deferred closure** (overarching mysteries span seasons), **recaps and redundancy** (for viewers who missed episodes), **cliffhangers** (formal requirement at boundaries). Seriality isn't length; it's structural rhythm.

## Level Adjustments

### At Beginner Level
- Focus on story vs. discourse, basic narrative arc, character functions
- Use contemporary, accessible texts (popular film, YA literature)
- Skip theoretical debates (classical vs. postclassical narratology)
- Minimal French theory terminology; use English equivalents where possible

### At Intermediate Level (This Curriculum)
- Master Genette's full framework: time (order/duration/frequency), focalization, narrative levels, voice
- Engage with both classical (Genette, Propp, Barthes) and postclassical (cognitive, transmedia) approaches
- Apply frameworks to diverse media (novels, film, games, serial TV)
- Introduce theoretical tensions (formalism vs. reader response, structuralism vs. post-structuralism)
- Use canonical examples (Austen, Nabokov, Flaubert) alongside contemporary (prestige TV, interactive fiction)

### At Advanced Level
- Engage primary theoretical texts (Genette's *Narrative Discourse*, Bal's *Narratology*)
- Debates within narratology: natural vs. unnatural, cognitive turn, feminist narratology, postcolonial critiques
- Apply to experimental/difficult texts (Robbe-Grillet, *House of Leaves*, *Ulysses*)
- Develop original analytical frameworks or critiques of existing ones
- Interdisciplinary applications (philosophy of time, cognitive science, computational analysis)

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### 1. Narrative Time and Einstein's Relativity
**When to drop this in**: After mastering order/duration/frequency (Lesson 8). The realization that narrative time manipulation (flashback, time dilation, simultaneity) mirrors philosophical and scientific puzzles about time itself. Bergson's *durée* vs. clock time; McTaggart's A-series (tensed) vs. B-series (tensionless) time. Students love this connection.

### 2. Focalization in Virtual Reality and Video Games
**When to drop this in**: After understanding focalization (Lesson 9-10). VR creates enforced first-person internal focalization — you *are* the focalizer. Games often feature ludic vs. narrative focalization conflicts (cutscenes change focalization from gameplay). Narratology meets ludology.

### 3. Metalepsis in Postmodern Architecture and Art
**When to drop this in**: After narrative levels/metalepsis (Lesson 12-13). Borges's *The Garden of Forking Paths*, Escher's *Drawing Hands*, Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* — all play with level transgression. Connect narrative metalepsis to broader postmodern boundary-crossing.

### 4. Free Indirect Discourse in Music and Film
**When to drop this in**: After FID (Lesson 15). How film uses "free indirect point of view" (camera movement suggesting character perspective without POV shot). How music can blend diegetic (characters hear it) and non-diegetic (only audience hears) — sonic equivalent of FID.

### 5. Cognitive Science of Gap-Filling
**When to drop this in**: After narrative gaps/reader response (Lesson 23). Iser's "blanks," cognitive psychology's "bridging inferences," AI's "commonsense reasoning" problem. How do humans infer unstated information? Why is this hard for machines?

### 6. Unnatural Narratology and Impossible Worlds
**When to drop this in**: Final lessons (Lesson 26). Connect to modal logic (possible worlds semantics), impossible geometries (Escher), quantum mechanics (superposition). Narratives that violate logic *show* us the boundaries of what's conceivable.

### 7. Transmedia and the MCU's Narrative Ecosystem
**When to drop this in**: Transmedia lesson (Lesson 25). The Marvel Cinematic Universe as massive transmedia narrative — films, TV, comics, theme parks. How does narrative coherence work across media and authorship? Jenkins's "convergence culture."

## Difficulty Progression Notes

**Lessons 1-4 (Foundations)**: Difficulty 2-3. Ease students in with intuitive concepts (story vs. discourse, causality) grounded in familiar examples. Build confidence.

**Lessons 5-8 (Narrative Time)**: Difficulty 3. First sustained conceptual climb. Order/duration/frequency are abstract but visualizable. Use timelines, diagrams, concrete examples. Lesson 8 is a teach-back to consolidate.

**Lesson 11 (First Review)**: Difficulty 1. Breathing room. Reinforce time and perspective before moving to advanced topics.

**Lessons 12-15 (Advanced Perspective)**: Difficulty 4. Peak difficulty. Narrative levels, metalepsis, FID are conceptually dense and require tracking multiple layers. Expect this to be the hardest stretch. Reward completion with rabbit holes.

**Lessons 16-19 (Character and Action)**: Difficulty 3. Shift from abstract (perspective) to structural (character functions). Different cognitive mode, but still substantial.

**Lesson 18 (Second Review)**: Difficulty 2. Consolidate voice/levels/character before moving to plot.

**Lessons 20-23 (Plot and Structure)**: Difficulty 2-4. Mix of accessible (narrative arc, genre) and challenging (gaps, reader response). Lesson 23 (gaps) is the second difficulty 4 peak.

**Lesson 24 (Third Review)**: Difficulty 1. Final consolidation before contemporary applications.

**Lessons 25-26 (Contemporary Applications)**: Difficulty 4. Transmedia and unnatural narratology are advanced extensions. Students who've mastered foundations can handle abstraction.

**Lesson 27 (Capstone)**: Difficulty 2. Teach-back, synthesis, reflection. Not cognitively difficult, but requires integration.

## Assessment Strategies

### Formative (Ongoing)
- **Analysis exercises**: Provide short excerpts (novels, film scenes, game cutscenes), ask students to identify focalization, narrative levels, temporal order
- **Timeline reconstruction**: Give a nonlinear narrative, have students build fabula timeline
- **Focalization diagrams**: Map who sees/who speaks in complex passages
- **Teach-backs**: Students explain a concept using their own example (Lessons 8, 18, 27)

### Summative (Milestone)
- **Close reading with narratological toolkit**: Analyze 2-3 pages of a text using 5+ narratological concepts accurately
- **Comparative media analysis**: Analyze the same story in two media (novel + film adaptation), focusing on medium-specific narrative techniques
- **Synthetic essay**: "How does [text] use narrative structure to create [effect]?" Requires integrating multiple concepts

### Self-Assessment
- **Before/after reflection**: Lesson 27 prompts "What can you see now that you couldn't before?" Students articulate their own growth
- **Concept checklist**: Can you define, identify, and apply each major concept?

## Engagement Tactics

1. **Use texts students love**: Don't force canonical examples. If a student loves *Breaking Bad*, analyze its seriality, unreliable narration, and temporal structure. If they love *The Legend of Zelda*, analyze ludonarrative focalization.

2. **Visualization**: Every abstract concept should have a diagram. Narrative levels = nested boxes. Focalization = eye icon vs. speech bubble. Order = dual timelines.

3. **Debates**: Set up tensions — "Are characters people or functions?" "Does the author's intent matter?" Narratology has unresolved questions; let students explore them.

4. **Creative application**: After learning a concept, have students try to *create* a narrative using it. Write a scene with internal focalization. Create a metaleptic moment. Design a narrative ellipsis.

5. **Transmedia variety**: Rotate between novels, films, TV, games, graphic novels. Every lesson should feel fresh in its examples.

6. **Rabbit holes as rewards**: After a difficult stretch (e.g., Lessons 12-15), offer a mind-blowing connection (narrative time and physics, metalepsis in art) as a reward for persistence.
