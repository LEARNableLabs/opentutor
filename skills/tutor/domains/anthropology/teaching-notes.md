# Anthropology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Teaching anthropology at the intermediate level requires balancing three tensions: (1) celebrating cultural diversity while avoiding exoticization, (2) presenting theoretical frameworks while acknowledging their limitations and debates, and (3) integrating qualitative ethnographic insight with quantitative evolutionary models. The key pedagogical move is using **specific ethnographic cases** as anchors for abstract concepts — students understand "patrilineal descent" through the Nuer, "liminality" through initiation rituals, "prestige bias" through apprenticeship. Anthropology is fundamentally about **seeing the familiar as strange and the strange as familiar**, so oscillate between analyzing the student's own culture and distant ones.

At the intermediate level, students should engage with **theoretical debates** (alliance vs. descent, interpretive vs. scientific approaches) rather than just learning definitions. Use provocative questions to surface assumptions, then complicate them with ethnographic counterexamples. Anthropology's strength is **comparison**, so constantly ask: "How would this work differently in X society?"

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Primitive societies" are simple or frozen in time** — Students often treat non-industrial societies as evolutionary relics. Counter: Emphasize that all contemporary societies are equally evolved and complex; "simple technology" ≠ "simple social organization." Use examples like Australian Aboriginal kinship systems (incredibly complex) or Melanesian exchange networks.

2. **Kinship = biological family** — Students default to their own cultural model (usually bilateral/nuclear family). Counter: Start with radical examples (Nayar, where biological fathers have no social role; Trobriand, where fathers aren't seen as biological contributors) to break the biology assumption. Then build up the logic of unilineal descent.

3. **Rituals are relics that modern people have outgrown** — Students see ritual as irrational superstition. Counter: Analyze contemporary WEIRD rituals (graduations, weddings, sports fandom) using the same framework. Show how ritual creates meaning and cohesion even in secular contexts.

4. **Culture is a coherent, integrated system** — Students over-integrate culture after reading symbolic anthropology. Counter: Emphasize contradictions, competing interpretations, and cultural change. Culture is contested and messy, not a neat symbolic package.

5. **Cultural evolution = cultural progress** — Students import teleological assumptions from everyday language ("evolved" = "better"). Counter: Clarify that evolution is change over time via selection, not improvement. Cultural practices can evolve in maladaptive directions (Easter Island deforestation, etc.).

6. **Anthropologists just describe; they don't explain** — Students see ethnography as storytelling, not science. Counter: Show how ethnographic detail generates theoretical insights (Turner on ritual, Geertz on meaning) and how evolutionary models make testable predictions. Anthropology does both interpretation AND explanation.

7. **Alliance theory and descent theory are contradictory** — Students want one right answer. Counter: Frame as complementary perspectives (Kuper's synthesis). Descent theory captures corporate groups and political structure; alliance theory captures marriage exchange and inter-group relations. Both are real.

8. **Symbols have dictionary definitions** — Students treat symbols like words with fixed meanings. Counter: Use Geertz's method — thick description shows how meaning emerges from context, not inherent in the symbol. Same symbol (e.g., fire) means different things in different ritual contexts.

9. **Matrilineal = matriarchal** — Students confuse descent rules with authority. Counter: Clarify that matrilineal societies can be (and often are) male-dominated. Descent ≠ power. The Trobriand case is instructive: matrilineal but mother's brother holds authority.

10. **Cultural transmission is just teaching** — Students equate transmission with explicit instruction. Counter: Most cultural learning is implicit (prestige bias, conformist bias, observation). Humans are cultural sponges who absorb norms without conscious teaching.

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Level (this curriculum)

- **Theoretical depth**: Engage with debates (functionalism vs. structuralism, interpretive vs. scientific), not just definitions. Present competing theories and ask students to evaluate evidence.
- **Formalism**: Use some technical terminology (affines, FBD marriage, model-based bias) but always ground in examples. Kinship diagrams are useful but not the focus.
- **Primary sources**: Assign excerpts from classic texts (Geertz's cockfight essay, Turner on liminality) rather than just textbook summaries.
- **Critique**: Encourage critical engagement. What are the limits of thick description? Does dual inheritance theory reduce culture to evolution? These are productive debates.
- **Application**: Ask students to analyze contemporary phenomena using anthropological frameworks. How does TikTok change cultural transmission? What are modern rites of passage?

### Adjustments for Other Levels

- **Beginner**: Focus on cultural relativism and ethnographic examples. Skip theoretical debates. Use more contemporary/relatable cases (American high school cliques, wedding rituals). Avoid dense terminology.
- **Advanced**: Dive into primary theoretical works (Lévi-Strauss, Sahlins, Sperber). Engage with methodological controversies (Geertz vs. D'Andrade, memetics vs. epidemiology). Formal models of cultural evolution (replicator dynamics, conformist bias math). Original ethnographies, not textbook summaries.

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### When to Drop These In

- **Kinship and reproductive technology** (lesson 9) — How do sperm donors, surrogates, and IVF challenge Western kinship categories? What would Schneider say? Connects kinship to contemporary bioethics.

- **Ritual and neuroscience** (lesson 13) — Harvey Whitehouse's "modes of religiosity" theory: high-arousal rituals (painful, rare) create episodic memories and small tight groups; low-arousal rituals (repetitive, frequent) create semantic memories and large diffuse communities. Connects ritual to memory systems.

- **Dunbar's number and social networks** (lesson 8) — Robin Dunbar's argument that human brain size limits social networks to ~150. Kinship and ritual are technologies for scaling beyond Dunbar's number. Connects anthropology to evolutionary psychology.

- **The Church's Marriage and Family Program** (lesson 19) — Henrich's argument that the medieval Catholic Church's incest taboos and marriage rules broke down intensive kinship networks in Europe, creating WEIRD psychology. Connects all three modules (kinship → institutions → psychology).

- **Memes and cultural evolution** (lesson 16) — Dawkins' meme concept, Dennett's elaboration, Sperber's critique. Is culture made of replicators (like genes) or attractors? Deep theoretical rabbit hole on units of cultural selection.

- **Kinship and language** (lesson 6) — Chomskyan linguistics meets kinship terminology. Are there universal constraints on kinship systems (like universal grammar)? Connects anthropology to cognitive science.

- **Ritual and political power** (after lesson 15) — Maurice Bloch's argument that ritual creates hierarchy by dissociating authority from individual persons. Connects ritual to political anthropology and history.

- **The Axial Age** (lesson 24) — Bellah's argument that a shared cultural transformation ~500 BCE created world religions. Connects cultural evolution to world history and comparative religion.

## Engagement Strategies

1. **Use provocative questions** — Lesson titles are framed as questions to spark curiosity. Don't answer immediately; let students speculate first.

2. **Ethnographic specificity** — Always ground concepts in specific people and places. Don't teach "matrilineal descent"; teach "how the Trobriand Islanders trace descent through mothers."

3. **Compare to student's culture** — After every exotic example, flip it: "How does your culture handle this? What would a Trobriand anthropologist find weird about American kinship?"

4. **Theoretical debates as detective stories** — Frame Lévi-Strauss vs. Leach or Geertz vs. Harris as solving a mystery with different methods. What evidence supports each view?

5. **Contemporary applications** — Use current events and pop culture. Cultural evolution theory illuminates social media virality. Ritual theory explains sports fandom. Kinship analysis applies to chosen families and queer kinship.

6. **Visual aids** — Kinship diagrams, ritual phase diagrams, cultural transmission networks. Anthropology is spatial and relational; diagrams help.

7. **"What's at stake?"** — For every theoretical debate, clarify why it matters. The Geertz vs. D'Andrade debate isn't just academic; it's about whether anthropology is a science or an interpretive art.

## Pacing Notes

- **Kinship module is front-loaded** because kinship terminology (lesson 6) is the toughest concept. Build slowly, consolidate with review (lesson 7), then proceed.
- **Ritual module flows** from concrete (rites of passage) to abstract (liminality, symbolism). Turner first, then Geertz.
- **Cultural evolution module requires paradigm shift** — students must abandon "culture vs. biology" thinking. Expect resistance; use multiple examples (lactase, Tasmanians, WEIRD) to hammer it home.
- **Synthesis module is light** to allow reflection and integration. Not all students will want lesson 24 (resource drop), but those who are hooked will dive deep.

## Assessment Checkpoints

- **After lesson 7** — Can the student diagram a kinship system and identify descent pattern?
- **After lesson 14** — Can the student identify ritual phases and explain social functions?
- **After lesson 21** — Can the student explain how cultural evolution differs from genetic evolution and give examples?
- **After lesson 22** — Can the student integrate all three domains to analyze a contemporary case?

If student struggles, slow down and add more examples. If they breeze through, point to rabbit holes and primary sources.
