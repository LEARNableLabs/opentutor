# Philosophy of Language — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Philosophy of language at the intermediate level is best taught through **thought experiments and puzzle cases** rather than formal semantics. The field is driven by intuition pumps (Morning Star/Evening Star, Twin Earth, empty names) that reveal deep problems with common-sense assumptions about meaning. Start with Frege's puzzles, use them to motivate Russell's theory, then show how Kripke and Putnam overturn the Frege-Russell framework. The progression is historical but also dialectical: each theory responds to problems in its predecessor. Students should engage with **primary texts** (even if excerpted) and learn to **construct counterexamples** — the field's signature move.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Sense and reference are the same as connotation and denotation"**
   - Why students get this wrong: The terminology sounds similar, and both involve two-level semantics
   - How to correct: Connotation/denotation is about literal vs. associated meaning; sense/reference is about cognitive mode of presentation vs. object picked out. 'The Morning Star' and 'the Evening Star' have different senses but the same reference, not different connotations

2. **"Proper names have no meaning, they just point to things"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Oversimplified reading of direct reference theory
   - How to correct: Even direct reference theorists think names have semantic content (the object itself); the debate is whether names have *descriptive* meaning. Names do semantic work — they're not mere labels

3. **"Kripke proved descriptivism is false"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Modal arguments are compelling and often presented as decisive
   - How to correct: Kripke provided strong counterexamples, but sophisticated descriptivist responses exist (metalinguistic descriptivism, reference fixing vs. meaning). Present as an ongoing debate, not a settled victory

4. **"Twin Earth shows that meanings aren't mental"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Putnam's slogan "meanings just ain't in the head" is catchy
   - How to correct: Twin Earth shows that *reference* (and perhaps truth conditions) aren't fully determined by mental states. Whether this means *meanings* aren't mental depends on what you think meaning is — many internalists accept the Twin Earth data but distinguish narrow from wide content

5. **"Rigid designators must always refer to something"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Confusing necessity of reference with necessary existence
   - How to correct: Rigid designation is about cross-world constancy of reference when the object exists, not about the object existing in all worlds. 'Aristotle' rigidly designates Aristotle but doesn't refer in worlds where he never existed

6. **"The causal theory says we need to trace a perfect causal chain to use a name correctly"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Taking the theory too literally
   - How to correct: The causal chain can be messy, have gaps, involve reference change. The theory is about rough historical continuity, not perfect transmission. It's a theory of reference determination, not a recipe for correct usage

7. **"Indexicals are just words with context-dependent meanings"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Over-generalizing from simple cases like 'I' and 'here'
   - How to correct: Many words are context-dependent ('local', 'enemy', 'ready'), but indexicals have a special semantic profile — their character (linguistic meaning) is a function from contexts to contents. Use Kaplan's distinction to show the specificity

8. **"Natural kind terms refer to superficial features we observe"**
   - Why students get this wrong: Pre-theoretical assumption that we define words by observable criteria
   - How to correct: Putnam's point is that 'water' refers to whatever shares the same hidden structure (H2O) as the paradigm samples, not to whatever looks/tastes like water. Use Twin Earth and molybdenum/aluminum cases

## Level Adjustments

### For Intermediate Students (this curriculum)

**Emphasize:**
- Thought experiments and intuition pumps as the primary methodology
- Comparing and contrasting competing theories (descriptivism vs. causal theory, internalism vs. externalism)
- Close reading of key passages from Frege, Russell, Kripke, Putnam
- Constructing and evaluating counterexamples
- Connecting to broader metaphysical and epistemological issues (modality, essentialism, mind-world relations)

**De-emphasize:**
- Heavy formal semantics (model theory, lambda calculus)
- Technical logic beyond basic propositional and predicate calculus
- Detailed history of analytic philosophy (provide context but don't get lost in it)
- Subtle variants of each theory (save for advanced study)

**Appropriate depth:**
- Students should be able to state the core claims of each major theory
- Students should understand the main arguments for and against each view
- Students should recognize how the theories connect to modal metaphysics and philosophy of mind
- Students should be able to apply the theories to novel cases

### Adjustments for Beginners

Would need:
- More scaffolding on basic logic (quantifiers, scope, validity)
- Simpler examples before the classic puzzles
- More explicit guidance on how to read philosophical arguments
- Less emphasis on modal arguments (harder without modal logic background)
- Avoid two-dimensional semantics entirely

### Adjustments for Advanced Students

Could add:
- Formal semantic frameworks (intensional logic, possible worlds semantics)
- Technical refinements (causal descriptivism, two-dimensional semantics, dynamic semantics)
- Connections to linguistics (generative grammar, formal pragmatics)
- Contemporary debates (semantic minimalism, contextualism, relativism)
- Applications to other areas (vagueness, fictional discourse, empty terms)

## Rabbit Holes

- **Frege's Context Principle** — "never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, only in the context of a sentence." Connects to holism, compositionality, and anti-psychologism. Drop in during lesson 3 (compositionality) or lesson 11 (cluster theory)

- **Madagascar Case** — Gareth Evans's example where 'Madagascar' came to refer to an island rather than part of the African mainland through reference shift. Challenges simple causal theory. Use during lesson 15 (causal chains) to show limitations

- **Gödel-Schmidt Thought Experiment** — Kripke's case where we use 'Gödel' even though the description "prover of incompleteness" might fit Schmidt. Devastating for pure descriptivism. Essential for lesson 14 (causal theory motivation)

- **Hesperus/Phosphorus as Necessary A Posteriori** — The identity statement "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true (if true at all) but was discovered empirically. Perfect case for lesson 27 and connects back to lesson 1

- **Donnellan's Referential/Attributive Distinction** — Definite descriptions can be used referentially (to talk about a particular individual) or attributively (whoever satisfies the description). Complicates Russell's theory. Good for lesson 5 or lesson 10

- **Føllesdal's Paradox of Identity** — Connects Frege's puzzle to modal logic and quantified modal logic. For students interested in formal aspects, mention during lesson 16 (rigid designation)

- **Burge's Arthritis Thought Experiment** — Shows social externalism (not just natural kind externalism). Use with lesson 21 (division of linguistic labor) to extend Putnam's insights

- **De Re vs. De Dicto Modality** — Believing "the number of planets might have been different" de dicto vs. believing of the number 8 that it might have been different de re. Connects scope, modality, and rigid designation. Great for lesson 17 (modal arguments)

## Difficulty Progression

### Early Foundations (Lessons 1-6, Difficulty 2-3)
Start accessible with Frege's familiar puzzles. Compositionality (lesson 3) is the first real challenge (difficulty 3) because it's abstract. Use-mention (lesson 4) drops back to 2 because it's intuitive once explained. Review at lesson 6 consolidates before the next jump.

### Description Theory (Lessons 7-13, Difficulty 3-4)
This is the first sustained challenge. Russell's logical form analysis (lesson 7-8) requires students to think in terms of quantifiers and scope — difficulty 3-4. Strawson's presupposition (lesson 9) hits difficulty 4 because it's subtle. Scope ambiguity (lesson 10) is slightly easier (3) because it's more mechanical. Reference borrowing (lesson 12) is hard (4) because it's a complex edge case. Review at lesson 13 is critical.

### Causal Theory (Lessons 14-19, Difficulty 3-4)
Kripke's causal theory (lesson 14) is conceptually easier than Russell's logical form, so starts at 3. Rigid designation (lesson 16) is the conceptual peak of the module at difficulty 4 — students must grasp possible worlds semantics. Modal arguments (lesson 17-18) sustain difficulty 4. Review at lesson 19 drops to difficulty 2.

### Natural Kinds (Lessons 20-24, Difficulty 3-4)
Twin Earth (lesson 20) is difficulty 4 — thought experiment is famous but the implications are subtle. Division of labor (lesson 21) is easier (3) once externalism is understood. Reference fixing vs. meaning (lesson 22) returns to difficulty 4 because it's a subtle distinction. Internalism/externalism debate (lesson 23) is also 4 — requires integrating multiple ideas. Review at lesson 24 provides relief.

### Advanced Topics (Lessons 25-28, Difficulty 3-5)
Indexicals (lesson 25) and demonstratives (lesson 26) are moderate difficulty (3) because they're intuitive, though character/content distinction adds complexity. Necessary a posteriori (lesson 27) is the curriculum's peak at difficulty 5 — it requires synthesis of modality, essentialism, rigid designation, and natural kinds. Final lesson (28) is a resource drop and integration (difficulty 3) to consolidate and point forward.

### Overall Arc
- Start accessible (2-3) to build confidence
- First peak: Russell's theory (3-4)
- Second peak: Rigid designation and modal arguments (4)
- Third peak: Twin Earth and externalism (4)
- Final peak: Necessary a posteriori (5)
- Regular review days (every 5-7 lessons) drop to difficulty 1-2 for consolidation
- Difficulty progression is wavelike: climb to a peak, review, climb again
