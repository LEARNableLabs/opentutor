# Ethnobotany — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Ethnobotany definition** — study of relationships between people and plants across cultures and time
2. **Human-plant coevolution** — mutual influence and adaptation between human societies and plant species
3. **Interdisciplinary methods** — combining botany, anthropology, ecology, linguistics, pharmacology
4. **Field methods** — participant observation, interviews, plant collection, documentation techniques
5. **Participatory research** — collaborative approaches that center community knowledge and agency
6. **Informed consent** — ethical requirement for research participation and knowledge sharing. Depends on: participatory research
7. **History of ethnobotany** — development of the field from plant exploration to collaborative science
8. **Bioprospecting** — searching for commercially valuable compounds in plants. Depends on: field methods, informed consent
9. **Research design** — formulating questions, sampling strategies, data collection protocols. Depends on: field methods, interdisciplinary methods
10. **Vavilov centers** — geographic regions where crop diversity originated. Depends on: human-plant coevolution
11. **Centers of diversity** — areas with highest genetic variation in crop species. Depends on: Vavilov centers
12. **Crop wild relatives** — wild ancestors and close relatives of domesticated plants. Depends on: centers of diversity
13. **Domestication syndrome** — suite of traits that distinguish domesticated from wild plants. Depends on: human-plant coevolution
14. **Artificial selection** — human-directed breeding over generations. Depends on: domestication syndrome
15. **Traditional agriculture** — farming systems developed over centuries of local adaptation. Depends on: artificial selection
16. **Polyculture** — growing multiple crops together for mutual benefit. Depends on: traditional agriculture
17. **In situ conservation** — preserving crop diversity in farms and gardens, not just seed banks. Depends on: traditional agriculture
18. **Seed sovereignty** — community control over seeds and genetic resources. Depends on: in situ conservation
19. **Agrobiodiversity** — genetic and species diversity in agricultural systems. Depends on: polyculture, in situ conservation
20. **Folk taxonomy** — cultural systems for classifying and naming plants. Depends on: interdisciplinary methods
21. **Ethnoclassification** — how different cultures organize knowledge of the natural world. Depends on: folk taxonomy
22. **Local knowledge** — place-based understanding developed through long-term observation. Depends on: ethnoclassification
23. **Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK)** — cumulative body of knowledge about environment-organism relationships. Depends on: local knowledge
24. **TEK validation** — testing traditional knowledge claims using scientific methods. Depends on: TEK, research design
25. **Knowledge integration** — combining traditional and scientific knowledge systems. Depends on: TEK validation
26. **Cultural transmission** — how knowledge passes between generations. Depends on: TEK
27. **Language loss** — disappearance of languages and associated knowledge. Depends on: cultural transmission
28. **Traditional medicine systems** — culturally-embedded healing practices using plants. Depends on: TEK
29. **Empirical knowledge** — understanding gained through observation and experience. Depends on: traditional medicine systems
30. **Ethnopharmacology** — scientific study of traditional medicines. Depends on: traditional medicine systems, interdisciplinary methods
31. **Bioassays** — laboratory tests for biological activity. Depends on: ethnopharmacology
32. **Drug discovery** — process of finding new pharmaceutical compounds. Depends on: bioassays, bioprospecting
33. **Bioactive compounds** — molecules with effects on living systems. Depends on: drug discovery
34. **Sacred plants** — species with religious or spiritual significance
35. **Ritual use** — plants in ceremonies and religious practices. Depends on: sacred plants
36. **Psychoactive compounds** — substances that alter consciousness or perception. Depends on: bioactive compounds
37. **Shamanic practices** — healing and spiritual traditions involving altered states. Depends on: psychoactive compounds, ritual use
38. **Symbolic meaning** — cultural significance beyond utilitarian value. Depends on: sacred plants
39. **Cultural change** — transformation of practices over time through contact and adaptation. Depends on: ritual use
40. **Syncretism** — blending of different cultural traditions. Depends on: cultural change
41. **Cultural appropriation** — adoption of cultural elements without permission or understanding. Depends on: cultural change
42. **Fiber plants** — species used for textiles and cordage
43. **Dye plants** — species yielding colorants for textiles
44. **Economic botany** — study of useful plants and their products. Depends on: fiber plants, dye plants
45. **Traditional technology** — tools and techniques developed within cultural contexts. Depends on: economic botany
46. **Biomaterials** — materials derived from living organisms. Depends on: traditional technology
47. **Biocultural diversity** — interlinked diversity of cultures, languages, and species. Depends on: language loss, agrobiodiversity
48. **Knowledge preservation** — efforts to document and maintain traditional knowledge. Depends on: cultural transmission, language loss
49. **Nagoya Protocol** — international agreement on access and benefit sharing. Depends on: bioprospecting, seed sovereignty
50. **Intellectual property** — legal frameworks for knowledge ownership. Depends on: Nagoya Protocol
51. **Traditional knowledge rights** — recognition of Indigenous and local community knowledge. Depends on: intellectual property
52. **Equitable partnerships** — collaborative research that fairly shares benefits. Depends on: traditional knowledge rights, participatory research

## Dependencies

### Foundational Cluster
- **Field methods** and **participatory research** are the practical foundation that enables all ethnobotanical work
- **Informed consent** builds on participatory research and is essential for ethical research
- **Interdisciplinary methods** spans the entire field — ethnobotany cannot exist without combining multiple knowledge systems

### Domestication Pathway
- Understanding **Vavilov centers** requires grasping human-plant coevolution
- **Domestication syndrome** explains the visible results of **artificial selection**
- **Traditional agriculture** represents the culmination of millennia of artificial selection
- **In situ conservation** only makes sense after understanding how traditional agriculture maintains diversity
- **Seed sovereignty** is both a practical conservation strategy and a political movement

### Knowledge Systems Pathway
- **Folk taxonomy** → **ethnoclassification** → **local knowledge** → **TEK** forms a progression from specific to general
- **TEK validation** bridges traditional and scientific epistemologies
- **Cultural transmission** and **language loss** are flip sides of the same process

### Medicinal Plants Pathway
- **Traditional medicine systems** rest on **empirical knowledge** gained over generations
- **Ethnopharmacology** applies scientific methods to traditional medicines
- **Bioassays** enable **drug discovery** by isolating **bioactive compounds**
- This pathway links directly to **bioprospecting**, raising ethical questions

### Cultural Significance Pathway
- **Sacred plants** serve as the foundation for **ritual use**
- **Psychoactive compounds** enable certain **shamanic practices**
- **Symbolic meaning** extends beyond chemistry to cultural systems
- **Cultural change** and **syncretism** show how traditions evolve

### Material Culture Pathway
- **Fiber plants** and **dye plants** are specific examples within **economic botany**
- **Traditional technology** builds on economic botany to create **biomaterials**
- This pathway often gets overlooked but represents enormous cultural knowledge

### Contemporary Issues Pathway
- **Biocultural diversity** synthesizes language, culture, and biodiversity
- **Knowledge preservation** responds to threats from **language loss**
- **Nagoya Protocol** addresses problems in **bioprospecting**
- **Traditional knowledge rights** and **equitable partnerships** represent evolving solutions

## Bottlenecks

### Methodological Bottleneck (Lessons 1-4)
Students must grasp ethnobotanical field methods and ethical protocols before progressing. Without understanding informed consent and participatory research, the entire field appears extractive.

### Coevolution Bottleneck (Lessons 5-6)
The domestication syndrome concept is abstract until students see specific examples (maize, wheat, rice). This "aha moment" unlocks understanding of human-plant relationships.

### TEK Validation Bottleneck (Lessons 10-13)
Students often get stuck trying to fit traditional knowledge into scientific frameworks or vice versa. The key insight is that both are valid ways of knowing with different strengths.

### Ethnopharmacology Methods (Lesson 16)
This is the technical peak — understanding bioassays, clinical trials, and research pipelines. Students need strong analytical thinking here.

### Ethics Synthesis (Lessons 29-31)
The final bottleneck is integrating everything into an ethical framework. Students must see how conservation, research, and cultural respect interconnect.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Ethnobotany is just cataloging plant uses"** — Actually, it's about relationships, knowledge systems, and power dynamics
2. **"Traditional knowledge is either validated by science or it's wrong"** — Different epistemologies can both be valid
3. **"All traditional medicines work"** — Some do, some don't; empirical testing is necessary but respect is still required
4. **"Domestication = improvement"** — It's adaptation for human use, which often means genetic narrowing and dependence
5. **"Indigenous peoples are stuck in the past"** — They are contemporary people with evolving knowledge systems
6. **"Bioprospecting benefits local communities"** — Historically it hasn't; new frameworks aim to change this
7. **"Plants are either medicinal, food, or symbolic"** — Most plants have multiple, overlapping uses
8. **"Ethnobotany is only about remote tribes"** — Urban ethnobotany, immigrant food plants, and heritage gardens are equally valid

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic biology** — needed for plant morphology, taxonomy, ecology (especially lessons 5-6, 14-17)
- **General ecology** — needed for understanding agricultural systems, conservation (lessons 5-8, 27-28)
- **Cultural awareness** — needed for respectful engagement with different worldviews (entire curriculum)
- **Research ethics basics** — helpful for lessons 2, 4, 12, 17, 28

## Learning Sequence Rationale

The curriculum moves from **methods** → **human-plant history** → **knowledge systems** → **applications** → **contemporary challenges**. This builds from concrete (field methods) to abstract (biocultural diversity), with regular returns to real-world examples. Review lessons at 7, 14, 21, and 28 consolidate learning every 6-7 lessons before major transitions.
