# Sourdough Science — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Sourdough science is uniquely suited to experiential learning because students can observe microbial processes in real time with minimal equipment. At intermediate level, bridge from sensory observations (smell, bubbles, rise) to mechanistic explanations (metabolic pathways, pH curves, species ecology). Use the student's starter as a living laboratory—every lesson should connect theory to something they can smell, see, or taste. Emphasize that sourdough is a model system for microbial ecology; principles here apply to gut microbiomes, soil communities, and fermented foods worldwide. Balance biochemistry rigor (pathways, species names, pH chemistry) with practical troubleshooting (why did my bread turn into a brick?).

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"LAB and yeast compete for resources"** — Students often assume a zero-sum game. Reality: syntrophy means they help each other. LAB break down maltose that yeast can't use efficiently; yeast produce B vitamins LAB need. Correct by showing growth curves of monocultures vs co-cultures.

2. **"More fermentation time = more sour"** — Partially true but incomplete. Reality: acid ratio (lactic:acetic) matters more than total acid. A warm, fast fermentation can be sharper than a long cool one due to acetic acid buildup. Correct by comparing pH curves at different temperatures.

3. **"All Lactobacillus species are interchangeable"** — Students treat LAB as monolithic. Reality: L. sanfranciscensis thrives at 30°C and produces mild lactic acid; L. brevis is heterofermentative and makes more acetic acid and CO2. Correct with species-specific temperature and metabolite profiles.

4. **"Commercial yeast is just domesticated wild yeast"** — True for beer/wine strains, but commercial *baker's* yeast (S. cerevisiae) is acid-sensitive and can't survive in mature sourdough. Wild yeast species (C. milleri, K. exigua) are acid-tolerant specialists. Correct by explaining ecological niches and stress tolerance.

5. **"The hooch layer means my starter needs feeding"** — Correct diagnosis, wrong mechanism. The liquid isn't alcohol (though some alcohol is present); it's metabolic waste products and water expelled from the gluten network as pH drops. Hooch signals overfermentation and resource depletion. Correct by describing water activity and metabolite buildup.

6. **"Gluten-free flours can't make sourdough"** — Students assume gluten is required for LAB/yeast. Reality: microbial fermentation works fine; you just lose structural gluten network. GF sourdough still produces acid, flavor, and rise. Correct by distinguishing fermentation (microbial) from structure (gluten).

7. **"You can kill a starter by neglecting it"** — Starters are remarkably resilient. Students panic about missed feedings. Reality: desiccated or refrigerated starters enter dormancy; a few feedings usually revive them. True death is rare (usually requires contamination or extreme heat). Correct by explaining endospore formation and cold tolerance.

8. **"Rye flour makes starter more sour"** — Students confuse flavor complexity with acidity. Reality: rye has more amylase (more sugar for microbes) and bran microbiota (more LAB diversity), but sourness depends more on fermentation temperature and time. Correct by isolating variables in controlled experiments.

9. **"Whole wheat is healthier because of the fiber"** — True but incomplete for sourdough. Reality: long fermentation degrades phytate (increases mineral bioavailability) and FODMAPs (improves digestibility) beyond just fiber. Sourdough *processing* adds health benefits independent of grain choice. Correct with biochemical pathways (phytase, protease).

10. **"Warmer is always better for faster rise"** — Students optimize for speed. Reality: LAB thrive in warmth (28-32°C) but yeast prefer cooler temps (18-24°C). Too warm = over-acidification before sufficient rise. Correct with Arrhenius curves and LAB:yeast ratio shifts.

## Level Adjustments

**For intermediate learners** (current level):
- Assume basic biology (cells, metabolism) but not microbiology coursework
- Introduce species names and metabolic pathways, but don't require memorization of every Lactobacillus species
- Use pH and temperature curves; students should read and interpret graphs
- Connect biochemistry to sensory outcomes (this metabolic pathway → this flavor)
- Include primary literature (accessible review papers) as resources, but don't expect critical reading of methods sections
- Troubleshooting should integrate multiple variables (not just "feed more often")

**If adjusting down to beginner**:
- Skip species-level distinctions; treat LAB and yeast as functional groups
- Focus on observable outcomes (bubbles, smell, rise) rather than metabolic pathways
- Simplify acid chemistry to "LAB make it sour" without hetero/homofermentation distinction
- Use more visual resources (videos, photo timelines) and fewer papers
- Troubleshooting as simple if-then rules (hooch → feed more often)

**If adjusting up to advanced**:
- Introduce metagenomics and 16S rRNA sequencing for community profiling
- Discuss metabolic flux analysis and stoichiometry of fermentation pathways
- Include enzymatic kinetics (Km, Vmax for amylase, protease)
- Explore industrial applications (continuous culture, controlled pH fermentation, strain selection)
- Deep dive into exopolysaccharide biosynthesis pathways
- Require primary literature reading and experimental design proposals

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

1. **Sourdough as a model for gut microbiome** — Same principles: LAB dominance, pH control, syntrophy with anaerobes, FODMAP degradation. Sourdough in a jar is easier to study than Lactobacillus in your colon, but the ecology is analogous. Drop this in around lesson 11 (pH control) or lesson 23 (digestibility).

2. **Ancient Egyptian bread and modern archaeology** — Researchers revived 4,500-year-old yeast from Egyptian pottery. How do spores survive millennia? What changed in domestication? Drop this in around lesson 4 (ecological succession) or lesson 26 (scaling).

3. **Biofilm formation in starter crusts** — The dried layer on jar walls isn't waste; it's a biofilm. LAB form structured communities with extracellular matrix. Same biology as dental plaque or industrial biofouling. Drop this in around lesson 22 (exopolysaccharides).

4. **Microbial terroir** — Does geography shape starter microbiomes? San Francisco fog, Belgian breweries, Italian air—romanticized but partially true. Bran microbiota varies by region; airborne inoculation is real but less dramatic than lore suggests. Drop this in around lesson 2 (who lives in your starter) or lesson 4 (colonization).

5. **Lactobacillus as probiotic** — Some LAB species in sourdough (L. plantarum, L. reuteri) are sold as probiotics. Do they survive baking? (No, heat kills them.) Do you get probiotic effects from eating sourdough? (Probably not live cells, but prebiotics and metabolites may still benefit gut.) Drop this in around lesson 5 (species diversity) or lesson 23 (digestibility).

6. **Sinkhorn algorithm and dough rheology** — Computational modeling of gluten networks uses optimal transport theory. Proteolysis changes network topology; can we predict texture from degradation rates? Unexpected math-biology connection. Drop this in around lesson 12 (proteases) for math-inclined students.

## Difficulty Progression Notes

- **Lessons 1-4**: Gentle onboarding. Build from sensory observations (smell, bubbles) to core concepts (LAB, yeast, acids, succession). Difficulty 1-2.
- **Lessons 5-7**: First difficulty peak. Introduce species-level diversity and syntrophy (conceptually challenging because it's counterintuitive). Difficulty 3-4.
- **Lesson 8**: Review to consolidate microbial ecology before moving to chemistry.
- **Lessons 9-13**: Biochemistry core. Enzymatic pathways, acid chemistry, proteolysis, CO2 production. Heavy on mechanisms. Difficulty 3-4, with lesson 13 as teach-back to ensure synthesis.
- **Lesson 14**: Review to consolidate chemistry before environmental factors.
- **Lessons 15-19**: Environmental control. Each lesson isolates one variable (temperature, hydration, flour, salt, feeding). Difficulty 3-4, with lesson 19 as teach-back (design a feeding schedule).
- **Lesson 20**: Review before flavor/texture module.
- **Lessons 21-24**: Flavor chemistry and applied biochemistry. Hardest conceptual material (VOCs, EPS, digestibility, sensory mapping). Difficulty 4, with lesson 24 as teach-back capstone.
- **Lessons 25-27**: Synthesis and application. Troubleshooting, scaling, experimental design. Difficulty 3-5, culminating in lesson 27 (design a starter from scratch = difficulty 5 peak).

## Assessment Strategies

- **Observational logs** — Have students document their own starter's behavior (rise time, smell, texture) while learning. Compare observations to theory in teach-back lessons.
- **Troubleshooting scenarios** — Present a failed starter (photos, description, timeline) and ask students to diagnose using microbial ecology principles. Can they distinguish over-acidification from yeast death from contamination?
- **Experimental design** — For final assessment, ask students to design an experiment to test a hypothesis (e.g., "Does rye flour increase lactic acid production compared to white flour?"). Requires integrating environmental factors, microbial ecology, and biochemistry.
- **Metabolic pathway mapping** — Given a set of conditions (temperature, hydration, flour type), predict acid ratio, rise speed, and flavor profile. Tests synthesis across modules.
- **Resource critique** — Have students evaluate a sourdough baking blog or YouTube video for scientific accuracy. Can they identify misconceptions and cite correct mechanisms?

## Engagement Hooks

- **Smell tests** — Have students describe their starter's aroma at different fermentation stages. Connect smell to VOC chemistry in lesson 21.
- **pH measurements** — If students have pH strips or a meter, track pH over fermentation time. Plot curves and compare to predictions.
- **Microscopy** — If accessible, view LAB and yeast under a microscope (even a phone microscope works). Seeing distinct cell morphologies makes the consortium real.
- **Taste comparisons** — Bake two loaves with different fermentation schedules (warm/fast vs cool/slow). Taste side-by-side and map flavors to metabolic pathways.
- **Starter swaps** — If teaching a cohort, have students exchange starters and compare microbial behaviors. Geography and flour history create real diversity.
