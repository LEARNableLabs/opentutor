# Microeconomics Research Summary

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Market Fundamentals
- Supply and demand mechanics
- Equilibrium analysis
- Comparative statics
- Elasticity and responsiveness
- Market clearing and price discovery

### 2. Consumer Theory
- Preferences and utility functions
- Budget constraints and opportunity cost
- Utility maximization
- Demand curves and the law of demand
- Income and substitution effects
- Revealed preference theory

### 3. Producer Theory
- Production functions and technology
- Cost structures (fixed, variable, marginal, average)
- Profit maximization
- Supply curves and the law of supply
- Returns to scale

### 4. Market Structures
- Perfect competition (price-taking behavior)
- Monopoly (price-setting power)
- Oligopoly (strategic interdependence)
- Monopolistic competition (differentiation)
- Market power and deadweight loss

### 5. Market Failures
- Externalities (positive and negative)
- Public goods and the free-rider problem
- Common resources and the tragedy of the commons
- Asymmetric information (adverse selection, moral hazard)
- Transaction costs and Coase theorem

### 6. Game Theory and Strategy
- Strategic form games
- Nash equilibrium
- Sequential games and backwards induction
- Repeated games and cooperation
- Applications to pricing, entry, and collusion

### 7. Welfare Economics
- Pareto efficiency
- Consumer and producer surplus
- Social welfare maximization
- Market efficiency vs. equity tradeoffs

## Learning Challenges

### Common Bottlenecks
1. **Graphical reasoning** — many students struggle to read and manipulate supply/demand diagrams
2. **Marginal thinking** — confusion between marginal vs. average concepts
3. **Optimization** — setting up and solving constrained optimization problems
4. **Strategic reasoning** — thinking through opponents' incentives in game theory
5. **Welfare analysis** — identifying winners and losers from policy changes

### Pedagogical Opportunities
- Real-world applications are everywhere (pricing, labor markets, environmental policy)
- Many concepts have strong visual/graphical representations
- Experimental economics allows hands-on learning
- Current policy debates provide motivation (carbon pricing, minimum wage, antitrust)
- Game theory applications to everyday life (dating, negotiation, cooperation)

## Intermediate-Level Considerations

At the intermediate level, students should:
- Be comfortable with basic calculus (derivatives for optimization)
- Understand algebraic manipulation and graphing
- Have some exposure to statistics (for understanding empirical applications)
- Be ready for formal models while appreciating real-world messiness
- Move beyond "common sense" to counterintuitive results (e.g., Giffen goods, backward-bending labor supply)
