Audience Profile

Audience Type
Undergraduate math or CS students (junior/senior level), recreational mathematicians, and mathematically inclined jugglers -- people who enjoy exploring unexpected mathematical structures in physical activities. Likely self-directed learners drawn to the topic out of curiosity rather than curricular obligation.
Goal
Intellectual enrichment through mathematical exploration. The learner wants to understand the deep combinatorial, algebraic, and probabilistic structures that govern juggling patterns -- not to pass an exam or build a product, but to experience the pleasure of seeing a physical art form through a mathematical lens and to build enough fluency to read foundational papers in the field.
Background
Solid discrete mathematics foundation: comfort with proof techniques (induction, contradiction, bijection), permutations and basic combinatorics, introductory graph theory, and elementary probability. Familiarity with linear algebra basics is helpful but not strictly required until later lessons. No prior knowledge of juggling or siteswap notation is assumed.
Tone
Rigorous but playful. The subject is inherently fun -- it is about juggling -- and the tone should reflect that without sacrificing mathematical precision. Key proofs are presented carefully, but motivation always comes first: show the pattern, build intuition with interactive tools, then prove it. The voice is that of an enthusiastic colleague sharing a favorite topic at the blackboard.
Depth
Intuition-first with key proofs worked in full. The student should be able to state and prove the average theorem, construct and analyze state graphs, derive the (b+1)^p counting formula, and set up the Markov chain model of random juggling. Algebraic-geometric and topological connections are introduced conceptually with clear statements of main results but without full proofs.
Time
15-20 minutes of focused study per lesson (reading, watching short clips, working through a key derivation), plus 10-15 minutes of exercises combining pen-and-paper proofs with interactive tool exploration. Consistent with the 1% rule: one concept per day, compounding over weeks.
Summary
The target learner is a mathematically fluent undergraduate or self-taught explorer with solid combinatorics and proof skills, drawn to the mathematics of juggling out of genuine curiosity. The north star is a learner who finishes the course able to read and follow a paper like Buhler et al. (1994) or Warrington (2003), and who can pick up any siteswap sequence and immediately see its combinatorial, algebraic, and probabilistic structure.
