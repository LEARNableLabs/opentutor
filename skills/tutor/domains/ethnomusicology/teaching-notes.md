# Ethnomusicology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Ethnomusicology at the intermediate level is **experiential and reflexive**. Students must move beyond describing "exotic" music to understanding how their own cultural assumptions shape what they hear. This is a listening-first, theory-second discipline—teach students to listen for what they don't already know, not just what they recognize.

The curriculum balances **theoretical frameworks** (emic/etic, fieldwork ethics, comparative methods) with **concrete case studies** (West African drumming, gamelan, Hindustani classical). Use audio examples constantly—ethnomusicology taught without sound is like teaching painting from text descriptions. Encourage critical listening journals where students reflect not just on "what did I hear?" but "what assumptions did I bring? What did I miss?"

At the intermediate level, students should begin to **conduct their own mini-ethnographies**: interview a musician from a different tradition, attend a cultural music event, transcribe and analyze a recording using culturally-appropriate frameworks. This is not a spectator sport.

## Common Misconceptions

### 1. "Ethnomusicology is the study of 'ethnic' (non-Western) music"
**Why students think this**: The term "ethno-" sounds like it means "ethnic" or "exotic." Early ethnomusicology was indeed focused on non-Western music.

**Correction**: Ethnomusicology studies **all music as culture**, including Western classical, pop, and folk. Bruno Nettl famously studied Western concert audiences as ethnographic subjects. The "ethno-" prefix refers to the ethnographic method, not the object of study. Some ethnomusicologists study only Western music.

**When to correct**: Lesson 1. Address this head-on in the first lesson or students will carry it through the entire curriculum.

### 2. "Emic/etic is just 'insider terminology' vs. 'outsider terminology'"
**Why students think this**: The distinction is often taught superficially as "emic = what they call it, etic = what we call it."

**Correction**: Emic and etic are **fundamentally different epistemologies**—different ways of knowing. An emic perspective understands music through the categories, values, and meanings of the culture itself. An etic perspective applies external analytical frameworks (often Western). Both are necessary, but the emic perspective has epistemological priority—the culture's own understanding is not just "one opinion" but the authoritative account of what the music means.

**When to correct**: Lesson 3. Use examples: "Is this rhythm in 6/8 or 12/8?" (etic) vs. "How do drummers count this pattern?" (emic). The etic question assumes Western meter; the emic question seeks the insider logic.

### 3. "Non-Western music is 'simpler' or 'more natural' than Western music"
**Why students think this**: Primitivist/romantic stereotypes persist. Students may think cultures without written notation have "simpler" musical systems, or that indigenous music is "closer to nature."

**Correction**: Musical complexity manifests differently across cultures. West African polyrhythm, Indian tala systems, and Middle Eastern maqam are **incredibly sophisticated**—often more complex than Western harmony in their respective dimensions (rhythm, improvisation, microtonal pitch). "Simpler" usually means "I don't yet understand the complexity." Oral transmission requires astonishing memory and pedagogical sophistication.

**When to correct**: Throughout, but especially lessons 13-24 (musical systems and case studies). Point out specific complexities that Western notation cannot capture.

### 4. "Transcription is just incomplete; better technology will fix it"
**Why students think this**: Students assume the problem is technical—if we had better notation or recording, we could capture everything.

**Correction**: Transcription is **theoretically biased, not just incomplete**. Western notation embeds specific music-theoretical assumptions (equal-tempered pitch, metrical rhythm, harmony as vertical simultaneity). Transcribing gamelan in Western notation doesn't just leave things out—it actively misrepresents the music by forcing it into Western theoretical categories. The "fix" is not better notation but multiple analytical frameworks, including the culture's own.

**When to correct**: Lesson 4. Show students a transcription of non-Western music and ask: "What does this notation hide or distort?" Use examples of rhythm notated as syncopation when it's actually an additive pattern.

### 5. "Culturally-grounded analysis means 'no criticism allowed'"
**Why students think this**: Students conflate cultural respect with uncritical acceptance. They think analyzing music on its own terms means suspending all judgment.

**Correction**: Culturally-grounded analysis means using **the culture's own evaluative criteria**, which can be very demanding. Hindustani musicians judge raga performance by rigorous standards of ornamentation, emotional affect, and improvisational creativity. West African drummers distinguish master drummers from novices. The difference is: whose standards are we using? Insider critique (emic) vs. outsider imposition (etic). You can critique music deeply using emic standards.

**When to correct**: Lesson 10. Give examples of insider critique—how do practitioners in this tradition judge good vs. bad performance?

### 6. "Globalization/world music is always cultural appropriation"
**Why students think this**: Contemporary social justice discourse makes students hyper-aware of appropriation but less clear on when cross-cultural exchange is legitimate.

**Correction**: The question is not "is cultural exchange happening?" (it always has) but "what are the **power dynamics and economic relationships**?" Appropriation involves: taking without attribution, profiting without reciprocity, decontextualizing to the point of distortion, and reinforcing colonial power structures. Cross-cultural collaboration can be ethical when it involves: attribution, equitable economic relationships, cultural context preservation, and mutual artistic respect. The key is power, not purity.

**When to correct**: Lessons 5, 9, 23, 26. Nuance this throughout rather than delivering a single lecture.

### 7. "Endangered music traditions should be preserved exactly as they are"
**Why students think this**: UNESCO World Heritage framing emphasizes preservation. Students romanticize "authentic" traditional music frozen in time.

**Correction**: **Living traditions change**—they always have. Preservation can kill a tradition by freezing it as museum artifact. The question is: who controls the process of change? Musicians from the tradition should decide whether to innovate, preserve, archive, or let something fade. "Preservation" can be a colonial impulse—outsiders deciding what a culture "should" keep. Better questions: How do practitioners want to evolve their tradition? How can we support cultural sovereignty over musical heritage?

**When to correct**: Lesson 27. This is a complex debate with no easy answer—present multiple perspectives.

### 8. "Fieldwork is about objectively documenting music"
**Why students think this**: Scientific language in early ethnomusicology emphasized "data collection" and "objective observation."

**Correction**: Fieldwork is **inherently subjective and relational**. The ethnomusicologist's presence changes what happens (observer effect). What gets recorded depends on relationships, trust, and social position. Reflexivity—awareness of one's own biases and impact—is essential. Fieldwork is not about extracting data from passive subjects but about building reciprocal relationships where musical knowledge is shared (not just taken).

**When to correct**: Lessons 5, 25. Emphasize ethics and reflexivity from the start.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner
- More structured—provide frameworks for every listening exercise
- Focus on broadening listening exposure—just getting students to hear non-Western music without Western assumptions
- Minimal theoretical frameworks—introduce emic/etic but don't dwell on epistemology
- Case studies as "tours"—descriptive rather than analytical
- No fieldwork—just listen and reflect

### Intermediate (this curriculum)
- Students should **apply frameworks independently**—given a new music tradition, use emic/etic, identify musical systems, conduct culturally-grounded analysis
- Critical listening—not just "what do I hear?" but "what assumptions am I bringing?"
- Begin fieldwork—mini-ethnographies, interviews, event attendance
- Engage with contemporary debates—appropriation, preservation, globalization
- Depth over breadth—fewer case studies, deeper analysis

### Advanced
- **Students generate their own research questions** and conduct original fieldwork
- Theoretical sophistication—engage with scholarly debates (Merriam vs. Blacking, universalism vs. relativism, applied ethnomusicology)
- Multiple field sites—comparative ethnography
- Methodological debates—positionality, representation, decolonizing methodologies
- Publication-quality work—students contribute to the field

## Rabbit Holes (Fascinating Connections)

### Music and Cognitive Science
**When to drop it**: After lesson 11 (musical universals vs. specificity)

The debate over musical universals connects to cognitive science research on innate vs. learned musical capacities. Are octave equivalence, consonance/dissonance, or rhythmic entrainment universal? Or are they culturally learned? Recent research (e.g., McDermott & Oxenham on Tsimane' people) shows that some "universal" musical perceptions are actually Western cultural constructs.

### Organology (Instrument Classification)
**When to drop it**: After lesson 16 (timbre and texture)

The Hornbostel-Sachs classification system (idiophones, membranophones, chordophones, aerophones) was an early attempt at cross-cultural organology. But it's Eurocentric—it privileges how sound is produced mechanically rather than cultural categories. Many cultures classify instruments by social function, ritual use, or gender. Example: Shona mbira is classified with ancestors and spirits, not with "plucked idiophones."

### Music and Language
**When to drop it**: After lesson 14 (maqam) or lesson 16 (timbre)

Tone languages (Mandarin, Yoruba) and music interact in fascinating ways—song melodies follow tonal contours to preserve linguistic meaning. Talking drums in West Africa encode language through pitch and rhythm. This challenges Western assumptions about the music/language boundary. (See works by David Locke, Steven Feld on "sound as culture.")

### Decolonizing Ethnomusicology
**When to drop it**: After lesson 9 (music and power) or lesson 25 (fieldwork ethics)

Contemporary scholars (Alejandro Madrid, Klisala Harrison, Robin Attas) are reimagining ethnomusicology without colonial assumptions. Questions: Can Western academia decolonize itself? Should indigenous scholars study their own traditions (insider advantage) or is that limiting? Who has authority to interpret? This connects to broader debates in anthropology and postcolonial studies.

### Music Technology and Digital Culture
**When to drop it**: After lesson 26 (globalization)

How do digital platforms (Spotify, YouTube, TikTok) change music circulation, identity formation, and genre boundaries? What happens to "field recording" when musicians self-publish on YouTube? How do diasporic communities use music streaming to maintain cultural connections? Ethnomusicologists now study digital fieldwork, algorithm bias, and online music communities.

### Ecomusicology
**When to drop it**: After lesson 8 (ritual music) or lesson 27 (preservation)

The emerging field of ecomusicology studies relationships between music, culture, and the natural environment. How do environmental changes (climate change, deforestation) affect music traditions dependent on natural materials or specific ecologies? How does music encode ecological knowledge? (See Jeff Todd Titon's work on sustainability.)

### Neurodiversity and Music Perception
**When to drop it**: After lesson 11 (musical universals)

Not everyone perceives music the same way—aphasia, amusia, autism, synesthesia all affect musical experience. This complicates claims about "universal" musical responses. Some autistic musicians perceive rhythm and timbre with extraordinary detail. This challenges normative assumptions about how music "should" be experienced.

## Difficulty Progression

### Lessons 1-6 (Foundations): Difficulty 1-3
- Start accessible (2) with defining the field and its history
- Build to moderate challenge (3) with emic/etic and transcription critique
- First review at difficulty 1

**Rationale**: Students need a gentle entry—ethnomusicology's core concepts (emic/etic, transcription bias) are intellectually challenging but not technical.

### Lessons 7-12 (Music and Culture): Difficulty 2-4
- Moderate start (2-3) with identity and ritual—concepts students recognize
- Peak difficulty (4) with music and power, culturally-grounded analysis—requires critical thinking and self-awareness
- Review at difficulty 2 (synthesis, not new material)

**Rationale**: Cultural analysis is the hardest part conceptually—students must critique their own assumptions while respecting other perspectives. This is cognitively demanding.

### Lessons 13-18 (Musical Systems): Difficulty 3-4
- Sustained high difficulty (3-4) because these lessons require **musical ear training** plus **conceptual reframing**
- Tuning systems, maqam, polyrhythm are all technically demanding
- Review at difficulty 1 to give students a break

**Rationale**: This is the technical peak—students are learning to hear differently (microtones, polyrhythm, timbral nuance). This is hard work.

### Lessons 19-24 (Case Studies): Difficulty 3-4
- Moderate to high difficulty (3-4) as students apply all prior learning to specific traditions
- Reviews at difficulty 2—synthesis across case studies

**Rationale**: Case studies are where everything comes together—students must use emic/etic, culturally-grounded analysis, musical systems knowledge, and ethical awareness simultaneously. Cognitively complex but intellectually rewarding.

### Lessons 25-27 (Fieldwork and Contemporary Issues): Difficulty 3-4
- Sustained high difficulty (3-4) because these are open-ended ethical questions with no easy answers
- Globalization, preservation, and fieldwork ethics require nuanced thinking

**Rationale**: The curriculum ends on ambiguity and complexity—students must grapple with unresolved debates in the field. This prepares them for real-world ethnomusicological practice.

### Review Cadence
- Reviews at lessons 6, 12, 18, 24 (every 5-7 lessons as recommended)
- Reviews are low difficulty (1-2) to consolidate learning and give cognitive breaks
- No final review—lesson 27 is a capstone discussion, not a review

## Teaching Tips

1. **Always use audio examples**. Ethnomusicology without sound is incoherent. Use UCLA Ethnomusicology Archive, Smithsonian Folkways, YouTube.
2. **Assign listening journals**. Have students listen to new music daily and write: What did I hear? What did I assume? What questions do I have?
3. **Encourage fieldwork**. Even intermediate students can attend a cultural music event, interview a musician, or transcribe a recording.
4. **Model reflexivity**. Share your own biases and assumptions as the teacher. "I first heard this as chaotic, then I learned..."
5. **Use comparison carefully**. Comparison is the heart of ethnomusicology but can reinforce hierarchies. Always ask: "On whose terms are we comparing?"
6. **Don't fetishize difference**. The goal is not to exoticize non-Western music but to denaturalize Western music—make Western assumptions visible.
7. **Center practitioners' voices**. Use interviews, documentaries, writings by musicians from the traditions studied. Don't just read Western scholars.
8. **Embrace ambiguity**. Ethnomusicology has unresolved debates (universals vs. relativism, preservation vs. change, insider vs. outsider authority). Don't smooth over these tensions—lean into them.
