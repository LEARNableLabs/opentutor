# Ethnomusicology — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Comparative musicology** — Early approach comparing musical systems across cultures; precursor to modern ethnomusicology
2. **Anthropology of music** — Studying music as cultural behavior, not just sound objects
3. **Emic vs. etic perspectives** — Insider (native) vs. outsider (analytical) viewpoints; foundational to all interpretation. Depends on: comparative musicology, anthropology of music
4. **Transcription limitations** — Western notation cannot capture microtones, timbral nuance, performance practice. Depends on: emic/etic perspectives
5. **Cultural ownership and ethics** — Who has the right to study, record, and represent music? Depends on: emic/etic perspectives, anthropology of music

6. **Music and identity** — How music constructs ethnicity, nationality, gender, class. Depends on: anthropology of music
7. **Sacred and ritual music** — Music's function in religion and ceremony. Depends on: anthropology of music, music and identity
8. **Music and power** — Protest, colonialism, decolonization, political resistance. Depends on: music and identity, ritual music
9. **Culturally-grounded analysis** — Analyzing music using frameworks from its own culture, avoiding ethnocentrism. Depends on: emic/etic perspectives, cultural ownership
10. **Musical universals vs. cultural specificity** — Tension between shared human musicality and cultural difference. Depends on: comparative musicology, culturally-grounded analysis

11. **Tuning systems** — Equal temperament, just intonation, gamelan tuning, maqam. Depends on: transcription limitations
12. **Modal systems (maqam)** — Middle Eastern approach to pitch organization. Depends on: tuning systems
13. **Polyrhythm and cyclic rhythm** — African and other traditions organizing rhythm beyond Western meter. Depends on: transcription limitations
14. **Timbre and texture** — Cultures that prioritize sound quality over pitch/rhythm. Depends on: transcription limitations
15. **Musical form (cyclical, call-and-response)** — Non-Western approaches to organizing time and structure. Depends on: polyrhythm, timbre

16. **West African drumming** — Ensemble interlock, master drummer, social function. Depends on: polyrhythm, call-and-response, culturally-grounded analysis
17. **Gamelan** — Indonesian ensemble music, colotomic structure, cosmology. Depends on: tuning systems, cyclic rhythm, music and worldview
18. **Hindustani classical music** — Raga, tala, improvisation within structure. Depends on: modal systems, cyclic rhythm, culturally-grounded analysis
19. **Native American ceremonial music** — Powwow, cultural sovereignty, ritual function. Depends on: sacred music, cultural ownership, music and identity
20. **Throat singing and world music** — Tuvan throat singing, globalization, market forces. Depends on: timbre, world music industry

21. **Fieldwork ethics** — Informed consent, representation, reciprocity in research. Depends on: cultural ownership, emic/etic perspectives
22. **Globalization and world music industry** — Cultural hybridity, authenticity debates, market forces. Depends on: music and power, fieldwork ethics
23. **Music preservation and repatriation** — UNESCO, digital archives, living traditions, who controls cultural heritage. Depends on: cultural ownership, globalization, fieldwork ethics

## Dependencies

### Foundational Theory Block
- **Emic/etic perspectives** require understanding both comparative musicology (the practice of comparison) and anthropology of music (the why behind studying music as culture)
- **Transcription limitations** become clear only after grasping emic/etic—you see that notation is an etic tool imposed on emic practices
- **Cultural ownership and ethics** emerge from the emic/etic tension—whose perspective counts?

### Music and Culture Block
- **Music and identity** builds on anthropology of music—once you see music as social behavior, you can see how it constructs identity
- **Ritual and sacred music** depends on music and identity—religious music is identity work
- **Music and power** requires understanding identity and ritual—political music mobilizes existing identity formations
- **Culturally-grounded analysis** synthesizes emic/etic with ethics—it's the method that respects insider perspectives
- **Musical universals vs. specificity** is the payoff for all prior concepts—can we compare if everything is culturally specific?

### Musical Systems Block
- **Tuning systems** require understanding transcription limitations first—why Western notation fails
- **Modal systems (maqam)** are a specific instance of alternative tuning/organization
- **Polyrhythm** and **timbre** also emerge from transcription limitations—they're what Western notation misses
- **Musical form** depends on understanding rhythm and timbre—form is built from these elements

### Case Studies Block
- All regional case studies depend on prior musical systems concepts (tuning, rhythm, timbre, form)
- All case studies also depend on culturally-grounded analysis—we analyze each on its own terms
- **West African drumming** showcases polyrhythm, call-and-response, and social function
- **Gamelan** exemplifies alternative tuning, cyclic structure, and music-as-cosmology
- **Hindustani classical** demonstrates modal systems, cyclic rhythm, and structured improvisation
- **Native American music** brings together ritual function, cultural ownership, and music-as-identity
- **Throat singing** illustrates timbre focus and introduces globalization themes

### Fieldwork and Contemporary Issues Block
- **Fieldwork ethics** synthesizes all prior ethics discussions (cultural ownership, emic/etic, representation)
- **Globalization** depends on understanding the case studies—what happens when these traditions enter global markets?
- **Preservation and repatriation** synthesizes ethics, ownership, and globalization—who controls endangered traditions?

## Critical Bottlenecks

### Emic/Etic (Lesson 3)
This is the **conceptual foundation** for everything that follows. If students don't grasp this distinction:
- They'll struggle with culturally-grounded analysis
- They won't understand why transcription is problematic
- Ethics discussions won't make sense
- They'll impose Western frameworks unconsciously

**Signs of trouble**: Student describes non-Western music only in Western terms (e.g., "it's in 6/8"), doesn't ask "how do practitioners think about this?"

### Culturally-Grounded Analysis (Lesson 10)
This is where **theory meets practice**. Students must be able to:
- Identify when they're using etic frameworks
- Seek out emic categories and concepts
- Analyze music using culturally-appropriate frameworks

**Signs of trouble**: Student treats all music as variations on Western norms, can't explain a musical tradition in its own terms.

### Transcription Limitations (Lesson 4)
Students need to understand that **notation is a choice, not a neutral tool**. This unblocks:
- Appreciation for tuning systems beyond 12-tone equal temperament
- Understanding of rhythms that don't fit Western meter
- Awareness of timbral complexity

**Signs of trouble**: Student insists "but how would you write it down?" as if notation is the only way to understand music.

## Common Prerequisite Gaps

Students often arrive with:
- **Western music theory bias**: Assume Western notation/theory is universal
- **Limited listening experience**: Only familiar with Western classical and pop
- **Anthropology gaps**: Don't understand cultural relativism, participant observation, reflexivity

## Learning Sequence Logic

1. **Foundations first** (Lessons 1-6): Establish the theoretical toolkit—emic/etic, transcription limits, ethics
2. **Culture theory** (Lessons 7-12): Apply toolkit to understand music's social roles—identity, ritual, power
3. **Musical systems** (Lessons 13-18): Learn specific musical elements that Western theory misses—tuning, rhythm, timbre
4. **Case studies** (Lessons 19-24): See it all in action—real traditions analyzed on their own terms
5. **Applied ethics** (Lessons 25-27): Grapple with contemporary issues—fieldwork, globalization, preservation

This sequence moves from **abstract concepts → cultural functions → musical specifics → real-world examples → contemporary challenges**.

## Misconception Warning Points

- **Lesson 3**: Students may think emic/etic is just "their words vs. our words" rather than fundamentally different knowledge systems
- **Lesson 4**: Students may think transcription is "incomplete" rather than "theoretically biased"
- **Lesson 10**: Students may think culturally-grounded analysis means "no criticism allowed"
- **Lesson 11**: Students may swing too far toward relativism ("everything is different") or universalism ("music is universal")
- **Lesson 13-17**: Students may exoticize non-Western systems rather than seeing them as equally valid approaches
- **Lesson 26**: Students may romanticize pre-globalization "authenticity" or dismiss all cultural hybridity as appropriation
