# Social Network Analysis — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Network thinking** — viewing systems as nodes (entities) and edges (relationships)
2. **Graph representation** — data structures for storing networks (adjacency matrix, edge list, adjacency list)
3. **Directed vs. undirected** — whether relationships have direction. Depends on: network thinking
4. **Weighted edges** — assigning strength/capacity to relationships. Depends on: graph representation
5. **Degree** — number of connections a node has. Depends on: graph representation
6. **Degree centrality** — simplest importance measure based on connection count. Depends on: degree
7. **Paths and distance** — sequences of edges connecting nodes, length of shortest path. Depends on: graph representation
8. **Betweenness centrality** — importance based on lying on shortest paths between others. Depends on: paths and distance, degree centrality
9. **Eigenvector centrality** — importance based on being connected to important nodes. Depends on: degree centrality, linear algebra
10. **PageRank** — Google's variant of eigenvector centrality with damping. Depends on: eigenvector centrality, random walks
11. **Clustering coefficient** — how clustered a node's neighbors are. Depends on: degree
12. **Triadic closure** — tendency for friends of friends to become friends. Depends on: clustering coefficient
13. **Small-world property** — high clustering + short average path length. Depends on: clustering coefficient, paths and distance
14. **Degree distribution** — probability distribution of node degrees. Depends on: degree
15. **Power laws** — degree distributions that follow P(k) ~ k^(-γ). Depends on: degree distribution
16. **Scale-free networks** — networks with power-law degree distribution. Depends on: power laws
17. **Connected components** — maximal connected subgraphs. Depends on: paths and distance
18. **Network resilience** — robustness to node/edge removal. Depends on: connected components, degree distribution
19. **Community structure** — groups of nodes more densely connected internally than externally. Depends on: clustering coefficient
20. **Modularity** — quality metric for community partitions. Depends on: community structure
21. **Community detection algorithms** — methods to find community structure (Louvain, label propagation, spectral). Depends on: modularity
22. **Random graphs** — networks generated by probabilistic rules (Erdős-Rényi). Depends on: degree distribution
23. **Preferential attachment** — new nodes connect to high-degree nodes ("rich get richer"). Depends on: degree, scale-free networks
24. **Network models** — generative models that reproduce real network properties (BA, WS, configuration model). Depends on: random graphs, preferential attachment
25. **Diffusion processes** — spread of information, disease, or behavior through networks. Depends on: paths and distance, network structure
26. **Cascades** — threshold-based spreading phenomena. Depends on: diffusion processes
27. **Temporal networks** — networks that change over time. Depends on: network models
28. **Link prediction** — predicting future edges based on current structure. Depends on: temporal networks, network models

## Dependencies

### Foundational Chain
- **Graph representation** is the foundation — all other concepts require understanding how to store and manipulate network data
- **Degree** builds directly on graph representation and is used everywhere (centrality, distributions, models)
- **Paths and distance** are fundamental for centrality measures (betweenness, closeness) and structure (small-world, components)

### Centrality Hierarchy
- **Degree centrality** is the simplest and serves as baseline for comparison
- **Betweenness centrality** requires understanding paths — it's computationally harder and captures flow/brokerage
- **Eigenvector centrality** requires linear algebra and recursive thinking (importance depends on others' importance)
- **PageRank** extends eigenvector centrality with teleportation to handle disconnected components and avoid sinks

### Structure Concepts
- **Clustering coefficient** measures local structure around individual nodes
- **Triadic closure** explains why clustering emerges in social networks
- **Small-world property** combines clustering (local) with short paths (global) — requires understanding both
- **Community structure** extends clustering to meso-scale groups
- **Modularity** quantifies community quality — needed before comparing detection algorithms

### Distribution and Models
- **Degree distribution** characterizes global network structure statistically
- **Power laws** are a specific distributional form common in real networks
- **Scale-free networks** are defined by power-law degree distributions
- **Random graphs** serve as null models for comparison
- **Preferential attachment** is a growth mechanism that generates scale-free networks
- **Network models** synthesize these concepts to generate realistic networks

### Dynamics
- **Diffusion processes** require understanding paths (how things spread) and structure (barriers/highways)
- **Cascades** add threshold behavior to diffusion
- **Temporal networks** extend static analysis to evolving structures
- **Link prediction** combines structure, dynamics, and models to forecast evolution

## Bottlenecks

### Linear Algebra (Eigenvector Centrality, PageRank, Spectral Methods)
Many students struggle with eigenvector centrality and spectral community detection without solid linear algebra. The connection between "importance depends on neighbors' importance" and "eigenvector of adjacency matrix" is not obvious. 

**Mitigation**: Use power iteration interpretation (random walk), visualize on small networks, emphasize intuition before formalism.

### Statistical Thinking (Degree Distributions, Power Laws)
Understanding why power laws matter and how to test for them requires comfort with distributions, log-log plots, and hypothesis testing.

**Mitigation**: Start with intuitive examples (city sizes, word frequencies), use visualization heavily, avoid heavy statistics initially.

### Algorithmic Complexity (Betweenness, Community Detection)
Many interesting network problems are computationally hard. Students need to understand why approximate/heuristic methods are necessary for large networks.

**Mitigation**: Start with small networks where brute force works, then introduce approximations when scaling up. Emphasize the insight-computation tradeoff.

### Dynamics and Causality
Moving from static structure to dynamic processes is conceptually hard. Students confuse correlation (network structure) with causation (how structure affects processes).

**Mitigation**: Use concrete examples (disease spread), run simulations, emphasize counterfactuals (what if the network were different?).

## Common Conceptual Confusions

### "High degree = most important"
Degree centrality is intuitive but often misleading. A node can have low degree but high betweenness (bridge). Students over-rely on degree.

### "Networks are all small-world"
Six degrees of separation is famous but doesn't apply to all networks. Fragmented networks, hierarchies, and spatial networks behave differently.

### "Power laws everywhere"
Students start seeing power laws in every degree distribution. Many real networks are not scale-free; testing requires rigor.

### "Community detection finds THE communities"
Communities are not unique — different algorithms, parameters, or resolutions give different results. Communities are exploratory, not ground truth.

### "Correlation = network effect"
Just because two connected nodes behave similarly doesn't mean the connection caused it (homophily vs. influence problem).

## Progressive Complexity

### Level 1: Foundations (Lessons 1-7)
Basic representations, simple metrics (degree), visualization. Students can construct networks and compute basic statistics.

### Level 2: Node Importance (Lessons 6-11)
Multiple centrality measures, interpretation, comparison. Students understand there are many definitions of "importance" and can choose appropriately.

### Level 3: Global Structure (Lessons 12-18)
Clustering, small-world, degree distributions, components. Students can characterize a network's macro properties and compare to others.

### Level 4: Mesoscale Organization (Lessons 19-23)
Community detection, modularity, algorithm comparison. Students can find and validate group structure.

### Level 5: Models and Dynamics (Lessons 24-28)
Generative models, diffusion, link prediction. Students can simulate networks, predict evolution, and understand processes on networks.

Each level builds on previous — can't understand PageRank without degree, can't model diffusion without understanding paths and communities.
