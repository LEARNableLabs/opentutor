# Weather Forecasting — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Cloud classification** — 10 cloud genera organized by altitude; basis for all cloud-based forecasting
2. **Atmospheric stability** — what determines whether air parcels rise or sink; explains cloud shapes
3. **Stratus/Stratocumulus/Cumulus** — low-level clouds; indicate current boundary layer conditions
4. **Altocumulus/Altostratus** — mid-level clouds; often signal approaching fronts. Depends on: cloud classification
5. **Cirrus/Cirrostratus/Cirrocumulus** — high-level ice clouds; precede warm fronts by 12-24 hours. Depends on: cloud classification
6. **Cumulonimbus** — towering convective clouds; produce severe weather. Depends on: atmospheric stability, cumulus
7. **Atmospheric pressure** — weight of air column; drives horizontal motion and vertical motion
8. **Isobars** — lines of equal pressure; reveal pressure gradients and wind patterns. Depends on: atmospheric pressure
9. **Anticyclones (high pressure)** — subsidence zones producing clear, stable weather. Depends on: atmospheric pressure
10. **Cyclones (low pressure)** — convergence zones producing lift, clouds, precipitation. Depends on: atmospheric pressure
11. **Cold fronts** — sharp boundary where cold air displaces warm air; rapid weather changes. Depends on: cyclones, cumulonimbus
12. **Warm fronts** — gradual boundary where warm air overruns cold air; stratiform clouds and steady precipitation. Depends on: cyclones, altostratus, cirrus
13. **Frontal lifting mechanisms** — how fronts force air upward to create clouds. Depends on: cold fronts, warm fronts, atmospheric stability
14. **Synoptic observation** — systematic multi-indicator weather monitoring (clouds, pressure, wind, etc.). Depends on: all prior cloud and pressure concepts
15. **Pressure tendency** — rate of pressure change over time; critical short-term forecasting tool. Depends on: atmospheric pressure, cyclones, anticyclones
16. **Cloud sequences** — predictable progression of cloud types before/during/after fronts. Depends on: cold fronts, warm fronts, all cloud types
17. **Wind patterns** — relationship between pressure gradients, Coriolis effect, and observed winds. Depends on: isobars, cyclones, anticyclones
18. **Geostrophic wind** — theoretical wind parallel to isobars; basis for Buys Ballot's law. Depends on: wind patterns
19. **Backing and veering** — directional wind shift patterns indicating front approach/passage. Depends on: wind patterns, fronts
20. **Precipitation types** — rain, snow, sleet, freezing rain determined by thermal profile. Depends on: cumulonimbus, stratiform clouds, fronts
21. **Nowcasting** — 0-2 hour forecasting using radar and current observations. Depends on: synoptic observation, radar interpretation
22. **Short-term forecasting (6-24 hours)** — synthesis of trends and pattern recognition. Depends on: cloud sequences, pressure tendency, wind patterns
23. **Numerical weather prediction (NWP)** — model guidance interpretation; complements observational forecasting. Depends on: short-term forecasting
24. **Forecast verification** — assessing skill and learning from errors. Depends on: all forecasting concepts
25. **Severe weather indicators** — cloud features (mammatus, wall clouds, shelf clouds) signaling dangerous convection. Depends on: cumulonimbus, atmospheric stability
26. **Integrated forecasting** — synthesizing clouds, pressure, wind, models, and uncertainty into actionable forecasts. Depends on: all prior concepts

## Critical Dependencies

### Why cloud classification comes first
Cloud identification is the most accessible entry point — anyone can look up and start learning. It builds observational discipline before introducing abstract pressure concepts.

### Why pressure follows clouds
Pressure systems explain *why* cloud patterns form and evolve. Students need concrete cloud examples before abstract pressure theory makes sense.

### Why fronts require both clouds and pressure
Fronts are the synthesis — they're pressure features (cyclones) that produce characteristic cloud sequences. You can't understand fronts without both foundations.

### Why forecasting is last
Forecasting requires integrating everything: recognizing cloud sequences, interpreting pressure tendencies, reading wind shifts, and assessing uncertainty. It's the performance layer.

## Bottleneck Concepts

### Atmospheric stability
This is the key to understanding *why* different cloud shapes form. If a student struggles here, use visual analogies (hot air balloons, convection in boiling water).

### Pressure tendency
Many students can read current pressure but struggle to interpret *change*. The 3-hour pressure tendency is often more valuable than absolute pressure — emphasize trends.

### Synthesis
The jump from "I can identify this cloud" to "I can forecast based on cloud sequences + pressure + wind" is large. Review days and real-world practice are essential.

## Common Misconception Triggers

1. **"Low clouds = low pressure"** — students often conflate altitude with pressure patterns. Clarify early.
2. **"Clear skies = high pressure everywhere"** — local clearing after a cold front passage doesn't mean a high has arrived yet.
3. **Oversimplifying cloud sequences** — not every warm front shows the textbook Ci → Cs → As → Ns progression.
4. **Forgetting pressure is relative** — what counts as "high" varies by region and season.
5. **Model worship** — students may over-rely on NWP and neglect observational skills.

## Prerequisite Topics (external)

- **Basic atmospheric science** — needed for: pressure, humidity, temperature concepts (all lessons)
- **Map reading** — needed for: isobars, surface analysis, wind vectors (lessons 7+)
- **Data interpretation** — needed for: METAR, radar, pressure tendency (lessons 13+)
- **Spatial reasoning** — needed for: 3D visualization of cloud layers and frontal boundaries (lessons 9+)

## Extension Topics (rabbit holes for motivated students)

- **Satellite meteorology** — interpreting visible, IR, and water vapor imagery
- **Mesoscale meteorology** — convective complexes, outflow boundaries, land-sea breezes
- **Mountain meteorology** — lee waves, föhn winds, orographic precipitation
- **Tropical meteorology** — hurricanes, tropical waves, monsoons
- **Climate patterns** — ENSO, NAO, blocking patterns
