# Constructed Languages — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Teaching constructed languages at the intermediate level requires balancing linguistic analysis with cultural appreciation. This topic is unique because it sits at the intersection of linguistics, philosophy, art, and community practice. Unlike natural language study, students can directly examine design decisions and their consequences. The pedagogy emphasizes **comparative analysis** — constantly asking "why did the creator choose X over Y?" and "what trade-offs does this create?" Use real conlang examples early and often; students should analyze actual Esperanto sentences, Toki Pona expressions, and Lojban logic structures, not just read about them abstractly. The topic is naturally hands-on and creative, so incorporate frequent "try it yourself" moments where students apply principles to mini-design challenges.

At the intermediate level, assume students have basic linguistics vocabulary (phoneme, morpheme, syntax) but may not have studied typology or historical linguistics deeply. Focus on **design thinking** rather than exhaustive linguistic formalism. The goal is not to memorize every Esperanto rule or become fluent in Lojban, but to understand the design space of language construction and make informed analytical and creative choices.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"All conlangs are like Esperanto"** — Students often assume all constructed languages are auxiliary languages designed for ease of learning. Reality: the vast majority are artistic (Tolkien, media conlangs) or experimental (Lojban, Ithkuil). Correct this early in Lesson 1-4 by showing the diversity of purposes and approaches.

2. **"Conlangs are inherently simpler than natural languages"** — Many assume constructed languages are "dumbed down." Counter-example: Lojban and Ithkuil are far more complex than most natural languages. Regularity ≠ simplicity. Address explicitly in Lesson 15 when introducing Lojban.

3. **"A posteriori means 'easy to learn'"** — Students confuse "based on existing languages" with "easy." Interlingua may look familiar to Romance speakers but is still a full language to learn. Clarify in Lessons 10-13 that familiarity helps but doesn't eliminate learning effort.

4. **"Toki Pona is just 120 words, so it's trivial"** — The minimalism is actually cognitively demanding because you must constantly construct meaning through composition. "jan pona mi" could mean "my friend," "my ally," "good person of mine," etc. Emphasize in Lesson 14 that simplicity in inventory creates complexity in usage.

5. **"Logic languages eliminate all ambiguity"** — Students think Lojban has no ambiguity, but semantic ambiguity still exists (is "blue-green" a blend or a choice?). Only syntactic ambiguity is eliminated. Nuance this in Lesson 15.

6. **"Artistic conlangs are just random sounds"** — Many underestimate the sophistication of Tolkien's linguistic work or Peterson's Dothraki. These languages follow rigorous typological patterns and historical evolution rules. Counter in Lessons 19-20 with concrete analysis of their systematic nature.

7. **"Naturalism is just adding irregular verbs"** — Students think making a language "natural" means randomly breaking rules. Reality: naturalism involves systematic historical change, typological plausibility, and cultural embedding. Address in Lesson 21 with specific patterns.

8. **"There's a 'best' conlang"** — Students often want to rank languages (Esperanto vs Lojban vs Toki Pona). Emphasize purpose-driven design: the best language is the one that achieves its goals. Esperanto excels as a practical auxlang; Toki Pona excels as a philosophical/artistic minimalist project; Lojban excels as a logical experiment. Reinforce throughout but especially in Lessons 23-25.

9. **"You need to speak a conlang for it to be 'real'"** — Some students dismiss conlangs with few or no speakers. Counter: a language can be a complete, well-documented system without a speech community. Artistic languages like Quenya are "real" linguistic systems. Address if it comes up.

10. **"Conlanging is a modern internet phenomenon"** — Students are often surprised that language construction dates back to the 12th century (Hildegard of Bingen's Lingua Ignota) and had major efforts in the 17th-19th centuries. Provide historical context in Lesson 2.

## Level Adjustments

### Intermediate Level (Current)
- **Assume**: Basic linguistics (phoneme, morpheme, syntax), familiarity with at least one Indo-European language
- **Emphasize**: Comparative analysis, design trade-offs, hands-on analysis of real conlangs
- **Formalism level**: Medium — use linguistic terminology correctly but don't require deep technical knowledge of feature geometry or optimality theory
- **Depth**: Cover major conlang types with 1-2 deep dives each (Esperanto, Toki Pona, Lojban, Quenya, Dothraki)
- **Creation component**: Basic phonology and vocabulary design, not full grammar

### If Adapting to Beginner Level
- Start with more linguistics 101 content (what is a phoneme? what is morphology?)
- Focus on just 2-3 conlangs (Esperanto, Toki Pona, maybe Quenya)
- Skip or simplify Lojban (too technical)
- More scaffolding on linguistic terminology
- Simpler creation tasks (just design an alphabet or 20-word vocabulary)

### If Adapting to Advanced Level
- Assume typological knowledge and historical linguistics background
- Go deep into formal properties (Lojban's PEG grammar, Ithkuil's morphology)
- Include more conlangs (Láadan, Kēlen, Spokil, Solresol)
- Require reading primary sources (Fundamento, Lojban CLL, Peterson's work)
- Creation component: full phonology + morphology + basic syntax + 200-word lexicon
- Add sociolinguistic angle: conlang communities, language evolution in practice

## Rabbit Holes

### Fascinating Connections (drop these in when momentum is high)

- **The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis and Toki Pona** (Lesson 14) — does speaking a minimalist language change how you think? Sonja Lang's philosophical intentions connect to linguistic relativity debates. Great for students interested in philosophy of language.

- **Tolkien's professional linguistics work** (Lesson 19) — he wasn't just a hobbyist; he was a philologist who studied Gothic, Finnish, and Welsh professionally. His conlangs reflect deep knowledge of historical language change. Show how Quenya → Sindarin mirrors real sound shifts.

- **The Klingon Language Institute** (Lesson 3 or 20) — a real organization that has translated Hamlet, the Epic of Gilgamesh, and parts of the Bible into Klingon. Fascinating case study in what happens when an artistic language gains serious speakers.

- **Blissymbolics and AAC** (anytime after Lesson 4) — constructed language that became a communication system for non-speaking people. Shows practical applications beyond auxlangs.

- **The failed Volapük movement** (Lesson 10) — Esperanto's predecessor, which was initially more popular but collapsed due to internal politics and complexity. Great cautionary tale about design choices and community management.

- **Sound symbolism across languages** (Lesson 19 or 21) — the bouba/kiki effect and how conlangers consciously use phonetic associations. Quenya sounds "flowing" because of liquids; Klingon sounds "harsh" because of uvulars.

- **The Language Creation Conference** (Lesson 23) — annual academic gathering of conlangers. Shows the serious scholarship side of what some dismiss as a hobby.

- **Constructed scripts** (anytime) — if a student shows interest, explore Tengwar (Tolkien), Klingon pIqaD, Lojban's experimental logography. Visual dimension adds richness.

- **Music and conlangs** (Lesson 19-22) — Solresol (musical language), singing in Quenya (Enya, Howard Shore's LOTR soundtrack). Multi-modal language design.

- **Pidgins and creoles as "natural conlangs"** (Lesson 21) — when does a constructed language become "natural"? Esperanto's denaskuloj (native speakers) blur the line.

## Difficulty Progression

### Arc Overview
The curriculum follows a **wave pattern** with difficulty rising and falling:

- **Lessons 1-4**: Easy entry (difficulty 1-2) — motivation, history, basic classification
- **Lessons 5-9**: First peak (difficulty 2-3) — linguistic foundations (hardest conceptual work)
- **Lesson 6**: Review dip (difficulty 1)
- **Lessons 10-13**: Moderate (difficulty 2-3) — Esperanto is accessible, helps consolidate linguistic concepts
- **Lesson 12**: Review dip (difficulty 1)
- **Lessons 14-16**: Second peak (difficulty 3-4) — Toki Pona and Lojban are cognitively demanding
- **Lesson 17**: Review dip (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 19-21**: Third peak (difficulty 2-4) — Artistic languages, naturalism is subtle
- **Lesson 22**: Review dip (difficulty 2)
- **Lessons 23-25**: Applied synthesis (difficulty 3-4) — creative work is challenging but motivating

### Pacing Notes
- **Lessons 7-9** (morphology, syntax, semantics) are the first bottleneck — expect students to need extra time here
- **Lesson 15** (Lojban) is the hardest single lesson — consider splitting if students struggle
- **Lesson 21** (naturalism) is conceptually subtle — requires comparing all previous examples
- **Lessons 24-25** are high difficulty but high engagement (creative work feels different from passive learning)

### Adjustment Strategies
- If students struggle with Lessons 7-9: add concrete examples from Esperanto or English, use visual diagrams
- If Lojban (Lesson 15) is too hard: focus on the conceptual goal (unambiguous syntax) and skip the technical details
- If students breeze through: add challenges like translating complex sentences, comparing more conlangs, deeper creation project

## Wild Cards

- **Celebrity conlangers** — mention David Peterson (Dothraki, High Valyrian), Marc Okrand (Klingon), Paul Frommer (Na'vi), Christine Schreyer (Kryptonian) if students are interested in the professional side
- **Conlang Relay** — annual community event where a text is translated through a chain of conlangs like telephone. Fun example of the community.
- **r/conlangs challenges** — point students to the subreddit's biweekly challenges if they want ongoing practice
- **Language learning stats** — Duolingo reported over 2 million Esperanto learners. Show that these aren't just academic curiosities.
