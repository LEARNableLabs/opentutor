# Stained Glass — Teaching Notes

## Approach

Stained glass is a highly **tactile and visual craft** where muscle memory and material intuition matter as much as conceptual understanding. At intermediate level, students have basic copper foil experience but often lack formal lead came training. The teaching approach should emphasize **comparative learning** (when to use which technique), **hands-on troubleshooting** (why did this solder joint fail?), and **progressive complexity** (start with flat panels, build to 3D forms).

Use visual resources heavily — this craft is difficult to learn from text alone. Encourage students to photograph their work at each stage and reflect on what went well vs what needs improvement. Mistakes in stained glass are often irreversible (can't unsolder cleanly, can't remove cement), so emphasize careful preparation and "measure twice, cut once" thinking.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Lead came is just old-fashioned copper foil"** — Students often think lead came is an outdated technique that copper foil replaced. In reality, lead came is structurally superior for large architectural panels, has a distinct aesthetic appropriate for traditional designs, and is the standard for historical restoration. Copper foil enables intricate 3D work that lead cannot match. They are complementary, not competitive.

2. **"More solder makes stronger joints"** — Beginners often "glob" solder thinking it increases strength. In reality, a thin, properly adhered bead is stronger than a thick, poorly flowed one. Excess solder looks amateur and adds unnecessary weight. Teach that solder strength comes from proper flux, heat, and flow — not volume.

3. **"You can skip stretching lead came if you're careful"** — Students underestimate how much unstretched lead compresses during assembly. Skipping this step results in loose, rattling panels that will fail structurally. The misconception stems from not understanding that lead is extruded with internal stresses that must be relieved.

4. **"Flux is flux — they're all the same"** — Different fluxes (liquid, paste, gel) have different activation temperatures, residue characteristics, and corrosiveness. Using the wrong flux causes cold joints, corrosion, or difficult cleanup. Students coming from electronics soldering often bring inappropriate flux assumptions.

5. **"Copper foil width doesn't matter much"** — Students often grab whatever foil is convenient. Too-narrow foil creates weak joints; too-wide foil bunches and creates lumpy seams. The width must match glass thickness. This is a precision detail that seems minor but has major quality impact.

6. **"Patina is just cosmetic"** — Students treat patina as optional decoration. In reality, patina chemically converts the solder surface, provides corrosion resistance, and is essential for professional finishing. Skipping it or applying it poorly causes tarnishing and degradation.

7. **"You should always use black patina"** — Many beginners default to black patina for everything because it's common. Copper patina better complements warm-toned glass; black suits cool tones and formal designs. Design-appropriate patina choice is part of finishing skill.

8. **"Lead came and copper foil can't be mixed"** — Students assume techniques are mutually exclusive. Advanced work often combines them strategically (e.g., copper foil for intricate center elements, lead came for structural border). The challenge is managing different thermal properties and solder flow characteristics.

## Level Adjustments

### Beginner → Intermediate Transition
Beginners typically know only copper foil (sun catchers, small ornaments). This curriculum assumes that foundation and builds lead came from zero. If a student has *no* copper foil experience, they should complete 2-3 small copper foil projects before starting this intermediate curriculum.

### Intermediate Focus (This Curriculum)
- Formalize technique selection (stop guessing, start analyzing)
- Introduce lead came systematically
- Elevate soldering from "functional" to "professional quality"
- Add structural thinking (reinforcement, large panels, installation)
- Introduce 3D construction and hybrid techniques

Skip: window restoration chemistry, advanced beveling, glass painting, kiln work, historical authentication.

### Advanced Extensions
If a student breezes through, add:
- Architectural installation and code compliance
- Restoration techniques (historical accuracy, reversible repairs)
- Glass painting and staining
- Acid etching and sandblasting
- Dalle de verre (concrete and glass) technique
- Commission workflow and pricing

## Rabbit Holes

- **Glass chemistry and manufacturing** — Why does opalescent glass behave differently than cathedral glass? Trace it to the manufacturing process and chemical composition. Great for students interested in materials science.

- **Medieval cathedral windows** — How did 12th-century craftspeople build structurally sound 40-foot windows without modern tools? Dive into historical engineering, ferramenta (armatures), and deterioration science. Connects to art history and architecture.

- **Tiffany's business empire** — Louis Comfort Tiffany didn't just invent copper foil — he industrialized luxury glass production. Explore the intersection of craft, art, and capitalism. Fascinating for students interested in design business.

- **Light and color physics** — Why do stained glass windows look different from inside vs outside? Why does time of day matter? Deep dive into transmission vs reflection, glass color mixing, and architectural lighting. Connects to optics and physics.

- **The glass waste problem** — Professional studios generate significant scrap glass. Explore recycling techniques, mosaic reuse, and sustainability in craft. Relevant for environmentally conscious students.

- **Therapeutic uses of stained glass** — Why is stained glass popular in art therapy and mindfulness programs? Explore the psychology of repetitive craft, color therapy, and meditative making.

## Difficulty Progression

The curriculum follows a **compare → separate mastery → integration** arc:

1. **Lessons 1-5** (Difficulty 1-2): Establish foundations, compare techniques, pattern skills
2. **Lessons 6-11** (Difficulty 2-4): Deep dive into lead came, peak difficulty at lesson 8-9 (joinery and soldering)
3. **Lesson 11** (Difficulty 2): Review and consolidation
4. **Lessons 12-17** (Difficulty 2-4): Deep dive into copper foil, peak difficulty at lesson 15-16 (advanced soldering and 3D construction)
5. **Lesson 17** (Difficulty 1): Review and consolidation
6. **Lessons 18-23** (Difficulty 2-4): Advanced integration, peak difficulty at lesson 22 (hybrid techniques)
7. **Lesson 23** (Difficulty 2): Final review and synthesis

Reviews are placed strategically after each major technique section. If a student struggles during a peak difficulty lesson (8-9, 15-16, 22), pause and insert an extra practice session before the review.

## Troubleshooting Common Struggles

- **Struggle: Uneven solder beads** → Return to heat control fundamentals, iron temperature, and solder feed rate. Often caused by moving too fast.
- **Struggle: Lead came joints won't stay tight** → Check if lead was stretched. If yes, check for accurate cutting angles. If yes, check glass fit tolerance.
- **Struggle: Copper foil lifting during soldering** → Burnishing failure. Re-teach proper edge preparation and burnishing pressure.
- **Struggle: Patina won't adhere** → Flux residue or oils on surface. Re-teach cleaning and neutralization.
- **Struggle: Panel is wobbly/weak** → Structural design flaw. Return to reinforcement concepts and span calculations.

## Engagement Strategies

- **Show-and-tell**: Ask students to photograph finished pieces and share what they learned from mistakes
- **Design challenges**: "Design a panel that *requires* lead came" or "Design something impossible with lead came"
- **Historical analysis**: Analyze a Tiffany lamp or cathedral window — what technique choices do you see and why?
- **Failure analysis**: Bring a failed joint/panel — diagnose what went wrong and how to prevent it
- **Resource curation**: Students find and share video tutorials or tools they discovered
