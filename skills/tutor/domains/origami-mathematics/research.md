# Origami Mathematics — Research Summary

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Origami Axioms
The mathematical foundation of origami is captured in 7 axioms that describe all possible single-fold operations. The first 6 axioms were formalized by Jacques Justin (1986) and independently rediscovered by Humiaki Huzita (1989). The 7th axiom was added by Koshiro Hatori (2001). These axioms enable constructions more powerful than classical compass-and-straightedge geometry, including angle trisection and cube duplication.

### 2. Geometric Constructions
Origami can solve classical impossibility problems: trisecting angles, doubling cubes, and solving cubic equations. Axiom O6 is particularly powerful, enabling constructions that cannot be achieved with Euclidean tools.

### 3. Flat-Foldability Theory
Mathematical conditions that determine whether a crease pattern can be folded flat. Includes Maekawa's theorem (difference between mountain and valley folds), Kawasaki's theorem (alternating angle sums), and the analysis of valid crease patterns.

### 4. Origami Design Algorithms
Computational methods for designing origami models, including:
- Circle packing method
- Tree method (Robert Lang)
- Base folding and grafting techniques
- Computational optimization for complex forms

### 5. Modular and Polyhedral Origami
Mathematical analysis of polyhedra constructed from multiple folded units. Geometry of edge units, vertex configurations, and structural stability.

### 6. Applications
Real-world applications in architecture (deployable structures), robotics (reconfigurable systems), manufacturing (sheet metal folding), aerospace (solar panel deployment), and biology (protein folding models).

## Key Academic Sources

### Textbooks
- **Origametry: Mathematical Methods in Paper Folding** by Thomas Hull (Cambridge University Press, 2020) — comprehensive treatment of origami mathematics, accessible to undergraduates
- **Geometric Folding Algorithms: Linkages, Origami, Polyhedra** by Erik Demaine and Joseph O'Rourke (Cambridge, 2007) — algorithmic and computational emphasis, graduate-level
- **Project Origami: Activities for Exploring Mathematics** by Thomas Hull (A K Peters, 2012) — hands-on activities

### University Courses
- **MIT 6.849: Geometric Folding Algorithms** — graduate course covering linkages, origami, and polyhedra with extensive lecture materials and videos
- **MAMT 590: Origami in Mathematics and Education** (Thomas Hull, Western New England University) — mathematical methods and pedagogical applications
- **UNC MATH 69: Math, Origami, & Fractals** — undergraduate exploration of geometric tessellations and symmetry

### Online Resources
- Thomas Hull's Origami Mathematics Page (http://origametry.net/origamimath.html) — bibliography, tutorials, course notes
- Robert Lang's website (langorigami.com) — origami design theory, TreeMaker software
- MIT OpenCourseWare materials (ocw.mit.edu) — full course lectures, notes, assignments
- gfalop.org — supplementary materials for Demaine & O'Rourke textbook

### Key Papers and Documents
- Jacques Justin's 1986 axiomatization (French)
- Robert Lang's papers on computational origami design
- Academic theses on flat-foldability and algorithmic design
- Research on applications to engineering and biology

## Prerequisites
- High school geometry (angles, triangles, circles, polygons)
- Basic algebra (solving equations, systems of equations)
- Trigonometry (sine, cosine, angle relationships)
- Optional but helpful: computational thinking, basic proofs

## Assessment of Material Depth
This is a moderately specialized topic suitable for intermediate learners with strong geometric intuition. The material ranges from accessible (basic axioms, simple constructions) to challenging (algorithmic design, rigorous proofs of flat-foldability theorems). Approximately 24-26 lessons can cover core theory, constructions, and applications while maintaining engagement through hands-on folding exercises.
