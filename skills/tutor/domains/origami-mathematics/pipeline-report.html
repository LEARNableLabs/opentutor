Audience Profile

Audience Type
STEM undergraduates, mathematically curious professionals (engineers, designers, educators), and serious self-taught learners with solid quantitative foundations. The common thread is comfort with mathematical reasoning and genuine curiosity about the geometry of folding -- not exam pressure or research deadlines.
Goal
Build working fluency in origami mathematics: understand, state, and apply the core theorems (Kawasaki-Justin, Maekawa) and axioms (Huzita-Justin), reason about flat-foldability and geometric constructibility, and connect paper-folding to broader mathematical structures. The student should finish able to read primary literature, use computational tools, and recognize origami-mathematical structure in engineering applications.
Background
Comfortable with algebra, coordinate geometry, and trigonometry. Some exposure to proof-based mathematics -- at minimum one college-level course in discrete math, linear algebra, or an intro-to-proofs course. Familiarity with basic calculus helpful but not a hard prerequisite. No prior origami technique required. No programming background assumed.
Tone
Hands-on and example-driven, with formal foundations introduced through physical intuition. Every theorem should first be discovered through folding before being stated precisely. Rigorous where it matters, conversational enough to sustain daily self-study. Rabbit holes encouraged.
Depth
Intuition plus key proofs, with applied grounding. Build geometric intuition through physical folding, then formalize with precise theorem statements and selected complete proofs. Provide proof sketches for harder results. Include computational exercises and hands-on folding tasks. Stops short of the full graduate treatment.
Time Commitment
10-15 minutes study + 10-15 minutes exercises. Some lessons add 5-10 minutes for hands-on folding. Total per lesson: 25-40 minutes, consistent with the 1% daily growth rule.
Summary
The target student has solid high-school mathematics and at least introductory exposure to proof-based reasoning. They are drawn to origami mathematics by intellectual curiosity or cross-disciplinary interest, not by exam deadlines. The course builds geometric intuition through physical folding, formalizes it with precise theorems, and connects the mathematics to real-world applications and computational tools.
