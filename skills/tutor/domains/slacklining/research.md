# Slacklining Physics and Proprioception — Research Summary

## Major Subtopics

### 1. Physical Mechanics
- **Static forces and tension** — catenary curves, force distribution across the line, anchor loads
- **Dynamic motion** — oscillations, damping, resonance frequencies, how body weight creates standing waves
- **Material properties** — webbing elasticity, stretch ratios, how different materials (nylon, polyester, dyneema) affect dynamics
- **Rigging physics** — mechanical advantage systems, safety factors, load calculations

### 2. Biomechanics and Body Mechanics
- **Center of mass control** — how small shifts in COM position stabilize or destabilize the system
- **Joint mechanics** — ankle/knee/hip articulation patterns, moment arms, torque distribution
- **Muscle activation patterns** — synergist and antagonist coordination, reflexive vs. voluntary control
- **Gait patterns on dynamic surfaces** — how walking/turning on a slackline differs from ground locomotion

### 3. Proprioception and Sensory Integration
- **Proprioceptive system** — muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, joint receptors
- **Vestibular integration** — inner ear signals, head position tracking, conflict resolution
- **Visual reliance vs. proprioceptive training** — how beginners over-rely on vision, how experts use proprioception
- **Sensory reweighting** — how the brain dynamically adjusts sensory weightings on unstable surfaces
- **Feedback loops** — predictive control vs. reactive correction, latency effects

### 4. Learning and Motor Control
- **Skill acquisition stages** — cognitive, associative, autonomous phases in slacklining
- **Practice variability** — how different line tensions, heights, and lengths affect learning transfer
- **Error-based learning** — how falls and wobbles drive motor adaptation
- **Mental models** — how experts develop internal simulations of line behavior

## Topic Scope Assessment

This is a **focused interdisciplinary topic** that combines:
- Engineering/physics (mechanics, materials)
- Kinesiology (biomechanics, motor control)
- Neuroscience (sensory systems, motor learning)

For an **intermediate student**, we assume:
- Basic physics background (forces, vectors, energy)
- General understanding of human anatomy and movement
- Some prior balance training or movement practice experience
- Curiosity about the underlying mechanisms, not just technique

The curriculum should bridge practical experience with theoretical understanding — helping practitioners understand WHY techniques work and HOW to optimize their training based on physical and neurological principles.

**Recommended lesson count: 25 lessons** (focused but with good depth across physics, biomechanics, and neuroscience dimensions)
