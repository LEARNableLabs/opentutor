# Sociology of Technology — Concept Map

## Core Concepts (in learning order)

1. **Technological determinism** — the view that technology develops independently and shapes society
2. **Social shaping** — the view that social factors determine technological development
3. **Mutual shaping** — technologies and societies co-evolve. Depends on: technological determinism, social shaping
4. **Politics of technology** — technologies embody values and power relations. Depends on: social shaping
5. **Values in design** — designers' choices encode particular worldviews. Depends on: politics of technology
6. **Relevant social groups** — groups that share meaning of an artifact. Foundation for SCOT
7. **Interpretive flexibility** — different groups see different artifacts in the same technology. Depends on: relevant social groups
8. **Technological frame** — shared assumptions within a social group. Depends on: relevant social groups
9. **Closure** — when interpretive flexibility ends and design stabilizes. Depends on: interpretive flexibility, relevant social groups
10. **Sociotechnical systems** — inseparable webs of technical and social elements. Depends on: mutual shaping
11. **SCOT methodology** — framework analyzing technology through social groups. Depends on: relevant social groups, interpretive flexibility, closure
12. **Actor-Network Theory** — framework treating humans and non-humans symmetrically. Depends on: sociotechnical systems
13. **Actants** — any entity (human or non-human) that makes a difference. Depends on: Actor-Network Theory
14. **Translation** — how actors enroll others into networks. Depends on: Actor-Network Theory, actants
15. **Obligatory passage points** — required nodes in a network. Depends on: translation
16. **Large Technical Systems** — interconnected sociotechnical infrastructures. Depends on: sociotechnical systems
17. **Technological momentum** — established systems resist change. Depends on: Large Technical Systems
18. **Path dependence** — early choices constrain future options. Depends on: technological momentum
19. **Gender and technology** — how gender shapes and is shaped by technologies. Depends on: politics of technology, values in design
20. **Appropriate technology** — context-sensitive design for development. Depends on: values in design, social shaping
21. **Technological controversy** — public disputes over technological risks. Depends on: politics of technology, relevant social groups
22. **Standardization** — creating uniform categories and protocols. Depends on: closure, Large Technical Systems
23. **Infrastructure** — background systems enabling action. Depends on: Large Technical Systems, standardization
24. **Algorithmic governance** — automated decision-making systems. Depends on: politics of technology, values in design
25. **Surveillance capitalism** — commodification of behavioral data. Depends on: algorithmic governance, power relations
26. **Algorithmic bias** — systematic discrimination in automated systems. Depends on: algorithmic governance, values in design
27. **Platform capitalism** — economic model based on digital intermediation. Depends on: surveillance capitalism, infrastructure
28. **Digital labor** — work mediated by platforms. Depends on: platform capitalism
29. **Participatory design** — democratic technology development. Depends on: values in design, relevant social groups
30. **Technology assessment** — systematic evaluation of social impacts. Depends on: technological controversy, participatory design

## Dependencies

**Foundational divide:**
- **Technological determinism vs. social shaping** is the starting point — all other concepts take a position on this debate
- **Mutual shaping** synthesizes them and enables most contemporary frameworks

**Framework dependencies:**
- **SCOT** requires understanding relevant social groups, interpretive flexibility, and closure
- **ANT** requires understanding actants, translation, and symmetry (treating humans and non-humans alike)
- **LTS** builds on sociotechnical systems and adds momentum and path dependence

**Historical to contemporary:**
- **Contemporary issues** (lessons 20-24) depend on understanding politics of technology, values in design, and infrastructure from earlier modules
- **Algorithmic governance** brings Winner's "politics of artifacts" into the digital age
- **Surveillance capitalism** and **platform capitalism** build on LTS concepts (momentum, infrastructure) applied to digital systems

**Bottlenecks:**
- **Relevant social groups** (lesson 3) — students must grasp this to understand SCOT
- **Sociotechnical systems** (lesson 5) — foundation for most frameworks
- **Actants and symmetry** (lesson 7) — conceptual leap required for ANT; often challenging
- **Translation** (lesson 8) — ANT's core mechanism; abstract until applied to cases

**Synthesis:**
- **Participatory design** and **technology assessment** (lessons 25-26) integrate multiple frameworks into practical interventions

## Prerequisite Topics

- **Basic sociology concepts** — needed for: social groups, power relations, institutions
- **Social theory exposure** — helpful for: Actor-Network Theory, structuration, power
- **Critical thinking** — needed for: identifying assumptions, analyzing cases, critiquing frameworks

## Common Learning Pathways

1. **Determinism → SCOT → ANT** — progressive refinement of how to analyze technology-society relations
2. **Winner's bridges → Contemporary algorithmic bias** — tracing politics of artifacts across eras
3. **Bicycle case → Student's own case study** — applying SCOT methodology
4. **Historical cases → Contemporary platforms** — recognizing recurring patterns

## Key Misconceptions to Address

- **Technological determinism is always wrong** — some technological properties do constrain social choices (not pure social construction)
- **SCOT and ANT are the same** — SCOT focuses on social groups; ANT eliminates human/non-human boundary
- **ANT denies human agency** — it redistributes agency, doesn't eliminate it
- **All technologies are neutral** — design choices embed values, even if unintentionally
- **Contemporary issues are unprecedented** — many patterns recur (e.g., telephone debates echo social media debates)
