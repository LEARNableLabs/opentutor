# Sociology of Technology — Teaching Notes

## Approach

This topic is **case-driven and theoretical**. Each framework (SCOT, ANT, LTS) makes most sense when applied to concrete examples — the bicycle, the telephone, nuclear power plants. At the intermediate level, we balance abstraction with application: introduce concepts through cases, then ask students to apply frameworks to new technologies. The field is naturally interdisciplinary, connecting sociology, history, philosophy, and policy, so expect rabbit holes into each. Encourage them.

Contemporary issues (lessons 20-24) feel urgent and relevant but require the theoretical scaffolding from earlier lessons. Resist the temptation to jump straight to algorithms and platforms — students need SCOT and ANT vocabulary first to analyze them rigorously.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **"Technology shapes society" is wrong, "society shapes technology" is right** — Students often overcorrect from determinism to pure social construction. The truth is mutual shaping: technologies create affordances and constraints while being designed through social processes. Use Latour's door-closer example or Winner's bridges to show how both are true simultaneously.

2. **SCOT and ANT are interchangeable** — SCOT focuses on relevant social groups (humans) and their interpretations. ANT treats humans and non-humans symmetrically as actants. They're compatible but distinct. Students often blur them. Emphasize: SCOT asks "which groups?"; ANT asks "which actors (human and non-human)?"

3. **ANT eliminates human agency** — Students worry that if non-humans are actors, humans lose responsibility. Clarify: ANT *redistributes* agency across networks; it doesn't deny human choice. A speed bump shapes driving behavior, but humans still built it and can remove it.

4. **All technologies are politically neutral until used** — Winner's core argument is that *artifacts themselves* can embody political properties. Moses' bridges weren't neutral infrastructure weaponized later — they were designed with exclusion in mind. Contemporary example: facial recognition systems that don't work well on darker skin aren't just "misused" — the training data choices and design decisions are the politics.

5. **Technological momentum means inevitability** — Students confuse momentum with determinism. Yes, established systems resist change (QWERTY, power grids), but they're not unchangeable. Emphasize: momentum raises the cost of change; it doesn't eliminate agency.

6. **SCOT only applies to historical cases** — Students see SCOT as "the bicycle method" and think it's for finished technologies. Show how interpretive flexibility exists in emerging technologies (AI, cryptocurrency, synthetic biology) — different groups still see radically different artifacts.

7. **Surveillance capitalism is just 'tech companies are bad'** — Zuboff's argument is structural, not moral. It's about a new form of capitalism, not individual corporate ethics. Help students distinguish critique of a socioeconomic system from critique of particular actors.

8. **Appropriate technology means 'simple is better'** — Students often reduce appropriate technology to "low-tech for poor countries." It means *context-appropriate*, which might be high-tech in some settings. Focus on fit with social context, not a hierarchy of complexity.

## Level Adjustments

**For intermediate students:**
- **Emphasize frameworks over history** — Give enough historical context to understand cases, but don't get lost in historiography. The goal is applying SCOT/ANT/LTS, not memorizing dates.
- **Use contemporary cases students care about** — Smartphones, social media, AI, climate tech. Let them bring examples from their worlds.
- **Moderate formalism** — Introduce key terms (actant, translation, closure) but don't require encyclopedic vocabulary. Focus on analytical moves, not jargon.
- **Expect critical engagement** — Intermediate students should critique frameworks, not just apply them. "When does SCOT work well? When does it miss things?" Push them to compare.

**What to skip/skim (save for advanced):**
- Detailed philosophy of science debates (realism vs. constructivism)
- Technical details of laboratory studies
- Full genealogy of STS disciplinary history
- Highly abstract Latour (e.g., *Pandora's Hope*)

**What to emphasize:**
- Applying frameworks to real cases (lessons 11, 18, 25)
- Connecting historical patterns to contemporary tech (lessons 15→21, 2→20, 13→22)
- Building case study analysis skills
- Recognizing when determinism or pure social construction is inadequate

## Rabbit Holes

- **Bruno Latour's door-closer essay** — drop in when discussing ANT. It's playful, accessible, and shows how mundane technologies distribute action. Students love it.

- **Winner's bridges controversy** — some historians dispute whether Moses actually designed low bridges to exclude buses. Use this as a teaching moment: even if the case is debated, the *concept* (artifacts can have politics) stands. What other examples fit?

- **QWERTY vs. Dvorak keyboard** — classic path dependence case. Was QWERTY really inferior? Economics literature debates this. Great for showing how STS intersects with economics.

- **Do Non-Humans Have Agency?** — when students struggle with ANT, push on agency. Does a speed bump "have" agency? Does a gun? This gets philosophical fast, but it clarifies what symmetry means. Link to contemporary debates on autonomous weapons and self-driving cars.

- **The politics of algorithms** — after lesson 20, students will want to dive deep on specific cases (facial recognition, predictive policing, content moderation). Let them. These are SCOT/ANT cases in real time.

- **Feminist STS** — Donna Haraway, Helen Longino, Lucy Suchman, Judy Wajcman. If the student is engaged with gender and technology (lesson 13), there's a massive literature. Haraway's "Situated Knowledges" and "Cyborg Manifesto" are canonical.

- **Science and Technology Studies (STS) vs. Sociology of Technology** — students may ask about disciplinary boundaries. STS is broader (includes science, medicine, environment); sociology of technology is a subset. Don't worry about policing boundaries — ideas move freely.

- **Technology and the Environment** — if interested, connect LTS (lesson 9) to energy systems, climate tech, and green transitions. Literature on sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff) and energy transitions (Geels) fits here.

- **Decolonial technology studies** — emerging area examining how coloniality shapes technology design and deployment. Connects to appropriate technology (lesson 14) and inequality (lesson 22). Authors: Sareeta Amrute, Arturo Escobar, Yuk Hui.

## Difficulty Progression

- **Lessons 1-5 (Foundations)** — Accessible entry. Difficulty 2-3. Big ideas, concrete examples.
- **Lessons 6-11 (Frameworks)** — Conceptual peak. Difficulty 3-4. ANT (lessons 7-8) is the hardest conceptual climb. SCOT is easier.
- **Lesson 12** — Review, drop to difficulty 2. Students should consolidate frameworks before moving to cases.
- **Lessons 13-18 (Historical Cases)** — Application phase. Difficulty 3-4, but different kind of challenge — less abstract, more analytical.
- **Lesson 19** — Review again before contemporary issues.
- **Lessons 20-24 (Contemporary Issues)** — High engagement, high difficulty (4). Students care deeply but must resist simplistic takes. Algorithms, surveillance, inequality, platforms — all require nuanced analysis.
- **Lessons 25-26 (Synthesis)** — Apply everything. Difficulty 3-4. Designing interventions is hard but rewarding.

## Delivery Tips

- **Use visuals for ANT** — Draw networks on whiteboards/diagrams. Who/what are the nodes? What are the connections? ANT is spatial.
- **Debate format for lesson 1** — Set up "technology shapes society" vs. "society shapes technology" as a debate, then show how both are too simple.
- **Case study workshops** — Lessons 11, 18, 25 are hands-on. Give students a technology (TikTok, Zoom, ChatGPT) and ask: map the relevant social groups (SCOT), draw the actor-network (ANT), identify the politics (Winner).
- **Contemporary readings** — For lessons 20-24, pull recent news articles, not just academic papers. Algorithmic bias, platform labor, surveillance — these are live issues. Connect theory to headlines.
- **Guest voices** — If possible, bring in practitioners (engineers, policymakers, activists) to discuss lesson 25 topics. STS is applied.

## Success Signals

Students are getting it when they:
- Stop asking "is this technology good or bad?" and start asking "for whom, in what context, with what trade-offs?"
- Apply SCOT to technologies in their own lives without prompting
- Recognize that design choices are social and political, not purely technical
- See historical cases as analogies for contemporary debates, not just trivia
- Push back on frameworks — "SCOT works here, but misses X" — rather than treating them as dogma
- Connect different frameworks: "This is an ANT answer, but a SCOT analysis would highlight..."
