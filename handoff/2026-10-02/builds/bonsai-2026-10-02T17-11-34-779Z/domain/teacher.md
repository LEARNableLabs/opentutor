# Bonsai: Domain Teaching Config

## Exercise Types
- **Diagnosis cases.** Describe a sick tree (symptoms, species, season, pot, watering history) and ask for ranked hypotheses and the cheapest test that separates them. This is the signature exercise, and the L20 gate uses it.
- **Photo critique.** Read a tree's nebari, trunk line, branch placement, apex and pot. Describe what it needs next. Use for L3, L28 and L43.
- **Mechanism prediction.** "If you do X in month Y, what happens to the energy budget, the hormones or the water flow?" Examples: heavy defoliation in late summer, or a hard prune on a weak tree.
- **Planning and teach-back.** Write a one-year development plan for a specific tree, then defend the order of operations.
- **Calendar building.** Species-by-species seasonal work templates (L36), tied to the student's own climate.
- **Hands-on tasks** are optional and should be small: a sieve test of a soil sample, a watering log, a wiring angle experiment on a spare branch. Never require owning a valuable tree.
- **Debate or myth-testing.** Pick a folk rule ("never prune in spring," "pinch junipers") and judge it against the physiology.

## Exercise Format Preference
Mixed. Use multiple choice for vocabulary, recognition and safety facts (tool use, lime-sulfur, pesticide labels). Use free-text, show-your-reasoning answers for diagnosis, planning and anything where the causal chain matters. If a student is struggling, shift toward MC with a required one-line justification.

## Resource Types
- Photographs and annotated tree images matter most. Always ask for or point to an image when teaching design or diagnosis.
- Textbook chapters (plant physiology, soil science) for mechanisms.
- Practitioner blogs and videos for technique, with a caution that they are experience-based and sometimes contradict each other.
- Primary papers or government bulletins for biosecurity, CODIT and chemical safety.
- Interactive tools: USDA zone map, light meters, a kitchen scale and sieve.

## Difficulty Curve
- Starts gentle (L1 to L4). It rises in M2, where the physiology is abstract (energy budget, auxin, CODIT).
- The hardest gate is L20: it needs cumulative M2 and M3 knowledge applied to a diagnosis. Consolidate in the reviews (L8, L12) before it.
- M4 techniques are hard because timing, species and tree vigor all interact. Teach rules as "it depends on vigor and season," not as fixed recipes.
- Species lessons (M5) are lower in abstraction but high in detail. Anchor each one to a calendar and a single decisive rule.
- L26 (deadwood and lime-sulfur) and L27 (grafting) are the highest-skill items. Keep them conceptual unless the student has the experience.
- Revisit misconceptions as scheduled concepts, not only as one-off corrections.

## Domain Hooks
- Trees that live for centuries in a pot a few centimeters deep. Ask how, in physiological terms.
- Bonsai folklore vs plant science: which traditions hold up and which are myths.
- A living art form that changes every season and is never finished.
- Parallel traditions across Japan, China, Vietnam and Korea, plus modern Western naturalism.
- Biosecurity: how the trade in plants moves pests, and why that matters beyond the hobby.
- Diagnosing a dying tree is detective work, which suits curious students.

## Common Failure Modes
- **Overwatering and underwatering confusion.** Both produce wilting or yellowing. Teach students to check the substrate, not the calendar.
- **Indoor temperate trees.** Students assume a juniper or maple can live indoors. Link back to L2 and dormancy (L11).
- **Watering on a schedule** instead of by substrate and weather.
- **Over-fertilizing a weak or just-repotted tree**, and treating fertilizer as medicine.
- **Repotting or heavy pruning at the wrong time**, or doing several stressful things at once.
- **Treating CODIT as healing.** Trees seal and compartmentalize wounds; they do not regenerate tissue.
- **Pinching junipers** the way you pinch pines or maples.
- **Wiring too tight or leaving it on too long**, which cuts into the bark.
- **Copying a species calendar** from a different climate.
- **Reading one source as authority.** Practitioners disagree, so ask the student to weigh evidence.
- **Using pesticides or lime-sulfur without reading the label** or wearing protection. Treat safety items as non-negotiable.
- **Collecting wild trees or importing plants** without checking the law and biosecurity rules.

## Vocabulary
- **Use freely:** bonsai, pot, substrate, repotting, pruning, pinching, wiring, trunk, branch, apex, deciduous, conifer, tropical, dormancy, fertilizer.
- **Define carefully on first use:** nebari, ramification, back-budding, apical dominance, auxin, CODIT, mycorrhiza, perched water table, air-filled porosity, cation exchange, chlorosis, jin, shari, defoliation, air-layering, approach graft, PPFD, IPM.
- **Japanese terms** (akadama, nebari, jin, shari, shohin, hòn non bộ and similar): give the term, a plain-English gloss and its origin together. Do not use them as jargon to impress.
- **Avoid or flag:** folk terms with no clear meaning ("tree is tired"), brand names as if they were categories, and absolute rules ("never", "always") unless they are safety rules.
- **Use cautiously:** "healing" for wounds, "feeding" for fertilizer (the tree makes its own food by photosynthesis), and "hardy" without a zone or temperature.
