# Bread Chemistry — Domain Teaching Config

## Exercise Types

- **Mechanism explanation (free text):** "Explain why X happens at the molecular level, and what you would see or feel in the dough." The core exercise type for this domain.
- **Predict-then-observe:** the student predicts an outcome before the lesson reveals it. Examples: salt added late, a cold retard, skipping the autolyse.
- **Troubleshooting diagnosis:** a defect is described (dense crumb, flying crust, pale crust, gummy interior, collapsed loaf) and the student reasons from cause to fix. Use this for interleaved review across modules.
- **Misconception check:** present a common false belief and ask the student to correct it with a mechanism.
- **Baker's-percentage calculation:** simple ratio and hydration problems. No equations beyond these.
- **Hands-on experiment (optional):** windowpane test, poke test, a flour-and-water paste rinsed to isolate gluten, a side-by-side salt or hydration comparison. Offer as an option, since the student may not bake.
- **Multiple choice:** for vocabulary and mechanism recall, such as which protein gives elasticity or what retrogradation is.

## Exercise Format Preference

**mixed** — multiple choice for vocabulary and recall, free text for causal explanations and troubleshooting. The student model may override this for struggling students.

## Resource Types

- **Textbooks and reference books (McGee, Delcour & Hoseney):** for accurate claims.
- **Practical baking sources (King Arthur, Serious Eats, The Perfect Loaf, Forkish, Reinhart):** tie the chemistry to kitchen results.
- **Video demonstrations:** most useful for tactile concepts such as windowpane, dough strength, poke test and crumb structure, which are hard to convey in text.
- **Photos of crumb and crust (Modernist Bread excerpts):** for linking structure to cause.
- **Peer-reviewed papers (Shewry et al.):** optional deep dives only, at the intermediate level.
- **Simple tools:** a scale, a thermometer, a percentage spreadsheet.

## Difficulty Curve

- **Early (flour components, gluten formation):** moderate. The ideas are concrete, but the student must drop the belief that flour already contains gluten.
- **Hardest part (fermentation chemistry and rheology):** viscoelasticity, elasticity vs. extensibility, strain hardening and gas retention are abstract. They need physical analogies (springs, ball bearings, balloons, rubber) and tie-backs to what the hands feel.
- **Gating concepts:** the gluten network gates gas retention, which gates oven spring. Starch gelatinization and protein setting gate crumb structure. Retrogradation gates the staling explanation.
- **Oven chemistry:** moderate, with the main trap of mixing up Maillard and caramelization.
- **Final module (rye, gluten-free, enriched doughs):** teach by contrast with the baseline; difficulty comes from combining earlier concepts, not new ones.
- **Consolidation:** place troubleshooting reviews after the mixing, fermentation and baking modules, where cross-module reasoning is needed.

## Domain Hooks

- Bread is a living, edible polymer: gluten behaves like a natural elastomer, like rubber.
- Every kitchen observation (stickiness, windowpane, poke test, crust color) has a molecular explanation.
- Flour contains gluten's precursors, not gluten. Water and mixing make it.
- Sourdough is a microbial ecosystem: yeast and lactic and acetic acid bacteria share the dough.
- Maillard chemistry connects bread crust to coffee roasting and seared steak.
- Staling is mostly starch recrystallizing, not drying out, and reheating can reverse it partly.
- Bread is ancient: Natufian flatbread residue about 14,000 years old.
- Debugging a bad loaf is detective work across the whole process.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **"Gluten is in flour."** Correct: flour has gliadin and glutenin; gluten forms with water and mixing.
2. **"Yeast makes bread rise by itself."** Correct: without a network to trap CO2, the gas escapes.
3. **"Maillard = caramelization."** Correct: Maillard involves amino acids and reducing sugars; caramelization is sugar alone at higher temperature.
4. **"Stale bread has dried out."** Correct: staling is mainly starch retrogradation. Moisture redistributes but is not mostly lost.
5. **"More kneading is always better."** Correct: development has an optimum, and over-mixing can degrade the dough, especially with machine mixing.
6. **"Sourness comes from yeast."** Correct: lactic and acetic acid bacteria produce the acids.
7. **Mixing up elasticity and extensibility,** or treating them as one property. Contrast them explicitly: glutenin gives snap-back, gliadin gives stretch.
8. **Memorizing bond names without the picture.** Anchor disulfide and hydrogen bonds to the spring and bridge models.
9. **Treating recipes as rules.** Push the student to reason from mechanism to adjustment.
10. **Overgeneralizing the baseline:** assuming rye or gluten-free doughs behave like wheat dough.

## Vocabulary

**Use freely:** flour, dough, knead, ferment, proof, gluten, starch, yeast, crumb, crust, hydration, oven spring, autolyse, windowpane, sourdough, starter, baker's percentage.

**Define carefully on first use:** gliadin, glutenin, disulfide bond, hydrogen bond, viscoelasticity, elasticity vs. extensibility, strain hardening, gelatinization, retrogradation, Maillard reaction, caramelization, denaturation, arabinoxylans (pentosans), damaged starch, amylase, lactic and acetic acid bacteria, pH.

**Avoid at this level:** reaction kinetics and rate equations, rheometry equations, protein genetics and wheat breeding terminology, detailed clinical celiac terminology, industrial engineering terms beyond a brief mention of Chorleywood.
