# Bread Chemistry: Gluten Networks and Baking Science. Curriculum Plan

## Scope
**Level:** intermediate. The student should know high-school chemistry: atoms and bonds, proteins as chains of amino acids, the basic idea of a reaction. They should also have some kitchen experience. No university biochemistry or food-science background is assumed.

**In scope:**
- What flour is made of: gliadin, glutenin, starch, damaged starch, arabinoxylans (pentosans), lipids and native enzymes such as amylases.
- How gluten forms: hydration, disulfide and hydrogen bonding, kneading, autolyse, the effect of salt, and the windowpane test.
- Fermentation chemistry: yeast metabolism, CO2 and ethanol, sourdough lactic and acetic acid bacteria, pH, and temperature control.
- Dough rheology at a conceptual level: viscoelasticity, elasticity vs. extensibility, strain hardening and gas retention.
- What happens in the oven: oven spring, starch gelatinization, protein denaturation and setting, Maillard browning vs. caramelization, and steam and crust formation.
- What happens after the bake: staling and starch retrogradation.
- Variations: enriched doughs (fat, sugar, eggs), rye, gluten-free systems and common improvers (ascorbic acid, enzymes).

**Out of scope:**
- Industrial process engineering (the Chorleywood process gets only a brief mention).
- Detailed protein genetics and wheat breeding.
- Quantitative rheometry (no equations beyond baker's percentages and simple ratios).
- Medical detail on celiac disease. It is mentioned once for context and is not taught clinically.
- Pastry and laminated doughs. These would make a good follow-on topic.

## Module Structure
The modules follow the dough's own timeline: **ingredients → mixing → fermentation → shaping and proofing → baking → cooling and variations.** Ordering by timeline has two benefits:
1. Each stage depends on the chemistry of the one before. You can't explain gas retention without the gluten network, and you can't explain oven spring without gas retention.
2. Every lesson lines up with a step the student can do in a real kitchen, so the theory always has a hands-on anchor.

Module 4 (Rheology & Shaping) comes deliberately after fermentation. 'Why does my dough tear or collapse?' only means something once the student understands both the network and the gas pushing on it. Module 6 comes last because the variations (rye, gluten-free, enriched) are best taught by contrast: 'what changes when you remove or dilute gluten?' That question needs the full baseline first.

## Pedagogical Decisions
- **Mechanism before recipe.** Every lesson answers 'what is happening at the molecular level, and what can you see or feel because of it?' Each concept is tied to something the student can observe: dough stickiness, windowpane, poke test, crumb structure, crust color.
- **Intuition before formalism.** The models are physical: gliadin as 'ball bearings / glue', glutenin as 'long springs linked by disulfide bridges'. Chemical names and bond types come in once the picture is in place. No reaction kinetics.
- **Predict, then observe.** Wherever possible, the student predicts an outcome before learning it. Examples: what happens if salt is added late, what a cold retard does to flavor.
- **Troubleshooting as the main way to apply knowledge.** A bread defect (dense crumb, flying crust, pale crust, gummy interior) is a diagnostic puzzle. Solving one needs causal reasoning across modules, which makes it ideal for interleaved review.
- **Misconceptions to confront directly:**
  - 'Gluten is in flour.' Wrong: flour contains its precursors, and gluten forms only with water.
  - 'Yeast makes bread rise by itself.' Wrong: without a network to hold the gas, there is no rise.
  - 'Maillard = caramelization.' Wrong: they are different reactions.
  - 'Stale bread has dried out.' Mostly wrong: staling is mainly starch retrogradation.
  - 'More kneading is always better.'
  - 'Sourdough sourness comes from yeast.' Wrong: it comes from the bacteria.

## Resource Strategy
**Note on research.md:** the automated arxiv results are off-topic (astronomy, network science, quantum computing) and should be ignored. The useful academic anchors are the OpenAlex results by Peter Shewry:
- 'Cereal seed storage proteins' (2002)
- 'Wheat' (2009)
- 'Seed storage proteins: structures and biosynthesis' (1995)
- The xylanase review by Collins et al. (2004), for the improvers lesson.

At intermediate level these work as optional deep dives, not required reading.

**Primary sources for lesson content:**
- Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking* (bread and grain chapters)
- *Modernist Bread* (Myhrvold & Migoya). Use its reference material and the published excerpts and photos.
- Delcour & Hoseney, *Principles of Cereal Science and Technology*. Use this for precise claims.
- The Wikipedia articles on gluten, the Maillard reaction, starch gelatinization and retrogradation.

**Engaging and practical sources:**
- Ken Forkish, *Flour Water Salt Yeast*
- Peter Reinhart, *The Bread Baker's Apprentice*
- King Arthur Baking's science articles
- Serious Eats (J. Kenji López-Alt, Stella Parks)
- The Maurizio Leo / The Perfect Loaf sourdough guides
- Videos: America's Test Kitchen science segments, Bake with Jack, and windowpane and poke-test demos

**Tools:** a baker's-percentage calculator or spreadsheet, and a kitchen scale and thermometer if the student bakes.

**Unexpected connections for wild cards:**
- Polymer physics: gluten as a natural elastomer, like rubber.
- The archaeology of bread: the 14,000-year-old Natufian flatbread residue from Jordan.
- Microbial ecology of sourdough starters.
- Maillard chemistry in coffee roasting and seared steak.

## Exercise Strategy
The format is **mixed**:
- **Multiple choice** for vocabulary and mechanism recall. Examples: which protein gives elasticity, and what retrogradation is.
- **Free text** for causal explanations and troubleshooting.

Exercise types:
1. **Mechanism explanation.** 'Explain in 3–4 sentences why salt tightens dough.'
2. **Predict-then-reveal.** 'You double the yeast and halve the proof time. What happens to flavor and why?'
3. **Baker's percentage calculations.** Hydration, salt % and preferment %. These are simple ratio arithmetic, and they make the chemistry concrete.
4. **Defect diagnosis.** Given a description or photo of a loaf, identify the likely cause and the chemistry behind it.
5. **Optional kitchen experiments.** Low effort, high payoff:
   - Wash a dough ball under water to isolate the gluten.
   - Compare an autolysed dough with an unautolysed one.
   - Brown two crusts, one brushed with a baking-soda solution and one not.
   - Leave bread out vs. in the fridge vs. in the freezer to show retrogradation.

   These experiments are always optional, and the lesson must work without them.
6. **Teach-backs.** 'Explain oven spring to a friend who's never baked.'

## Pacing
**Size:** 32 lessons across 6 modules, about 3–5 minutes of reading each.

**Difficulty curve:** gentle start (2s), peaks at 4 for:
- disulfide network formation and the glutenin macropolymer
- fermentation biochemistry and sourdough ecology
- the oven-stage interactions (gelatinization + denaturation + Maillard at once)
- the gluten-free / rye contrast

One lesson reaches 5: the cross-module troubleshooting capstone.

**Reviews:** review lessons sit at the end of Modules 2, 3 and 5, and the final review is the capstone. That puts a review roughly every 5–7 lessons. Reviews use interleaved defect-diagnosis problems rather than recall drills.

**Biggest risk:** Module 2's protein chemistry. If the student struggles there, add a lesson that goes back to protein structure basics (amino acids, folding, cysteine) before moving on.
