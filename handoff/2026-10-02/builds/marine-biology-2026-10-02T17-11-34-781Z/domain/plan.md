## Scope
**In scope:** the ocean below about 200 m. That covers:
- the physical environment: zonation, light, pressure, temperature, oxygen and circulation
- how organisms adapt: physiology, biochemistry, behaviour and reproduction
- the two energy economies: photosynthetic material sinking from above, and chemosynthesis on the seafloor
- the main habitats: midwater, abyssal plains, seamounts, vents, seeps, whale falls and hadal trenches
- how we study the deep sea
- human impacts and governance

**Out of scope:**
- shallow reefs and coastal ecology, except as contrast
- full taxonomy and systematics
- detailed physical oceanography mathematics
- formal biochemistry beyond simple redox reactions and osmolytes
- fisheries stock-assessment modelling

## Student Level Assumptions (intermediate)
The student is assumed to know high-school biology: cells, photosynthesis, respiration, food chains and basic evolution. They are also assumed to know basic chemistry: what an ion is, a simple equation, and a rough idea of oxidation versus reduction.

No university background is assumed. Define these terms the first time they appear: chemosynthesis, piezolyte, symbiont, stable isotope, and the biological pump. Quantitative work stays at back-of-envelope level. Examples:
- Pressure rises by about 1 atm for every 10 m of depth.
- Light falls by about a factor of 10 for every 75 m in clear water.
- Only about 1–3% of surface productivity reaches the abyssal seafloor.

## Module Structure and Rationale
The sequence follows one idea: **environment → constraints → solutions → energy → places → people.**

1. **The Physical Deep Sea (5).** Students need to understand the constraints (darkness, cold, pressure, scarce food, low oxygen) before adaptations make sense. This module ends with a review that also covers how we explore the deep, so students know where the evidence comes from.
2. **Life Under Pressure: Adaptations (6).** This is the hook module: bioluminescence, eyes, pressure biochemistry, energy saving and finding mates. Each adaptation is framed as an answer to a constraint from Module 1. That repeats Module 1 in new contexts.
3. **Food From Above (6).** Most of the deep sea runs on food sinking from the surface. Covers marine snow, the biological carbon pump, diel vertical migration, whale falls, and how food webs are traced with isotopes. This connects the deep sea to the global carbon cycle.
4. **Chemosynthetic Ecosystems (7).** This is the conceptual peak: ecosystems that run on chemical energy instead of sunlight. Covers the 1977 vent discovery, the redox chemistry, symbiosis (*Riftia*), seeps and brine pools, how vent communities colonise and change over time, and the origin-of-life connection. It is the longest module because it carries the hardest new idea (difficulty 4–5) and needs room for consolidation.
5. **Seafloor Habitats and Biodiversity (5).** Covers abyssal plains and the sediment biosphere, seamounts and cold-water corals, and hadal trenches. It also covers the puzzle of why the deep sea is so diverse when food is scarce. This brings Modules 2–4 together place by place.
6. **Humans and the Deep (6).** Covers deep-sea mining, bottom trawling, climate stressors (warming, oxygen loss, acidification), and pollution (plastic and other pollutants found in hadal amphipods). Then it covers governance: ISA negotiations and the BBNJ High Seas Treaty. It ends with a capstone teach-back. Applied, value-laden material comes last because students need everything before it to argue about it well.

**Total: 35 lessons.** Review and teach-back lessons fall at roughly 5, 11, 17, 24, 29 and 35, which keeps gaps between reviews at 5–7 lessons.

## Pedagogical Decisions
- **Constraint first, solution second.** Every adaptation lesson opens with the problem: 'You are a fish at 3,000 m and your proteins are being crushed. What do you do?' The student predicts before the reveal. This is the main teaching move for the domain.
- **Intuition and evidence over formalism.** Use real footage and specimens (MBARI and NOAA video) as the primary 'data'. Introduce one simple quantitative idea per lesson at most.
- **Energy as the thread through the whole course.** Each lesson ends by asking where this organism's energy ultimately comes from. This prepares the big conceptual shift in Module 4 and helps with the misconception that all life depends on the sun.
- **Visual-heavy.** Use depth profiles (light, temperature, O2 against depth), annotated diagrams of zones, and food-web diagrams. Many difficult ideas here are spatial.
- **Name the uncertainty.** Stress that most of the deep sea is unexplored and that species are still being discovered on most expeditions. Present some claims as open questions: the biodiversity puzzle, the depth limit for fish, and how vent animals disperse between vents. This counters the misconception that the deep sea is a solved, empty desert.
- **Difficulty curve.** Module 1 is 2–3, Module 2 is 2–3, Module 3 is 3, Module 4 is 3–5 (peak at chemosynthesis chemistry and symbiosis), Module 5 is 3–4, and Module 6 is 3–4. Review lessons drop to 1–2.

## Resource Strategy
Prioritise free, authoritative and visual sources:
- **Institutions:** MBARI (video library, Deep-Sea Guide), NOAA Ocean Exploration (Okeanos Explorer dive livestreams and education modules), Schmidt Ocean Institute (ROV *SuBastian* footage), WHOI (Dive & Discover, Alvin history), Census of Marine Life (archives).
- **Accessible primary literature, one per module at most, abstract-level reading:**
  - Ramirez-Llodra et al. 2010, 'Deep, diverse and definitely different' (Biogeosciences)
  - Haddock, Moline & Case 2010, 'Bioluminescence in the sea' (Annual Review of Marine Science)
  - Martini & Haddock 2017, on how common bioluminescence is in the water column
  - Yancey et al. 2014 (PNAS), on TMAO and the depth limit for fish
  - Smith & Baco 2003, on whale-fall ecology
  - Corliss et al. 1979, on the discovery of Galápagos vents
  - Danovaro et al. 2008, linking biodiversity to deep-sea ecosystem function
  - Jamieson et al. 2019, on plastic ingested by hadal amphipods
  - Orr et al. 2005, on ocean acidification
- **Books:** *The Deep* (Claire Nouvian) for imagery. *Below the Edge of Darkness* (Edith Widder) for bioluminescence narrative. Cindy Lee Van Dover's *The Ecology of Deep-Sea Hydrothermal Vents* as an optional reference for keen students.
- **Interactive:** 'The Deep Sea' scrolling depth visualiser (neal.fun). Google Earth ocean-floor view. NOAA live dive streams when they are running.
- **People:** Edith Widder, Cindy Lee Van Dover, Alan Jamieson, Steven Haddock, Lisa Levin, Robert Ballard.
- **Wild-card links:** sonar and the 'deep scattering layer' mistaken for a false seafloor; astrobiology at Europa and Enceladus; biotech enzymes from vent microbes (Taq polymerase came from a hot-spring microbe, a close analogue); deep-sea gigantism and Kleiber's law.

## Exercise Strategy
Use mixed formats: multiple choice for zone names, vocabulary and recall, and free text for reasoning and application. Specific types:
- **Predict-then-reveal:** 'This animal lives at 1,000 m. Predict its colour and eye size, then explain why.'
- **Profile interpretation:** read a depth profile of light, temperature or O2 and locate zones or the oxygen minimum zone.
- **Back-of-envelope estimates:** pressure at a given depth, or the fraction of surface carbon that reaches the seafloor.
- **Sorting and ranking:** order habitats by energy source or depth; match adaptation to constraint.
- **Design-a-creature:** write a short design for an organism at a stated depth and habitat, then justify each trait.
- **Trace the energy:** draw or describe the energy path from source to a given organism.
- **Structured debate (Module 6):** argue for or against a mining moratorium using evidence on recovery times and the value of the metals.
- **Teach-back (review lessons):** explain a concept to an imagined 12-year-old.

## Pacing
There are 35 lessons of about 3–5 minutes each. That is about 7 weeks at 5 lessons a week, in line with the 1% growth rule.

Consolidate at each review. The biggest jump in difficulty is at lessons 19–21 (redox-driven chemosynthesis and symbiosis). If the student struggles there, the Builder or Teacher may split chemosynthesis chemistry into two lessons.

Module 6 topics are live policy issues. The Teacher should check the current status of the ISA mining code and BBNJ ratification at delivery time and should not hard-code it.

## Exit Criteria (draft)
- Describe the deep-ocean zones and the physical gradients that define them, and estimate pressure at depth.
- Explain at least five deep-sea adaptations as solutions to specific environmental constraints.
- Trace energy flow in both a food web supplied from the surface and a chemosynthetic one, including the chemistry of chemosynthesis in words.
- Explain the role of the deep sea in the biological carbon pump and the global carbon cycle.
- Compare vents, seeps, whale falls, seamounts, abyssal plains and trenches by energy source, how long the habitat lasts, and the communities they support.
- Evaluate a deep-sea human impact (mining, trawling or climate) using evidence, and describe the governance framework.
