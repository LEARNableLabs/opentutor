# Competitive Rock Climbing — Teaching Notes

## Approach

The course is footage-first and evidence-labelled. The student climbs indoors already, so their body intuition is the anchor. Every abstract idea (scoring, CF, tendon adaptation) gets tied to a clip or a move they have felt. Route reading is taught as prediction followed by comparison: the student commits to a read, then checks it against elite footage (L12 → L20, L15, L37). Training science is taught with explicit evidence labels: cited study, practitioner knowledge, or no direct evidence.

## Hard Rules

- **No body-weight or body-composition targets**, ever, in any lesson, example or exercise. If the student raises weight loss as a performance strategy, redirect to L33 (fuelling, REDs) and to qualified professionals.
- **No medical diagnosis or rehab prescription.** For injury questions, explain the anatomy and red flags, then refer to a sports physician or physiotherapist (L32).
- **Safety gate (L22) before any hangboard content (L23).** A fail, an adolescent student, or a student new to structured training → Track B (L26). Campus board content (L24) is for experienced adults only.
- **L19 own-data collection is optional and must not use maximal hangs.** The pre-filled data set is the default.
- **Scoring rules change.** Check the current IFSC rulebook and the current Olympic format before delivering L1, L2, L4 and L35. L2 uses IFSC sources ONLY. The determinants reviews (Giles 2006, Mermier 2000, Saul 2019) are reserved for L16.

## Common Misconceptions

1. **'Comp setting is just harder gym setting.'** Comp problems are designed questions with a deliberate test (power, coordination, balance) and are calibrated to separate the field under the scoring rules. Correct with setter-intent analysis (L3, L14).
2. **'Reading a route means memorising holds.'** Experts chunk hold patterns into movement units and plan rests and clips. They do not list holds. Correct with the chunking analogy from chess (L10).
3. **'Pump is lactic acid building up.'** Pump is better explained by sustained intermittent contractions restricting blood flow and oxygen delivery, with recovery depending on rests and shakeouts. Correct with L17, and avoid oversimplifying the metabolite story.
4. **'Climbing performance comes down to one thing (finger strength, or strength-to-weight).'** The determinants reviews show performance is multi-factor: strength, endurance, technique, tactics and psychology. Taught in L16 ONLY. Do not pre-empt it elsewhere. Elsewhere, just point back to L16.
5. **'If my muscles are ready, my fingers are ready.'** Tendons and pulleys adapt far more slowly than muscle. Strength can outrun tissue tolerance, and in adolescents the growth plate is the weak point (L21, L22).
6. **'More hangboarding is always better.'** Protocol, edge depth, volume and recovery matter, and the hangboard is not appropriate for everyone (L23, L26).
7. **'CF is a test score you should max out.'** CF is a model parameter: the boundary between sustainable and unsustainable intermittent force. W' is the finite reserve above it. Both are used to interpret and design training, not as targets to chase recklessly (L18, L19, L25).

## Maths (L18 worked example, L19 exercise)

Model: F = CF + W'/t. Multiply by t to get the linear form F·t = CF·t + W'. Plot impulse (F·t) against time t: the slope is CF and the intercept is W'. Units here are kgf for force and s for time; W' is in kgf·s.

**L18 worked example (teacher shows every step):**

| t (s) | F (kgf) | F·t |
|---|---|---|
| 30 | 50 | 1500 |
| 60 | 40 | 2400 |
| 120 | 35 | 4200 |

- Slope = (2400 − 1500)/(60 − 30) = 30, and (4200 − 2400)/(120 − 60) = 30 → **CF = 30 kgf**.
- Intercept: W' = 1500 − 30 × 30 = **600 kgf·s**.
- Check: 30 + 600/120 = 35 ✓.

**L19 pre-filled data set (the student works it out):**

| t (s) | F (kgf) |
|---|---|
| 40 | 45 |
| 80 | 35 |
| 160 | 30 |

Answer key: impulses are 1800, 2800 and 4800. Slope = 25 → **CF = 25 kgf**. W' = 1800 − 25 × 40 = **800 kgf·s**. Then ask: which climber, the one from L18 or this one, recovers better on long routes, and which has more reserve for a short crux?

**Caveat to teach:** these numbers are idealised to fit a perfect line. Real data scatter, so you fit a best line or average the pairwise slopes. Research CF tests (e.g. Giles et al. 2019) use an all-out intermittent protocol, not this simplified time-to-failure set.

## Level Adjustments

For an intermediate indoor climber, use climbing vocabulary freely after L5-L6. Introduce physiology terms (occlusion, W', RFD, epiphysis) with a plain-language definition the first time. Keep maths to slope and intercept, with no calculus. Frame training as reasoning about evidence ('what does this study actually show?'), not as a prescription to follow. In L31, make sure the student designs the block on the track they qualified for at L22.

## Rabbit Holes

- **Chess chunking (Chase & Simon 1973)**: drop this in at L10. The pattern-recognition mechanism behind chess expertise also explains route-reading expertise.
- **Critical power in cycling and running**: drop this in at L18. CF is the same hyperbolic model that cyclists use for FTP-style pacing.
- **Paraclimbing adaptations**: drop this in at L14. Watching how setters adapt problems across sport classes shows what a problem is really testing.
- **Speed wall standardisation**: drop this in at L4. Because the speed route is the same everywhere, world records are comparable across venues, which is unusual in climbing.
